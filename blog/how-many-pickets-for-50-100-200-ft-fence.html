How Many Pickets for a 50, 100 and 200 ft Fence? (Tables)

How many pickets for a 100 ft fence? With standard 6-inch pickets (5.5 inches actual width) fitted tight with no gap, you need 218 pickets. Leave a half-inch gap between boards and the count drops to 200; a 1-inch gap brings it to 185. For a 50 ft fence the same three layouts need 109, 100 and 93 pickets, and a 200 ft fence needs 436, 400 and 370. Those three numbers — fence length, picket width and gap — are the whole calculation, and this guide works through them with tables for every common board size, the gate and corner deductions people forget, a 10% waste rule, and what the pickets cost in 2026.

The formula (and the one trap in it)

The formula is simple: convert the fence length to inches, then divide by the width of one picket plus its gap. A 100 ft fence is 1,200 inches long. Divide 1,200 by 5.5 inches (a 6-inch picket with no gap) and you get 218.18, which rounds up to 219 boards if you want the run fully covered — in practice most builders quote 218 and let the final board be trimmed or the last gap absorbed, but rounding up is the safe order quantity, and the tables below already round up. With a half-inch gap each board occupies 6.0 inches, so 1,200 divided by 6.0 is exactly 200. With a 1-inch gap each board occupies 6.5 inches, giving 184.6, rounded up to 185.

The trap is the nominal-versus-actual width. A picket sold as a 1x6 is not 6 inches wide — it is 5.5 inches wide after planing, just as a 2x4 is really 1.5 by 3.5 inches. A picket sold as a 1x4 is really 3.5 inches wide. If you run the formula with 6 instead of 5.5, a 100 ft fence comes out at 200 pickets instead of 218, and you will be 18 boards short with the fence 90% built and the lumber yard closed. Always use the actual width: 3.5 inches for 1x4 pickets, 5.5 inches for 1x6 pickets. Dog-ear, flat-top and pointed-top pickets all share the same widths, so the style of the top does not change the count — only the width and the gap do.

Read the gap columns as a design choice, not just a saving. No gap means a true privacy fence: no sightlines, less wind passing through, and the most boards. A half-inch gap is the classic spaced picket look — it reads as a fence rather than a wall, lets air through so the fence catches less wind, and saves roughly 8% of the boards. A 1-inch gap is airy and ranch-like, and it saves about 15% against the no-gap count. Wider gaps than 1 inch are for decorative or agricultural fences; on a suburban boundary they start to look unfinished, and small pets can slip through a 2-inch gap. If privacy is the reason for the fence, do not buy the gap saving — buy the boards.

Worked examples, step by step

Take a 100 ft backyard fence in 1x6 cedar pickets with no gap, the most common privacy-fence order in the United States. Step one: 100 ft is 1,200 inches. Step two: each picket covers 5.5 inches. Step three: 1,200 divided by 5.5 is 218.18, so 219 boards cover the run with nothing left over. Step four: add 10% waste for split boards, warped boards and cutting mistakes — 219 plus 22 is 241 boards to order. At a 2026 cedar picket price of roughly $3.50 to $5.50 each, that is $845 to $1,325 in pickets alone for the 100 ft run, before posts, rails and concrete.

Now the same fence in 1x4 pickets with a half-inch gap, a lighter, more open look. Each board now occupies 4.0 inches (3.5 plus 0.5). Divide 1,200 by 4.0 and you get exactly 300 pickets, plus 10% waste is 330 to order. The narrower boards need 50% more boards than the 1x6 no-gap layout, but each 1x4 board costs less — roughly $2.00 to $3.50 in pine or cedar — so the picket bill lands around $660 to $1,155. The trade is real: narrower boards mean more fasteners, more nailing time and more boards to inspect for warping, which is why most contractors default to 1x6 for privacy fences and reserve 1x4 for short decorative runs.

A third example shows why the gate section matters. A 200 ft fence sounds like 436 pickets in 1x6 with no gap, but suppose it has two gates: a 4 ft walk gate and a 10 ft double drive gate. Those openings total 14 ft, or 168 inches, which at 5.5 inches per board is 30.5 boards — call it 31 pickets that are never installed. The corrected count is 436 minus 31, or 405 boards, plus 10% waste is 446 to order. Skip the deduction and you over-order by about $110 to $170 of cedar; forget the waste allowance and you under-order by 40 boards and stall the job a day. Both mistakes are avoidable, and both come from skipping one line of arithmetic.

Gates, corners and ends: the deductions

Gate openings are the biggest deduction. Measure each gate as the clear opening between its posts, not the gate frame width, and convert it the same way: opening in inches divided by picket-plus-gap width, rounded to the nearest board. A 4 ft walk gate in 1x6 no-gap layout removes about 9 pickets; a 10 ft double gate removes about 22; a 12 ft gate removes about 27. If the gate itself is built from pickets (a framed gate clad in the same boards), those boards come back — a 4 ft gate clad in 1x6 pickets uses about 9 boards itself — so only deduct gates that are open metal frames or pre-built panels, and keep the gate boards in the count for picket-built gates.

Corners and ends need no deduction, but they do need a decision. A picket run ends at a post, and the last board is almost never a full board — it gets ripped to width to close the final few inches. That ripped board still counts as one board in the order, which is why the tables round up rather than down. On a run that ends against a house wall, measure to the wall face, not to the last post, and expect the same ripped final board. One more subtle case: a fence that steps down a slope. The picket count does not change with slope — the run length measured along the ground is what matters — but stepped fences leave small triangular gaps under the downhill end of each section, and racked fences (boards following the slope) use slightly longer boards at the downhill side. Neither changes the board count, only the board length and the look.

Waste: why 10%, and when to use 15%

Ten percent is the standard waste allowance for fence pickets, and it covers three things: boards rejected for warping, splitting or bark-edge defects (cedar in particular arrives with a few dogs in every bundle); boards cut wrong or split by a fastener near the end; and the ripped final board on each run, which consumes a full board for a partial width. On a 100 ft fence that is 22 extra boards on a 218-board count — enough to absorb a bad bundle without a second trip to the yard. Use 15% instead of 10% when any of these apply: you are buying lower-grade boards or ungraded mill-run stock; the fence has many short runs (each run wastes its own ripped final board); or the fence is being built by a first-timer, where miscuts are more likely. On a 200 ft cedar fence, the difference between 10% and 15% is about 22 boards and $75 to $120 — cheap insurance against a stalled Saturday. Buy the waste boards in the same lot as the rest if you can; cedar and pine weather to different shades by batch, and a second-trip bundle can show as a stripe on the fence for a year.

What the pickets cost in 2026

Picket prices vary by species, region and whether you buy by the bundle or by the board, but 2026 retail ranges are stable enough to budget from. Pressure-treated pine 1x6 pickets run roughly $2.00 to $3.50 each; cedar 1x6 pickets run $3.50 to $5.50; redwood, where available, runs $4.50 to $7.00. Multiply by the table counts and the pattern is clear: species matters more than gap. A 100 ft cedar privacy fence at 218 boards plus waste costs roughly $845 to $1,325 in pickets, while the same fence in treated pine costs roughly $480 to $840. A spaced pine fence with a 1-inch gap (185 boards plus waste, about 204 boards) drops the picket bill to roughly $410 to $715. For the full fence budget, pickets are typically 30% to 40% of the material cost — posts, rails, concrete and fasteners make up the rest — so a picket saving of $200 is real money but not the whole job.

One cost trap deserves a line of its own: picket length. Fence pickets are sold in 6 ft and 8 ft lengths (and 5 ft in some regions), and the price scales with length — an 8 ft picket costs noticeably more than a 6 ft one, and cutting 8 ft boards down to 6 ft wastes money. Order the length that matches your fence height plus the rail reveal: a 6 ft fence typically uses 6 ft pickets mounted with the tops flush, while an 8 ft fence needs 8 ft boards. Do not order long and trim; order right and spend the saving on the waste allowance instead.

Double-sided and shadowbox fences

Two popular styles break the single-row formula. A double-sided fence (boards on both faces of the rails, the "good neighbor" fence that looks finished from both sides) simply doubles the count: a 100 ft fence in 1x6 no-gap needs 218 boards per side, or 436 boards before waste, plus the usual 10%. A shadowbox fence (boards alternating front and back with a small overlap) is subtler: each side carries boards spaced about one board-width apart, so each side needs roughly half the no-gap count plus the overlap — in practice, order about 1.5 times the single-row count. For a 100 ft shadowbox fence in 1x6, that is roughly 327 boards before waste, or about 360 with waste. Shadowbox looks like a privacy fence from an angle and breathes like a spaced fence in the wind, which is why it survives storms that flatten solid privacy fences — the wind load argument alone justifies the extra boards in exposed locations.

Measuring the fence line before you count

Every number in this guide assumes you know the true fence length, and that is where most miscounts start. Measure along the ground where the fence will stand, not along a property line on a plat map — a plat distance can differ from the built length by several feet on a sloped or irregular lot. Walk the line with a long tape or a measuring wheel, mark each corner and gate, and add the runs together; a fence that encloses three sides of a yard is three runs, and each run gets its own rounded-up count (rounding per run, not on the total, is the honest method — three runs of 33.4 ft do not add up to one clean 100 ft count). Check for the two classic surprises: a utility easement that forces the fence a few feet inside the boundary, shortening the run, and a neighbor's existing fence on one side that you may share or mirror, which can remove an entire run from your order. And before ordering anything, confirm the fence height and style against local rules — a 6 ft fence is standard in most backyards, but front-yard limits are often 3 to 4 ft, and a miscounted height is a more expensive mistake than a miscounted board.

Installing without wasting the count

The count you order only holds if installation does not waste boards. Three habits protect it. First, use a spacer block cut to your gap width — a scrap of 1x2 for a half-inch gap, or a piece of the picket itself — and carry it down the run instead of eyeballing each gap; eyeballed gaps drift, and a drifted run ends with a final gap too wide to close with one ripped board. Second, pre-drill or use the right fasteners near board ends: pickets split most often within an inch of the end, and a split board is a wasted board. Stainless or hot-dipped galvanized screws and nails resist the rust streaks that plain fasteners leave on cedar within a season. Third, sort the boards before you start: set aside the worst warped boards for the shortest cuts and the gate cladding, and put the straightest boards on the most visible run, usually the street-facing side. Sorting takes twenty minutes and saves more boards than any waste percentage.

Frequently asked questions

How many pickets do I need for a 100 ft fence?

218 pickets with 1x6 boards (5.5 inches actual width) and no gap, 200 with a half-inch gap, or 185 with a 1-inch gap — before waste. Add 10% waste (about 22 extra boards on the no-gap count) and subtract any gate openings before ordering.

How many pickets for a 50 ft fence?

109 pickets with 1x6 boards and no gap, 100 with a half-inch gap, or 93 with a 1-inch gap. With 1x4 boards the counts are 172, 150 and 134. Add 10% waste and subtract gate openings.

How many pickets for a 200 ft fence?

436 pickets with 1x6 boards and no gap, 400 with a half-inch gap, or 370 with a 1-inch gap — before waste and gate deductions. With 10% waste the no-gap order is about 480 boards.

Do I count a 1x6 picket as 6 inches wide?

No — a 1x6 picket is actually 5.5 inches wide, and a 1x4 is 3.5 inches wide. Using the nominal width instead of the actual width undercounts a 100 ft fence by 18 boards, so always use 5.5 or 3.5 inches in the formula.

How much waste should I add for fence pickets?

10% is standard, covering warped or split boards, miscuts and the ripped final board on each run. Use 15% for lower-grade stock, fences with many short runs, or first-time builders.

Do gates reduce the picket count?

Yes — subtract each gate opening (measured between its posts) from the fence length before dividing, unless the gate itself is clad in the same pickets, in which case those boards stay in the count. A 4 ft walk gate removes about 9 pickets from a 1x6 no-gap count.
