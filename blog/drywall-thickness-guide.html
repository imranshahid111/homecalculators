Drywall Thickness Guide: 1/2 vs 5/8 Inch — Which Thickness Where?

Drywall thickness is a two-horse race for almost every residential job: 1/2 inch, the standard sheet that covers most walls and ceilings in American homes, and 5/8 inch, the heavier sheet that fire codes, long-span ceilings and noise complaints call for. This drywall thickness guide settles the choice with real numbers — sheet weights, 2026 prices, sag limits, fire ratings and the code situations that remove the choice entirely — plus a room-by-room decision table so you buy the right thickness for every surface in the house.

The two thicknesses, side by side

Nearly all drywall sold for home work is one of two thicknesses. Half-inch (12.7 mm) is the default: it is what most walls in your house are made of, what contractors quote unless they say otherwise, and what big-box stores stock deepest. Five-eighths inch (15.9 mm) is the heavy-duty sibling — about 25% thicker, about 35% heavier, fire-rated (Type X) in most products, and required by code in specific places. There are thinner specialty sheets — 1/4 inch for curved walls and skim-over jobs, 3/8 inch for matching old plaster depths — but for full walls and ceilings, the decision is always 1/2 vs 5/8. Here is the comparison that drives everything else in this guide:

The headline takeaway: 1/2 inch is the everyday sheet and 5/8 inch is the "this location has a reason" sheet. The reasons are fire rating, long spans, sound and code — everything else defaults to 1/2 inch.

What 1/2 inch drywall is (and where it wins)

Half-inch drywall is the workhorse of residential construction for one simple reason: it is strong enough for every interior wall that does not carry a special requirement, at the lowest weight and the lowest price. A 4×8 sheet weighs about 52 pounds, which one person can carry and hang on walls without a helper; a 4×12 sheet weighs about 76 pounds, manageable for two people. Prices in 2026 run roughly $13–$18 per 4×8 sheet, making it the cheapest way to get a paintable wall.

On walls, 1/2 inch is the right thickness for bedrooms, living rooms, hallways, closets and offices — every ordinary interior wall framed with studs 16 inches on-center. It takes screws and anchors normally, holds baseboard and trim fine, and finishes to a level 4 paint-grade surface like anything else. There is no durability problem with 1/2 inch on walls: the idea that it dents or breaks through under normal household use is mostly contractor folklore from the days of thinner plaster. If a wall needs to hold a heavy TV or a grab bar, the fix is blocking in the framing or a plywood backing strip, not thicker drywall.

On ceilings, 1/2 inch is standard where joists or trusses are spaced 16 inches on-center — the most common framing in homes built in the last few decades. The sheet's stiffness is plenty for a 16-inch span, and ceilings hung on 1/2 inch over 16-inch OC framing stay flat for the life of the house. The problems start only when the span gets wider, which is where 5/8 inch earns its keep.

There is one more 1/2-inch product worth knowing: moisture-resistant board (green or purple faced), made in the same 1/2-inch thickness for bathrooms, laundry rooms and other damp spaces. It costs a few dollars more per sheet but resists mold and moisture behind paint far better than standard board. Note that moisture-resistant drywall still is not a tile backer — tile in wet areas goes on cement board or an approved tile underlayment, not on any drywall.

What 5/8 inch drywall is (and why garages get it)

Five-eighths drywall weighs roughly 70 pounds per 4×8 sheet — a third heavier than 1/2 inch — and that mass is doing three jobs at once: resisting fire longer, spanning wider framing without sagging, and blocking more sound. Most 5/8-inch board sold today is Type X, a fire-rated formulation with glass fibers and other additives that let a single layer rate about one hour of fire resistance in tested assemblies. The same formulation exists in 1/2-inch Type C (a step beyond Type X, used in specific assemblies), but 5/8 Type X is the sheet the code book keeps asking for by name.

The most common place homeowners meet 5/8 inch is the garage. Building codes require fire separation between an attached garage and the house: typically 1/2-inch drywall on the garage side of walls is not enough — the walls between garage and living space, and the ceiling of the garage when there is habitable space above it, must be 5/8-inch Type X. Furnace rooms, utility rooms with fuel-burning equipment, and the walls around them often get the same treatment. In multi-family buildings, party walls between units and corridor walls are commonly 5/8-inch Type X assemblies (sometimes double-layer) to meet both fire and sound requirements.

On ceilings, 5/8 inch is the safe choice whenever framing is spaced 24 inches on-center — a common layout in newer construction using engineered trusses. A 1/2-inch sheet spanning 24 inches will visibly sag between joists over the years, especially with texture or insulation piled above it; 5/8 inch spans 24 inches flat. (The exception is 1/2-inch "sag-resistant" or "ceiling" board, a reinforced product rated for 24-inch OC ceilings — it works, but 5/8 Type X is cheaper and more widely stocked.)

Prices for 5/8-inch Type X run roughly $20–$30 per 4×8 sheet in 2026 — about $7–$12 more per sheet than 1/2 inch. On a whole house that adds up, which is exactly why nobody specs 5/8 inch everywhere: it goes where fire, span or sound rules require it, and 1/2 inch goes everywhere else.

Ceilings: the sag question

Ceiling sag is the one failure that is purely about thickness choice, and it is slow — the sheet droops between joists over months and years, visible as gentle waves under raking light or, in bad cases, visible from across the room. The rule is mechanical and simple: stiffness grows with the cube of thickness, so a 5/8-inch sheet is roughly twice as stiff as a 1/2-inch sheet. That stiffness is what resists gravity pulling the sheet down between its supports.

The practical rules, which match both manufacturer tables and common code language: 1/2-inch drywall on ceilings is rated for framing spaced 16 inches on-center or less. At 24 inches on-center, use 5/8-inch (or 1/2-inch sag-resistant ceiling board). Never hang 1/2-inch regular board on 24-inch OC ceilings to save a few dollars — the sag is not a maybe, it is a when, and fixing it means either tearing down or strapping and re-hanging over the sagged ceiling, which costs many times what the thicker sheets would have.

Insulation makes sag worse, not better: attic insulation piled on a ceiling adds sustained load between joists, and high-R-value attics over 24-inch OC framing are exactly where sag shows up first. If you are upgrading insulation over a 1/2-inch ceiling on 24-inch centers, that is the time to strap the ceiling with 1×3 furring strips perpendicular to the joists and screw new 5/8-inch board to the strapping — you get a fresh flat ceiling and a thermal break in one move.

Code: where the rules force the choice

Some locations do not offer a thickness choice — the building code picks for you, and an inspector will fail the job if you guess wrong. The rules below reflect the International Residential Code as widely adopted across US jurisdictions; your local amendments can add requirements, so always check with the local building department before a permit job. The common mandatory 5/8-inch Type X locations in a single-family home are:

LocationRequired assemblyWhy
Wall between garage and house5/8 in Type X on garage sideFire separation — slows fire reaching living space
Garage ceiling under living space5/8 in Type XFire separation from below
Furnace/boiler room walls5/8 in Type X (common requirement)Fuel-burning equipment fire risk
Water heater closets (gas)5/8 in Type X (common requirement)Same ignition-source logic
Ceilings, 24 in OC framing5/8 in (or sag-resistant 1/2 in)Structural sag resistance
Soffits and eaves (some jurisdictions)5/8 in Type X or cement boardFire spread at the eave line

Two important clarifications. First, Type X is a fire-rating of the board formulation, not a thickness — the 5/8-inch sheet most codes require happens to be Type X because 1/2-inch Type X is a specialty product. When the code says 5/8-inch Type X, bring 5/8-inch sheets actually labeled Type X; standard 5/8-inch board without the rating does not satisfy the assembly. Second, bathroom and kitchen walls near water get cement board behind tile, not thicker drywall — no drywall thickness is an approved tile backer in a wet area under current code.

If your project needs a permit, the permit application or the inspector will tell you which assemblies your jurisdiction wants. For non-permitted cosmetic work (a bedroom refresh, a basement finish in some jurisdictions), the same rules are still worth following — especially the garage separation, which is about life safety, not paperwork.

Weight math: what thickness means on the wall and on your back

Thickness is not just a spec — it is weight you carry up stairs and hold overhead. A standard 4×8 1/2-inch sheet weighs about 52 pounds; the same sheet in 5/8 inch weighs about 70 pounds. Multiply by a room and the difference becomes real labor:

Room (8 ft ceilings, walls + ceiling)Drywall area1/2 in: sheets / total weight5/8 in: sheets / total weight
10×10 bedroom420 sq ft16 sheets / ~830 lb16 sheets / ~1,120 lb
12×12 bedroom528 sq ft20 sheets / ~1,040 lb20 sheets / ~1,200 lb (4×10 mix)
20×20 living room1,040 sq ft37 sheets / ~1,920 lb37 sheets / ~2,590 lb

For that 20×20 room, choosing 5/8 inch means hauling an extra ~670 pounds of board and holding 70-pound sheets overhead on every ceiling run instead of 52-pound ones. A solo DIYer feels that difference acutely; even a two-person crew works slower. This is the honest trade behind every "just use 5/8 everywhere" recommendation: the heavier sheet costs more per sheet, more per delivery, and more per hour of labor — for benefits (fire rating, stiffness, sound) that most interior walls never use.

Delivery math matters too: a truck delivery often has a sheet or weight minimum, and heavier sheets reach a vehicle's safe payload faster. And on ceilings, the extra weight is exactly why 12-foot sheets in 5/8 inch (~102 lb each) are a two-person-or-a-lift proposition, while the same length in 1/2 inch (~76 lb) is merely awkward.

Sound and strength: the quiet benefits

Heavier board blocks more sound — the physics is blunt (more mass per square foot, less vibration) and the improvement is real: swapping 1/2 inch for 5/8 inch on both sides of a wall typically buys 2–3 STC points, the difference between hearing muffled conversation and barely noticing it. For a home office wall, a shared bedroom wall in a duplex, or a media room, 5/8 inch on both sides (or double-layer 1/2 inch) is the cheapest sound upgrade in construction — far cheaper per decibel than specialty acoustic board or resilient channel, though combining them works better still.

Strength gets exaggerated in both directions. Thicker board resists dents and corner damage slightly better, and screws bite marginally deeper — but no drywall is a structural panel. If a wall must hold weight (floating shelves, a wall-mounted vanity, a heavy mirror), the answer is the same for either thickness: find the studs, use the right anchors for the load, or add blocking during framing. Buying 5/8 inch to make a wall "stronger" for hanging things is spending money on the wrong upgrade.

Cost comparison: what thickness adds to a project

At 2026 prices, 1/2-inch 4×8 sheets run roughly $13–$18 and 5/8-inch Type X 4×8 sheets run roughly $20–$30 — a gap of about $7–$12 per sheet. The worked budgets below use mid-range prices ($15.50 vs $25) for three standard rooms at 8 ft ceilings, materials only:

Room1/2 in materials5/8 in materialsThickness premium
10×10 bedroom (16 sheets)~$248~$400~$152
12×12 bedroom (20 sheets)~$310~$500~$190
20×20 living room (37 sheets)~$574~$925~$351

Those premiums are sheet prices only; the labor premium is harder to quote but real — heavier sheets slow hanging, especially overhead. On a whole house, spec'ing 5/8 inch everywhere it is not needed typically adds $1,500–$3,000 in materials and noticeably more in labor, for fire ratings and stiffness most walls never call on. Spend it where the table above says to; save it everywhere else.

Mistakes that cost real money

First, 1/2-inch regular board on 24-inch OC ceilings — the sag described above, fixed only by tearing down or strapping over. Second, hanging tile on any drywall in a wet area: thickness does not fix it; wet-area tile needs cement board or an approved membrane panel, and drywall behind shower tile is a mold and failure guarantee. Third, buying 5/8 inch for the whole house "to be safe": safe from what? The fire rating helps in exactly the locations code already requires; elsewhere it is weight and money spent for nothing. Fourth, forgetting that Type X must be labeled Type X — a 5/8-inch sheet without the fire rating does not satisfy a fire-separation assembly, and an inspector will make you prove it with the stamp on the sheet. Fifth, mixing thicknesses on the same plane: where a 5/8-inch wall meets a 1/2-inch wall at an inside corner, the finished surfaces will not line up by an eighth of an inch — plan the transition with a shim, a corner bead, or an intentional reveal, or the bump will show through paint forever. Sixth, assuming 1/4-inch or 3/8-inch sheets are cheaper substitutes for 1/2 inch on walls: they are specialty products (curves, plaster matching, skim-overs) that cost as much or more per sheet and fail faster as standalone walls.

Frequently asked questions

When should I use 1/2 inch vs 5/8 inch drywall?

Use 1/2 inch drywall for ordinary interior walls and for ceilings framed 16 inches on-center — it is lighter, cheaper and strong enough. Use 5/8 inch Type X where fire rating, long spans or sound call for it: garage-to-house walls and garage ceilings under living space (code), furnace and water heater rooms, ceilings with 24-inch on-center framing, and walls where you want noticeably better sound blocking.

Is 5/8 inch drywall required by code?

In specific places, yes. Building codes commonly require 5/8-inch Type X drywall on the garage side of walls between an attached garage and living space, on garage ceilings below habitable rooms, and around furnace rooms and gas water heater closets. The requirement is for the fire-rated Type X formulation, not just the thickness — a standard 5/8-inch sheet without the Type X label does not satisfy the assembly. Always confirm with your local building department, since adopted code editions vary.

Can I use 1/2 inch drywall on a ceiling with 24-inch joist spacing?

Not regular 1/2-inch board — it will sag between 24-inch on-center joists over time. Use 5/8-inch drywall, or 1/2-inch sag-resistant ceiling board specifically rated for 24-inch spacing. On 16-inch on-center ceilings, standard 1/2-inch drywall is the normal choice and stays flat.

How much heavier is 5/8 inch drywall than 1/2 inch?

A 4×8 sheet of 1/2-inch drywall weighs about 52 pounds; the same sheet in 5/8 inch weighs about 70 pounds — roughly 35% heavier. On a 20×20 room that difference totals about 670 extra pounds of board to carry and hang, which is why heavier board slows crews down and raises labor cost, especially on ceilings.

Does 5/8 inch drywall block more sound?

Yes — more mass means less vibration, and swapping 1/2 inch for 5/8 inch on both sides of a wall typically gains 2–3 STC points, enough to noticeably mute conversation. It is the cheapest sound upgrade in construction for home office, media room and party walls, though it does not make a wall "stronger" for hanging heavy objects — that is a studs-and-anchors question.

Can I hang tile on 5/8 inch drywall in a shower?

No. No drywall thickness is an approved tile backer in a wet area — shower and tub-surround tile must go on cement board or an approved waterproof tile panel. Moisture-resistant drywall (green or purple faced) is for damp rooms behind paint, not behind tile.

Is it worth using 5/8 inch drywall for the whole house?

Almost never. The materials premium runs roughly $1,500–$3,000 on a whole house, plus slower hanging from the extra weight, and the fire rating and stiffness only matter where code, span or sound rules require them. Spec 5/8-inch Type X for garages, furnace rooms and 24-inch OC ceilings; use 1/2 inch everywhere else.
