How Many Pavers Do I Need for a 10×10, 12×12 and 20×20 Patio?

How many pavers for a 10x10 patio? With the standard 4×8-inch paver most patios use, the answer is 495 — buy 500. That is 450 pavers for the 100 square feet plus a 10% waste allowance for cuts and breakage. It is a one-minute calculation once you know the formula, but it is also the calculation most DIYers get wrong, because they count the pavers and forget the cuts, or they order by the pallet and discover a pallet rarely covers exactly what the store's square-foot figure says. This guide gives you the exact counts for the three most common patio sizes — 10×10, 12×12 and 20×20 — across the four paver sizes you will actually see at the yard, then walks the formula, the waste factor your laying pattern demands, and the base and sand quantities that ride along with every paver order.

The 30-second answer: counts by patio and paver size

Every number below assumes a 10% waste allowance — the right default for a first-timer laying a rectangular patio in a simple pattern. Multiply by 1.15 instead if you are running a herringbone or 45-degree pattern (more cuts, more waste — see the pattern section).

Two things to notice. Second, the "order" numbers for 4×8 pavers on the 10×10 and 20×20 are clean pallet math: a full pallet of 4×8 pavers covers roughly 100 square feet (about 480–500 pavers), which is why the 10×10 takes one pallet and the 20×20 takes four. If the store sells by pallet, those are your order quantities.

The formula (both versions)

There are two ways to run the math, and both give the same answer. Use whichever matches the numbers you have.

Method 1 — the square-foot method. Divide the patio area by the square footage of one paver, then add waste:

Pavers = (patio length × patio width) ÷ (paver length × paver width) × 1.10

Work it for a 12×12 patio with 4×8 pavers, all in inches: (144 × 144) ÷ (4 × 8) = 20,736 ÷ 32 = 648 pavers, × 1.10 = 712.8 → 713. Matches the table.

Method 2 — the per-square-foot rate. Memorize how many pavers cover one square foot, multiply by the patio area, add waste. The rates worth knowing:

Work it for a 10×10 patio with 6×9 pavers: 100 sq ft × 2.67 = 267 pavers, × 1.10 = 293.4 → 293. Same answer, faster on a phone calculator in the store aisle.

One caveat on both methods: the rates assume nominal paver sizes. Some pavers are sold with slightly smaller actual dimensions to leave room for joints (a "4×8" that is really 3⅞×7⅞ is common) — the difference is under 2% and the waste allowance absorbs it, but if you are ordering within a tight budget, measure one paver before you commit.

The waste factor: your pattern sets it

Ten percent is the default, not the law. Waste comes from two places: cuts along the edges, and pavers that break, chip or arrive damaged. The pattern you choose moves the number a lot, because a diagonal pattern forces a cut at nearly every edge while a straight running bond cuts only at the perimeter.

Laying patternWaste factor10×10 count (4×8 pavers)

The practical advice: if this is your first patio, order the higher factor. A herringbone 10×10 at 15% waste is 518 pavers — 18 more than the default 495, roughly $13–$20 at basic paver prices. That is the cheapest insurance in the entire project. Running out mid-install is not just a second delivery fee; a second batch can come from a different production run with a slightly different dye lot, and your patio will show a permanent color seam where the old batch meets the new. Leftover pavers stack neatly in the garage and become free replacements for any paver that settles or stains later.

Curves, borders and the edge tax

The counts above assume rectangles, which cut politely along one edge at a time. Curved patios are hungrier: every paver that meets a curve needs an angled cut, and curved cuts waste the offcut half of every paver they touch. For a patio with one curved edge, add 5% on top of your pattern waste — call a diagonal-pattern curved 10×10 a 20% waste job, or about 540 of the 4×8s.

Soldier-course borders (a row of pavers standing on edge around the perimeter) are a separate count: measure the perimeter in pavers, not square feet. A 10×10 patio has a 40-foot perimeter; with 8-inch pavers laid long-side-out, that is 60 pavers for the border, ordered before the field count. Contractors price borders as a line item because they are real extra material, not free — budget them explicitly.

The quantities that ride along: base and sand

Pavers are only half the order. Every patio also needs a compacted base and joint sand, and under-ordering gravel is the most common reason a DIY patio install stalls on day one. The standard residential spec: 4–6 inches of compacted crushed gravel over a landscape-fabric separator, then 1 inch of bedding sand, then the pavers, then joint sand swept between them.

For the 4-inch minimum on our three patio sizes (compacted volume, ordered as loose gravel with a little extra):

Gravel is sold by the ton (crushed stone runs about 1.4 tons per cubic yard), and you order 15–20% extra loose volume because compaction eats the air out of it — the table above already includes that. Polymeric sand covers roughly 80–100 square feet per 50-pound bag on standard 3/16-inch joints; wider joints eat more. Delivery note: a ton of gravel costs $30–$50 at the yard in 2026, but delivery fees ($50–$100) are per load — ordering the gravel and the pavers on the same truck is the oldest money-saving trick in the book.

What this means in money

Paver counts are abstract until they hit a budget. Basic 4×8 concrete pavers run about $0.50–$0.80 each in 2026 (plain gray), $1.00–$1.50 for tumbled or colored finishes — use these to sanity-check any quote.

Two rules of thumb hide in this table. First, pavers are about 70–75% of the material cost — the base is cheap by comparison, which is exactly why skimping on base depth is false economy (a sunk patio costs more to lift and relay than the gravel you saved). Second, the 20×20 is not four times the 10×10 in cost — it is about 3.8× — because delivery fees and tool rentals amortize over the bigger job. That is the economies-of-scale argument for building the patio you actually want the first time rather than expanding later.

Frequently asked questions

How many pavers do I need for a 10x10 patio?

495 with the standard 4×8-inch paver — 450 for the 100 square feet plus a 10% waste allowance for cuts and breakage. In practice, order a full pallet (roughly 100 sq ft of 4×8 pavers) and you land right on it. If you are using 12×12 pavers instead, you need 110, and for 6×9 pavers, 293.

How many pavers do I need for a 12x12 patio?

713 with standard 4×8 pavers at 10% waste (648 for the 144 sq ft plus waste), rounded up to an order of 720. With 12×12 pavers you need 159 (order 160); with 6×9 pavers, 423. A diagonal or herringbone pattern needs 12–15% waste instead — about 745–750 of the 4×8s.

How many pavers do I need for a 20x20 patio?

1,980 with standard 4×8 pavers at 10% waste — order 2,000, which is four full pallets of roughly 500 each. With 12×12 pavers you need 440; with 6×9 pavers, about 1,175. The 20×20 also needs roughly 7 tons of loose gravel for a 4-inch compacted base and 4–5 bags of polymeric joint sand.

How much extra should I order for waste?

5–8% for a straight or running-bond pattern parallel to the house, 10% as the default for most DIY patios, and 12–15% for herringbone or 45-degree diagonal patterns. Add another 5% if the patio has curved edges. First-timers should always take the higher end of the range — leftover pavers are free future repairs, while a second delivery from a different dye lot can leave a visible color seam.

Do I count pavers for a border separately?

Yes. A border (soldier course) runs around the perimeter and is measured in linear pavers, not square feet — a 10×10 patio's 40-foot perimeter needs 60 of the 8-inch-side pavers for the border alone. Count and order the border first, then run the field count for the interior.

How thick should the paver base be?

4–6 inches of compacted crushed gravel for a residential patio (plus 1 inch of bedding sand), over landscape fabric. Driveways need 6–8 inches. For a 10×10 patio at 4 inches, order about 1.7 tons of loose gravel — compaction and settlement eat roughly 15–20% of loose volume, so never order the exact compacted number.
