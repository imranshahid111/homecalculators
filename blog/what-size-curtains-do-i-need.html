What Size Curtains Do I Need? Width, Fullness and Length Rules

What size curtains do I need is the question almost everyone asks standing in the curtain aisle with a tape measure in one hand and a panel in the other — and it is a question with a real answer, not a matter of taste. A curtain that is the wrong size does not just look slightly off; it looks wrong in a way you can see from across the room. Too narrow and the panel stretches flat across the window like a bed sheet, with no folds at all. Too short and it hangs like it shrank in the wash. Too long and it pools into a dusty puddle nobody asked for. The good news is that sizing curtains is arithmetic, not guesswork: three measurements and one multiplication give you the panel width, the panel count and the length, and this guide works through all three with real windows from 36 to 96 inches wide, a fullness table by fabric type, the length rules for sill, apron, floor and puddle, and what each size costs ready-made versus custom.

The short answer: three numbers, one formula

Every curtain size comes down to three numbers. First, the rod width — not the window width, the rod width, which is wider than the window because the rod extends past the frame on both sides. Second, the fullness factor, which is how many times wider the fabric is than the rod: 2 times is the standard that gives proper folds, 1.5 times is the minimum that still looks like a curtain, and 2.5 times is the lush, gathered look. Third, the length, measured from the rod down to wherever the curtain should end. The formula for width is simple: total fabric width = rod width × fullness factor. Divide that by the width of one panel — 54 inches for a standard ready-made panel — and round up to an even number of panels, because panels hang in pairs and an odd panel count leaves one side heavier than the other. The length is measured, not calculated: from the top of the rod (or the top of the ring, if you use rings) down to the sill, the apron, the floor, or the floor plus a puddle. Get those three numbers right and the curtain fits; get any one of them wrong and it shows.

The most common sizing mistake is measuring the window instead of the rod. A 36-inch window does not take a 36-inch curtain. The rod sits 3 to 6 inches past the frame on each side so the panels can stack back clear of the glass when they are open, which makes the rod 42 to 48 inches wide for a 36-inch window. At 2 times fullness that is 84 to 96 inches of fabric — roughly two standard 54-inch panels. A single 54-inch panel on that rod would be 1.1 times fullness, which is barely more fabric than the rod itself, and it would hang almost flat. The second most common mistake is buying the length that matches the window instead of the length that matches the wall: a 63-inch panel on a rod mounted 84 inches from the floor ends 21 inches short of the floor, which is the classic "shrank curtain" look. Measure the rod, multiply by the fullness, measure the drop — in that order.

Step 1: measure the rod width (not the window)

The rod width is the finished width the fabric has to cover, and it is always wider than the window. The standard rule is to extend the rod 3 to 6 inches past the window frame on each side — 3 inches is the minimum that lets the panel stack back off the glass, 6 inches is the generous end that makes the window look wider than it is. On a wide window or a room where you want the window to read larger, 8 to 10 inches per side is not unusual. The table below shows the rod width for the five most common window sizes at the standard 6-inch extension, and what that means for the fabric width at the three fullness levels.

Two things in that table matter. First, the jump from 1.5 times to 2 times fullness is not a small change — on a 60-inch window it is 36 inches of extra fabric, which is the difference between a curtain that looks adequate and one that looks properly gathered. Second, the 2.5 times column gets very wide very fast: a 72-inch window at 2.5 times needs 210 inches of fabric, which is four standard panels, and a sliding door at 2.5 times needs 270 inches, which is five panels — and five is an odd number, so in practice you would buy six or accept 2 times. Fullness is a trade-off between look and cost, and the table makes the cost visible: every extra half-point of fullness is another panel on most windows.

One more measurement matters before you buy: the stack-back. When the curtains are open, the fabric bunches to each side of the window, and that bunch — the stack — covers part of the glass. A good rule is that the stack takes up about one-third of the total fabric width when gathered, so 96 inches of fabric stacks into roughly 32 inches of bunch, or 16 inches on each side. That is exactly why the rod extends past the frame: those 16 inches of stack sit on the wall, not on the glass. If the rod only just covers the window, the open curtain eats a third of your light. Measure the rod with the stack in mind, and if the window is the main light source in the room, use the 6-inch extension rather than the 3-inch one.

Step 2: pick your fullness by fabric type

Fullness is not one number for every fabric. Sheer, thin fabrics need more of it, because a sheer panel at 1.5 times fullness looks like a flat sheet of gauze with no visible folds. Heavy, stiff fabrics need less of it, because a thick velvet at 2.5 times fullness bunches into a stack so deep it swallows the rod. The table below gives the fullness range that works for each common fabric, and what it means in panels for the most common window in the house — a 48-inch window on a 60-inch rod, where 2 times fullness is 120 inches of fabric.

Fabric typeFullness rangeLookPanels for a 60 in. rod
Sheer / voile2.5× to 3×Soft, airy folds; light still passes3 panels (162 in. = 2.7×)
Linen / cotton2×Crisp, even folds; the standard look3 panels (162 in. = 2.7×); 2 panels (108 in. = 1.8×) also acceptable
Velvet2× to 2.5×Deep, heavy folds; rich but bulky stack3 panels
Blackout / lined1.5× to 2×Flatter folds; lining adds stiffness2 panels (108 in., 1.8×)
Polyester (ready-made)1.5× to 2×Light, easy-care folds2 to 3 panels

Read the linen row carefully, because it is where most people get caught. A 60-inch rod at 2 times fullness needs 120 inches of fabric. Two standard 54-inch panels give 108 inches, which is 1.8 times — close enough for linen or blackout, and most people would never notice. But three panels give 162 inches, which is 2.7 times — noticeably fuller, and noticeably more expensive. The honest choice is between 2 panels at 1.8 times (neat, slightly flat, cheaper) and 3 panels at 2.7 times (lush, heavier stack, one more panel to buy). There is no 2.5-panel option, and this is the step where the even-number rule forces a real decision. For a bedroom where the curtains are mostly closed, 2 panels are fine. For a living room where the folds are part of the look, 3 panels earn their money.

Sheers deserve a warning of their own. Because sheer fabric is so thin, the folds are the only thing that gives it any presence, and 2 times fullness on a sheer reads as almost flat. If you are buying sheers as the only window treatment, budget for 2.5 to 3 times — that is 4 panels on a 60-inch rod, not 2. If the sheers sit behind a heavier pair of curtains, 2 times is enough, because the heavier pair carries the look and the sheer is just a light filter. The same logic applies in reverse to blackout curtains: the lining makes the fabric stiff, so 1.5 times still holds a fold, and buying 2.5 times of lined blackout fabric gives you a stack so thick it may not clear the rod brackets.

Step 3: panel count — the division that decides the price

The panel count is where the sizing turns into a shopping list, and it is a simple division with one rule: always round up to an even number. Total fabric width divided by panel width gives the raw count; rounding up to the next even number gives the panels to buy. The table below does that division for every common window at the standard 2 times fullness, using the standard ready-made panel width of 54 inches.

The asterisks on the 48-inch and 60-inch rows are the interesting part. The even-number rule would say 4 panels for the 48-inch window (raw count 2.22 rounds up to 4, not 3), but 3 panels is the practical answer most decorators use, because 3 panels at 2.7 times fullness already look lush and the middle panel can be split — one and a half panels per side — so the window still reads as balanced. The strict even-number rule matters most when the count is 2: a single panel on a window looks wrong, and two panels are the minimum for any window. From 3 panels up, the symmetry question is about how the panels are distributed, not whether the count is even, and 3 well-hung panels beat 4 skimpy ones. The table shows the practical count, not the textbook count, and the actual fullness column shows what you really get.

Wide panels change the arithmetic. Some ready-made panels come 100 or 108 inches wide, and one wide panel can replace two standard ones — a 96-inch sliding door at 2 times fullness needs 216 inches, which is 4 standard panels or 2 wide panels, and 2 wide panels are usually cheaper than 4 standard ones and leave fewer seams. Custom panels can be made any width, so a custom order for that same door would be 2 panels of 108 inches each, exactly 2 times fullness with no rounding at all. If the window is wider than 72 inches, check the wide-panel price before buying four standard panels; the per-inch cost of a wide panel is usually lower, and the result looks cleaner.

Step 4: length — sill, apron, floor or puddle

Length is measured from the rod to the ending point, and the ending point is a style choice with a right answer for each room. There are four standard lengths, and they are not interchangeable: a sill-length curtain in a living room looks unfinished, and a puddle-length curtain in a kitchen is a hazard. The table below gives each length, how to measure it, and where it belongs.

Length styleHow to measure itBest forWatch out for
Sill lengthRod to windowsill, minus ½ in.Kitchens, bathrooms, above radiatorsLooks short anywhere else; must clear the sill
Apron lengthRod to sill, plus 4 to 6 in.Kitchens, casual rooms, above furnitureCan look indecisive if the room is formal
Floor lengthRod to floor, minus ½ to 1 in.Living rooms, bedrooms, dining roomsMeasure on the finished floor, not the subfloor
Puddle lengthRod to floor, plus 2 to 8 in.Formal living rooms, bedroomsCollects dust and pet hair; trips children

Floor length is the default for most rooms, and the measurement is the one people get wrong most often: it is measured from the top of the rod — or from the bottom of the ring if the curtain hangs on rings, which drops it about 1.5 inches — to the floor, minus half an inch to one inch so the hem just kisses the floor without dragging. Measure at three points across the window, because floors are rarely level, and use the shortest of the three measurements; a curtain that is the right length on the left and an inch long on the right will drag on one side. If the floor is carpeted, measure to the top of the carpet, and remember that a thick-pile carpet compresses under the hem — measure with a book or a flat weight on the carpet to get the true finished height.

The rod height decides the length before you ever measure the drop. The standard is to mount the rod 4 to 6 inches above the window frame, which on an 8-foot ceiling with a standard window puts the rod about 84 inches from the floor — and that is exactly why 84-inch panels are the best-selling ready-made length in the US: rod at 84 inches, floor-length panel at 84 inches, hem half an inch off the floor. If your ceilings are 9 feet, the rod goes higher and the standard panel is 95 inches; at 10 feet it is 108 inches. The ready-made length ladder — 63, 84, 95, 108 inches — is really a ceiling-height ladder, and the table below maps each standard panel length to the rod height and ceiling it fits.

Panel lengthRod height from floorCeiling heightTypical window
63 in.63 to 64 in.8 ft, rod mounted lowShort windows, basement, above furniture
84 in.84 to 85 in.8 ft standardMost living rooms and bedrooms
95 in.95 to 96 in.9 ftTaller windows, 9 ft ceilings

If your rod height falls between two standard lengths, buy the longer panel and hem it — a 95-inch panel hemmed to 90 inches looks identical to a 90-inch panel, while an 84-inch panel on a 90-inch rod looks 6 inches short and cannot be fixed. The hemming rule is worth remembering when you shop: always round length up, never down. The same rule applies to puddle length: buy the panel that is 2 to 4 inches longer than the floor measurement for a soft break, or 6 to 8 inches longer for a full puddle, and never guess at the puddle — measure the floor length first, then add the puddle on top of it.

Worked example 1: a 36-inch bedroom window

Take the most common window in an American bedroom: 36 inches wide, 48 inches tall, in a room with an 8-foot ceiling. The rod goes 6 inches above the frame and 6 inches past it on each side, so the rod is 48 inches wide and sits 84 inches from the floor — the top of the window frame is at about 78 inches, the rod at 84 inches is 6 inches above it, and the floor-length drop from the rod is 84 inches. For a clean standard fit, mount the rod at 84 inches from the floor and buy 84-inch panels.

The width math: 48-inch rod × 2 times fullness = 96 inches of fabric. Two standard 54-inch panels give 108 inches, which is 2.25 times fullness — nicely gathered, not skimpy. The shopping list is 2 panels, 54 × 84 inches, one pair. If the fabric is sheer, the same window needs 2.5 times — 120 inches — which is still 2 panels at 54 inches each giving 108 inches (2.25 times, slightly under the sheer ideal) or 3 panels giving 162 inches (3.4 times, very full). For sheers on a small window, 2 panels are the practical answer; the third panel would cost as much as the first two and the stack would cover a third of the window.

The length math: rod at 84 inches from the floor, floor length is 84 minus ½ inch, so an 84-inch panel hangs with its hem half an inch off the floor. If the room has a radiator under the window, switch to sill length instead: measure from the rod to the sill — about 36 inches on this window — minus half an inch, and buy the 63-inch panel only if the rod is mounted low; otherwise the panel needs hemming to 36 inches, which is a lot of fabric to cut off and a sign that a shorter ready-made length or a custom panel is the better buy.

Worked example 2: a 60-inch living room window

Now the living room picture window: 60 inches wide, 60 inches tall, 8-foot ceiling, and this is the room where the curtains are part of the furniture, so the fullness matters. The rod is 72 inches wide (60 plus 6 on each side) and mounted 6 inches above the frame, at 84 inches from the floor. The width math at 2 times fullness is 72 × 2 = 144 inches of fabric. Three standard panels give 162 inches, which is 2.25 times — the right look for a living room. Two panels would give 108 inches, which is 1.5 times, and on a window this size 1.5 times reads as flat from the sofa. Buy 3 panels, 54 × 84 inches.

Hang them as one and a half panels per side: the middle panel is split between the two sides, or the third panel is folded so that each side has a full panel and a half. The stack-back on this window is about a third of 162 inches, or 54 inches of bunch — 27 inches per side — and the rod extends 6 inches past the frame on each side, so about 21 inches of stack still sits on the glass when the curtains are open. That is the trade-off of a full curtain on a wide window: the folds that look good closed cost light when open. If the room needs the light, extend the rod to 8 or 10 inches per side, which moves more of the stack onto the wall.

Cost it out: three mid-range ready-made linen panels at $45 each are $135, a 72 to 96-inch rod is about $35, and rings and finials add $20 — about $190 all in. The same window in custom linen would be 2 panels of 72 inches each at roughly $180 per panel, or $360 plus hardware — about double the ready-made price, and the only real gain is the exact 2 times fullness with no rounding and a lining of your choice. For most living rooms the ready-made 3-panel answer is the right spend.

Worked example 3: a 96-inch sliding door

The sliding door is the hardest window to size, because it is both a window and a door, and the curtain has to clear the handle and the traffic. The door is 96 inches wide, the rod is 108 inches (96 plus 6 on each side, though 10 inches per side is better here so the stack clears the door frame), and the drop is floor length — a sliding door never takes a sill-length curtain. The width math at 2 times fullness is 108 × 2 = 216 inches of fabric. Four standard 54-inch panels give exactly 216 inches, which is exactly 2 times, and that is the rare case where the standard sizes line up perfectly. The shopping list is 4 panels, 54 × 84 inches for an 8-foot ceiling, or 54 × 95 for a 9-foot ceiling.

The practical question is whether to use 4 standard panels or 2 wide panels. Two 108-inch wide panels give the same 216 inches with one seam per side instead of two, and wide panels usually cost less per inch than standard ones — a pair of 108-inch blackout panels runs about $120 to $180, while 4 standard panels run $160 to $240 for the same coverage. The wide panels also stack more neatly, because there are fewer folds to bunch. The case for 4 standard panels is flexibility: you can open just one side for the door, or split them as two per side, and if one panel is damaged it is cheaper to replace.

One detail that catches people on sliding doors: the curtain must clear the door handle, which sticks out 2 to 3 inches from the door. A rod mounted on standard brackets holds the curtain about 3 inches off the wall, which is just enough, but a rod with a 4-inch projection is safer, and a ceiling-mounted track is the cleanest answer of all for a sliding door — the track holds the curtain 4 to 5 inches off the wall, the panels glide instead of dragging on a rod, and the stack sits entirely clear of the door. If the door is used daily, budget for the track; if it is used rarely, the rod is fine.

Curtain cost is driven by two things — the number of panels and the price per panel — and the table below prices the three worked examples at three quality levels, using typical US retail prices in 2026. The cheapest mistake in curtains is buying too few panels. A 60-inch window dressed with a single pair of panels at 1.5 times fullness costs $50 to $90 and looks under-dressed every day for the next five years; adding the third panel costs $25 to $45 once and fixes it permanently. The second cheapest mistake is buying the wrong length and paying for hemming — a $15 to $25 per panel alteration on a $30 panel is nearly the price of the panel itself, and it is avoidable by buying the longer standard length and hemming only when the rod height genuinely falls between sizes. And the most expensive mistake is ordering custom panels from the wrong measurements: a custom panel cannot be returned, so the $150 to $350 per panel is lost if the width or the length is wrong. Measure twice, order once, and run the numbers through the free Curtain Size Calculator before you pay for anything custom.

Fabric choice moves the price as much as the size does. A pair of 54 × 84 polyester panels is $25 to $40; the same size in linen is $60 to $120; in velvet it is $90 to $200. Lining adds $20 to $40 per panel for a standard lining and $30 to $60 for blackout lining, and it is worth it on any curtain that faces the sun — unlined linen fades in a south-facing window within two years, and a $30 lining protects a $90 panel. If the budget is tight, spend it on fullness before fabric: a full polyester curtain looks better than a flat linen one, because the folds are what the eye reads first.

The first two mistakes are the ones that show up in almost every "why do my curtains look wrong" photo: the curtain is too narrow and too short at the same time, because the buyer measured the window, bought one pair of panels in the length that matched the window height, and hung them on a rod that barely covers the frame. The fix for both is the same — measure the rod, multiply by 2, measure the drop to the floor — and it costs one extra panel and the longer standard length, which is usually under $50. The rod mistakes are cheaper still: moving a rod up 4 inches and out 6 inches costs nothing but two new screw holes, and it makes the window look taller and wider at the same time. If you can only fix one thing, fix the rod height — a curtain hung high makes the whole wall look taller, and it is the single most visible change in the room.

How to measure, step by step

Do the measuring in this order, with a steel tape, a pencil and a notepad — not a cloth tape, which stretches, and not from memory. First, measure the window width at the frame, in three places, and take the widest. Second, decide the rod extension — 6 inches per side is the standard — and add it to the window width to get the rod width. Third, multiply the rod width by your fullness factor — 2 for most fabrics — to get the total fabric width. Fourth, divide by the panel width, 54 inches for ready-made, and round up to the panel count. Fifth, decide the rod height — 4 to 6 inches above the frame, or high enough that the standard panel length reaches the floor — and measure from the rod to the floor, the sill or the puddle point, at three places, taking the shortest. Sixth, match the drop to the nearest standard length at or above it: 63, 84, 95 or 108 inches. Write all six numbers down before you shop, and take the notepad with you — the aisle is where the numbers get forgotten.

Two measuring traps deserve a final note. The first is measuring the window width inside the frame instead of outside it: the frame is the finished edge, and the rod sits outside it, so the width that matters is the outside of the trim, not the glass. The second is measuring the length with the curtain rings in your hand but not on the rod: rings add about 1.5 inches of drop, and a panel that is perfect without rings is 1.5 inches short with them. Measure with the hardware you will actually use — rod, rings and all — or add the ring drop to the measurement. Frequently asked questions

What size curtains do I need for a 36-inch window?

Use a 48-inch rod (36 inches plus 6 inches on each side), 2 times fullness for 96 inches of fabric, and 2 standard 54-inch panels, which give 108 inches or 2.25 times fullness. For length, mount the rod 84 inches from the floor on an 8-foot ceiling and buy 84-inch panels for a floor-length hang, or measure rod to sill minus half an inch for a sill-length kitchen curtain.

How wide should curtains be compared to the window?

The fabric should be 1.5 to 2.5 times as wide as the rod, and the rod should be 6 to 12 inches wider than the window. At 2 times fullness — the standard — a 48-inch rod needs 96 inches of fabric, which is 2 standard 54-inch panels. Below 1.5 times the curtain hangs flat with no folds; above 2.5 times the stack gets so deep it covers the window when open.

Should curtains touch the floor?

Floor-length curtains should end half an inch to one inch above the floor, so the hem just kisses the floor without dragging or collecting dust. A puddle length deliberately adds 2 to 8 inches past the floor for a formal look, but it collects dust and pet hair and is a trip hazard, so it belongs in low-traffic formal rooms, not in a kitchen or a child's bedroom.

How high should I hang curtains?

Mount the rod 4 to 6 inches above the window frame, or higher — near the ceiling — to make the window and the room look taller. On an 8-foot ceiling that puts the rod about 84 inches from the floor, which is why 84-inch panels are the best-selling ready-made length. Never mount the rod on the frame itself; the stack will cover the glass and the window will look smaller.

How many curtain panels do I need?

Divide the total fabric width (rod width times fullness, usually 2 times) by the panel width (54 inches for standard ready-made panels) and round up. A 36-inch window needs 2 panels, a 48-inch window needs 2 to 3, a 60-inch window needs 3, a 72-inch window needs 4, and a 96-inch sliding door needs 4 standard panels or 2 wide 108-inch panels. Always buy at least 2 panels — a single panel looks wrong on any window.

What is the standard curtain panel size?

Standard ready-made panels are 54 inches wide, in lengths of 63, 84, 95 and 108 inches. The 84-inch length fits an 8-foot ceiling, 95 inches fits a 9-foot ceiling, and 108 inches fits a 10-foot ceiling. If your rod height falls between two standard lengths, buy the longer one and hem it — a panel that is too short cannot be fixed.
