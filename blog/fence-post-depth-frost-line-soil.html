How Deep Should Fence Posts Be? Frost Line, Soil and Rules

How deep should fence posts be? As a starting rule, bury at least one-third of the post's total length, with a minimum of 24 inches for a standard 6 ft fence — so a 6 ft fence needs a 9 ft post set 3 ft deep, and an 8 ft fence needs a 12 ft post set 4 ft deep. But that rule is only the floor. In cold climates the hole must also reach below the frost line, or winter will heave the post upward a little each year until the fence leans. In soft or sandy soil, depth alone is not enough — the hole needs to be wider, or the post needs a concrete footing that spreads the load. This guide works through all three factors — height, frost and soil — with worked depths for line posts, gate posts and corner posts, and the mistakes that make fences lean within two winters.

The one-third rule, and why it is only the floor

The one-third rule says the buried portion of a fence post should be at least one-third of the post's total length. A 6 ft fence therefore uses a 9 ft post with 3 ft underground; a 4 ft fence uses a 6 ft post with 2 ft underground; an 8 ft fence uses a 12 ft post with 4 ft underground. The logic is leverage: wind pushing on the fence above ground acts as a lever, and the buried section is the anchor resisting it. The taller the fence, the longer the lever arm, and the deeper the anchor must be. A 6 ft privacy fence catches a lot of wind — it is effectively a sail — so its posts work much harder than the posts of an open picket fence of the same height.

But one-third is a minimum, not a target. Three things push you deeper than the rule. The first is the frost line, covered next, which can demand 4 ft or more even for a short fence in northern states. The second is soil: in loose sand or soft fill, a post at one-third depth can still rock in its hole, because the soil itself gives way before the post does. The third is load: gate posts, corner posts and end posts carry more force than line posts, so they go deeper and get bigger footings, as the worked examples below show. If you remember one sentence from this guide, make it this one: the depth you dig is the greater of the one-third rule and the frost line depth, plus extra for gates, corners and bad soil.

Frost line: the depth that decides in cold climates

When wet soil freezes, it expands — by roughly 9% by volume — and that expansion pushes upward on anything buried in it. A post whose footing sits above the frost line gets gripped by the freezing soil and lifted a fraction of an inch each winter, a process called frost heave. One winter's lift is barely visible. Five winters of it, and the post sits an inch or two high, the rails no longer line up, and the fence takes on its characteristic lean. The fix is simple and non-negotiable: the bottom of the post, and the bottom of its concrete footing, must sit below the local frost line, the maximum depth to which the ground freezes in a typical winter.

Frost depths vary enormously across the United States. In Florida, southern Texas and southern California the ground effectively never freezes, so the frost line is zero and the one-third rule governs alone. In the mid-Atlantic and the lower Midwest it runs roughly 30 to 36 inches. In the upper Midwest and New England it runs 42 to 60 inches, and in the far north of Minnesota, North Dakota and Maine it can reach 60 to 72 inches or more. The table below gives typical ranges; your local building department or county extension office publishes the exact frost depth for your area, and that number wins over any table, because frost depth varies with local soil, snow cover and microclimate. Snow, paradoxically, insulates the ground — a region with deep, persistent snow cover can have a shallower frost line than a colder but windswept region nearby.

Notice what the table does to the one-third rule. A 6 ft fence in Minnesota needs a post roughly 11 to 12 ft long — 6 ft above ground plus 5 to 6 ft below — not the 9 ft post the one-third rule alone would suggest. Buying 9 ft posts in a frost-line state is one of the most common fence mistakes, and it is an expensive one to fix, because the fix is digging every post out and starting over. When you buy posts, buy for the frost depth first and the fence height second: total post length equals fence height plus the greater of one-third of the post length and the frost depth, plus a small margin for uneven ground.

Soil type: when depth alone is not enough

Soil decides how a post behaves in its hole, and the four common types behave very differently. Clay holds a post tightly when dry but swells when wet and shrinks when dry, working the post loose over wet-dry cycles; clay also holds water against the post, which accelerates rot at the ground line, so drainage matters as much as depth. Sand drains beautifully but grips poorly — a post in loose sand needs a wider hole and a concrete footing, or the wind will rock it loose even at full depth. Gravelly soil drains well and grips well, and is the easiest soil to build a long-lasting fence in. Loam, the gardener's favorite, sits in the middle: decent grip, decent drainage, and the one-third rule plus frost depth usually suffices.

Two practical adjustments follow from soil type. First, hole width: a standard post hole is about three times the post width — roughly 12 inches across for a 4x4 post — but in sand or soft fill, widen it to 14 or 16 inches and let the concrete footing spread the load over more soil. Second, footing material: in gravel or firm loam, a post can be set in compacted gravel alone, which drains and lets you replace the post later without breaking concrete. In clay or sand, concrete is the safer choice, because it locks the post to a larger mass that resists both wind leverage and frost grip. Whichever footing you choose, the bottom of the footing — not just the bottom of the post — must sit below the frost line, or the frost will lift footing and post together.

One more soil trap: fill dirt. A fence built on a recently graded lot, a filled-in pool area, or a berm of imported topsoil is not standing on real ground — it is standing on loose material that will settle and shift for years. Posts in fill need to reach through the fill into undisturbed soil below, which can mean holes 4 to 5 ft deep even for a short fence in a warm climate. If you can push a probe rod easily through the top 2 ft, you are in fill, and you should dig until the rod meets resistance. A fence that looks perfect in year one and leans in year three was usually built on fill nobody tested.

Worked examples: line, gate and corner posts

Take a standard 6 ft privacy fence in Ohio, where the frost line runs about 32 inches. The one-third rule gives 36 inches of burial; the frost line demands at least 32 inches plus a margin, so 36 inches governs, and the post is 9 ft long with 3 ft underground. In gravel or firm loam, a 12-inch hole with concrete or compacted gravel works. That is the textbook case, and it is the fence most guides describe — which is why so many guides mislead builders in other regions and soils.

Now the same fence in Minnesota, where the frost line runs about 60 inches. The one-third rule still says 36 inches, but frost demands 60, so the post is 11 ft long with 5 ft underground, in a hole whose concrete footing bottoms out below 60 inches. The post costs more, the holes take longer to dig, and the fence costs noticeably more than the Ohio version — but the alternative is a fence that heaves every winter. The honest budget for a northern fence includes the longer posts from the start, not as a surprise discovered at the lumber yard.

Gate posts are a different animal. A gate is heavy, it swings, and every swing levers the post. Gate posts should go at least 6 inches deeper than line posts, and for a wide or heavy gate — a 10 ft or 12 ft double drive gate, or a solid wood gate — a full foot deeper, with a wider footing (16 to 18 inches across) and more concrete. A 6 ft fence with a 4 ft walk gate in Ohio therefore has line posts at 36 inches and gate posts at 42 inches, set in 16-inch footings. Corner posts and end posts sit between the two: they carry the pull of the fence in two directions (corners) or the full tension of the run (ends), so give them the gate-post treatment — 6 inches deeper than line posts — even though nothing swings on them. The extra concrete and depth on a handful of posts is the cheapest insurance on the whole fence.

Concrete, gravel, or dry-set? Choosing the footing

Three footing methods dominate, and each has a place. Wet-set concrete — post placed in the hole, concrete poured around it, post braced plumb while it cures — is the strongest and the default for gate, corner and frost-line posts. Its weakness is drainage: concrete holds water against the wood at the ground line, so dome the top of the concrete slightly above grade and slope it away from the post, so water sheds instead of pooling. Dry-set concrete mix — dry mix poured around the post, then watered from above — is popular with DIY builders because it needs no mixing, but it cures unevenly in dry soil and should be reserved for line posts in mild climates, not gate posts. Compacted gravel — the hole backfilled in layers with crushed stone, each layer tamped — drains best, makes post replacement easy, and is a legitimate choice for line posts in well-drained soil, but it grips less than concrete, so it is a poor choice in sand or for gate posts.

Whichever method you choose, three details matter more than the method itself. First, keep the post bottom off the very bottom of the hole: set the post on 2 to 4 inches of gravel so water drains below it instead of pooling under the end grain, which is where rot starts. Second, brace the post plumb in both directions while the footing sets — a post that cures an inch out of plumb stays an inch out of plumb for the life of the fence, and no amount of rail adjustment fully hides it. Third, never let concrete or soil sit above grade against the post: the ground line is where posts rot, and burying the post deeper into a mound of soil or mulch only moves the rot zone upward. Finish grade should sit just below the top of the concrete dome, with the post exposed above it.

Rules, permits and the call before you dig

Depth rules from guides do not override local rules. Some municipalities specify minimum post depths or footing sizes in their fence ordinances, and some homeowners' associations specify post material or depth indirectly through height and style rules. Front-yard fences often face a height limit of 3 to 4 ft, which changes the post math entirely — a 4 ft fence needs only a 6 ft post at 24 inches in a mild climate, but still needs frost depth in a cold one. Before digging anywhere in the United States, call 811 (the national "Call Before You Dig" number) at least a few business days ahead: utilities are marked free, and hitting a buried gas or electric line is a danger no depth table can excuse. Mark the fence line, confirm property boundaries against a survey rather than a neighbor's memory, and check for easements that forbid building over a utility corridor — a fence built over an easement can be ordered removed at your expense.

The five mistakes that make fences lean

First, digging to the one-third rule in a frost-line state, which guarantees heave. Second, using the nominal post length as the buried depth — a 9 ft post set with 6 ft above ground is only 3 ft deep, and on a slope the downhill side of the fence shows less post above ground, tempting builders to set posts shallow on the high side; measure burial from the lowest adjacent grade, not the highest. Third, setting posts in concrete that stops above the frost line, so the footing itself becomes the thing the frost lifts. Fourth, skipping bracing while concrete cures, so posts drift out of plumb before the fence is even built. Fifth, backfilling with the excavated topsoil instead of gravel or concrete — topsoil holds water, settles, and leaves the post loose within a season. Every leaning fence you have ever seen traces back to one of these five, and none of them is a materials problem; all of them are depth and footing decisions made on digging day, when they are still cheap to get right.

Estimate disclaimer: Depths are planning guidance based on standard practice and typical frost lines. Frost depth, soil conditions and local fence rules vary — confirm the frost line and any permit requirements with your local building department, and call 811 before digging.

Frequently asked questions

How deep should fence posts be for a 6 ft fence?

At least 36 inches (one-third of a 9 ft post) in a mild climate, and below the local frost line in cold climates — 48 to 60 inches in the upper Midwest and New England. Dig to the greater of the one-third rule and the frost line depth.

What is the frost line, and why does it matter for fences?

The frost line is the maximum depth the ground freezes in winter. Soil expands as it freezes and grips the post, lifting it a little each winter (frost heave). The bottom of the post and its footing must sit below the frost line or the fence will lean over a few winters.

How deep should gate posts be?

At least 6 inches deeper than line posts, and a full foot deeper for wide or heavy gates, with a wider concrete footing (16 to 18 inches across). A 6 ft fence with a 4 ft walk gate in a mild climate has line posts at 36 inches and gate posts at 42 inches.

Do fence posts need concrete?

Not always. Compacted gravel works for line posts in well-drained gravel or loam and makes replacement easier. Concrete is the safer choice in clay or sand, for gate and corner posts, and anywhere the frost line is deep, because it locks the post to a larger mass.

How deep should fence posts be in sandy soil?

At least the one-third rule and frost depth, plus a wider hole (14 to 16 inches) and a concrete footing — sand grips poorly, so depth alone will not hold the post against wind. In soft fill, dig through the fill into undisturbed soil below.

Should fence posts sit on gravel at the bottom of the hole?

Yes — set the post on 2 to 4 inches of gravel so water drains below the end grain instead of pooling under it. Pooling water at the post bottom is a leading cause of rot, and it starts where you cannot see it.
