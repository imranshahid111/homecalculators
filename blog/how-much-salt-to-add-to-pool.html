How Much Salt to Add to a Pool: The PPM Guide

A saltwater pool does not run on guesswork, and the number on the bag is not the answer — the answer is how many pounds of salt your pool's exact volume needs to reach your chlorinator's target. For the most common setup, a 20,000 gallon pool filled with fresh water needs about 534 pounds of salt — fourteen 40-pound bags — to reach the usual 3,200 ppm target. A 15,000 gallon pool needs about 400 pounds, and a 10,000 gallon pool about 267 pounds. But most pool owners are not starting from zero: if your water already tests at 2,400 ppm, that same 20,000 gallon pool needs only about 134 pounds, or three to four bags. This guide works through the one formula behind every salt dose, gives you the pounds-and-bags table for pools from 10,000 to 30,000 gallons, shows the top-up amount from any current reading, prices the job, and explains what to do when you overshoot — because the only thing worse than too little salt is too much, and there is no chemical that removes it.

The one formula behind every salt dose

Salt is measured in ppm — parts per million by weight — which is just the weight of salt divided by the weight of the water. One gallon of water weighs 8.34 pounds, so the formula that turns a ppm shortfall into pounds of salt is simple: pounds of salt = gallons × (target ppm − current ppm) × 8.34 ÷ 1,000,000. Every term matters. The gallons are your pool's real volume, not the number on the brochure — a pool listed as 20,000 gallons often holds 18,500 once steps, benches and the actual waterline are accounted for, and a 7 percent volume error is a 7 percent salt error. The target ppm comes from your chlorinator's manual, not from a neighbor: most systems want 2,700–3,400 ppm with a common target of 3,200, but some units are specified up to 4,500 ppm, and running far above your unit's range triggers high-salt shutdowns and is hard on heaters and metal fittings. The current ppm comes from a test, not from the cell display — a liquid test kit or fresh test strips give a more honest number than the chlorinator's own reading, which can drift as the cell ages.

Run the formula once by hand and the pattern sticks. Take a 20,000 gallon pool starting from fresh water, target 3,200 ppm: 20,000 × 3,200 × 8.34 ÷ 1,000,000 = 534 pounds. Divide by 40 and you get 13.4 bags, so buy 14. Now take the same pool in spring, testing at 2,400 ppm: the shortfall is only 800 ppm, so 20,000 × 800 × 8.34 ÷ 1,000,000 = 134 pounds — three and a half bags, so buy 4. The difference between the two jobs is the difference between a fresh fill and a seasonal top-up, and it is why "how many bags" is a meaningless question without the current reading. One handy rule of thumb falls out of the same math: one pound of salt raises a 10,000 gallon pool by about 12 ppm, so a 20,000 gallon pool moves about 6 ppm per pound and a 15,000 gallon pool about 8 ppm per pound. Two things in that table deserve a second look. First, the cost is a one-time hit, not a running cost: salt does not evaporate and the cell barely consumes it, so a fresh fill's salt lasts for years and the annual top-up is a few bags at most. Second, the table assumes your volume is right, and volume is where most dosing errors start. If your pool is an irregular shape, a kidney or a freeform, measure it properly before you buy salt — the free Pond Volume Calculator works from length, width and average depth and is a good check on any volume estimate, and a rectangular pool is simply length × width × average depth × 7.48 gallons per cubic foot. A pool that is really 18,000 gallons dosed as if it were 20,000 ends up about 350 ppm over target, which is still inside most ranges — but a pool dosed the other way round, 20,000 gallons treated as 15,000, lands near 2,400 ppm and the cell complains it is starving.

The same gaps scale with pool size in direct proportion: a 10,000 gallon pool needs exactly half the amounts in the table, a 30,000 gallon pool needs half as much again. So a 15,000 gallon pool at 2,400 ppm needs about 100 pounds — two and a half bags, buy three — and a 30,000 gallon pool at 2,000 ppm needs about 300 pounds, or eight bags. The practical rule is to dose in two stages whenever the gap is large. Add about two-thirds of the calculated amount, circulate for 24 hours, retest, and then add the rest. Salt dissolves slowly and the test reading lags the dose by a full day, so a single full dose based on a stale or low reading is the classic way to overshoot — and unlike an undershoot, an overshoot cannot be fixed by adding anything. If the reading is only 100–200 ppm short, do not chase it at all: a 200 ppm gap on a 20,000 gallon pool is one bag, the cell will run fine slightly low, and the next splash-out and refill cycle will move the number anyway.

What salt to buy — and what not to use

Pool salt is just high-purity sodium chloride without anti-caking additives, and a 40-pound bag typically costs $7–$12 in the USA. That is the only salt that belongs in the pool. Table salt is iodized and contains anti-caking agents that cloud the water and can stain; water-softener pellets often contain additives too and dissolve slowly in clumps. Rock salt and solar salt sold for pools are fine as long as the bag says pool salt and lists no additives. Buy one extra bag beyond the calculation and keep it dry: salt absorbs moisture and turns into one solid brick in a damp shed, and a brick does not pour, does not dissolve evenly, and has to be smashed apart before it is useful. Store bags off the floor on a pallet or shelf, sealed, and they keep indefinitely.

The price gap between the cheapest and the most expensive pool salt on the shelf is mostly branding. The salt itself is the same sodium chloride either way, and the cell does not care about the label. What does matter is the form: fine-grain pool salt dissolves faster than coarse crystals, which matters when you are dosing in spring and want the reading stable within a day. Either form reaches the same ppm in the end; the fine grain just gets there sooner. Do not pay a premium for salt marketed as "cell-friendly" or "extra pure" unless your manual specifically calls for it — the standard is already 99.8 percent pure sodium chloride or better.

How to add salt, step by step

Adding salt is a process, not a pour. Done in the right order it takes ten minutes of work and a day of waiting; done wrong it leaves undissolved piles on the pool floor and a cell reading that swings for a week.

1. Test first, dose second. Take a water sample from elbow depth, away from the returns, and test with a liquid kit or fresh strips. Write the number down. If the cell display and the kit disagree by more than about 200 ppm, trust the kit — the cell's salt sensor is the part that drifts, and a failing sensor is one of the commonest reasons a pool gets overdosed.

2. Switch the salt cell off. The cell should be off while salt is dissolving. A running cell reads the temporary spike of concentrated salt water passing over it, can throw a high-salt fault, and in some units will shut itself down until it is reset. Leave the pump running — circulation is what dissolves the salt.

3. Broadcast it in the shallow end. Pour the salt across the shallow end with the pump running, not in one pile and not into the skimmer. Salt poured into the skimmer goes straight to the filter and the cell in a concentrated slug. Spread it, brush any piles that settle toward the main drain, and do not vacuum undissolved salt into the filter.

4. Circulate a full 24 hours, then retest. Salt takes about a day to dissolve fully and distribute evenly, longer in cold water. Do not test after two hours and add more because the number has not moved — that is how pools end up 500 ppm over target. After 24 hours, test again, switch the cell back on, and add the remainder only if the reading still shows a real shortfall.

5. Swim after it dissolves. Wait until the salt has fully dissolved and circulated — typically 24 hours — before swimming. Undissolved salt on the floor is rough underfoot and can bleach or mark some finishes if it sits in one spot, which is another reason to brush piles apart rather than leave them.

Why your pool keeps losing salt

Salt only leaves with water. It does not evaporate, it is not burned off by the sun, and the cell converts only a tiny fraction of it into chlorine, which then reverts to salt again — the loop is nearly closed. So when the level drops, water left the pool. The usual suspects are splash-out from swimmers, backwashing the filter, rain overflow, and leaks. A pool that loses an inch of water a week to splash-out and backwash on a 20,000 gallon pool is losing roughly 500 gallons a week, and at 3,200 ppm that is about 13 pounds of salt a week — a bag every three weeks in heavy swim season. That is normal, and it is why a spring top-up of three to four bags and a mid-season bag or two is the typical annual pattern.

If you are losing salt much faster than you are losing water, suspect a leak. The test is simple: mark the water level, turn the pump off for 24 hours, and see whether the level drops. If it drops with the pump off, the leak is in the shell or fittings; if it only drops with the pump running, it is in the plumbing. Either way, fix the leak before dosing again — adding salt to a leaking pool is pouring money down a hole you can measure in bags. A pool that needs salt every month is not a pool with a salt problem; it is a pool with a water problem.

Too much salt: how to fix it safely

Too much salt shows up three ways: the water tastes noticeably salty, the chlorinator shuts down on a high-salt fault, and the corrosion risk on heaters, ladders and metal fittings rises. There is no chemical neutralizer and no filter setting that removes salt — the only fix is to dilute it by partially draining the pool and refilling with fresh water. The math is proportional: to bring a 20,000 gallon pool from 4,500 ppm down to 3,200 ppm, drain the fraction (4,500 − 3,200) ÷ 4,500, which is about 29 percent, or roughly 5,800 gallons, then refill. Drain in stages and retest between stages if you can, because draining a pool too far brings its own risks — a pool drained below the skimmer in a high water table can lift, and plaster exposed to hot sun can crack. Never drain an inground pool completely without professional advice.

Prevention is cheaper than the fix. The overshoot almost always comes from dosing on a stale reading, dosing the full calculated amount in one go, or trusting a cell display that reads low. Dose two-thirds, wait 24 hours, retest, then finish. If your chlorinator manual gives a range rather than a single number, aim for the middle of the range, not the top — the cell runs the same at 3,000 ppm as at 3,400 ppm, and the middle leaves room for a test error in either direction. And if the pool was just refilled after a repair or a drain, test before you dose at all: municipal water in some areas already carries a few hundred ppm of salt, and that is salt you do not need to buy.

How much salt costs per year

Put the numbers together and the annual salt bill is small. A 20,000 gallon pool costs $98–$168 to salt from a fresh fill, then roughly $28–$48 a year in top-ups — three to four bags in spring and one or two more across the season, at $7–$12 a bag. A 10,000 gallon pool is about half that; a 30,000 gallon pool about half as much again. Compare that with the chlorine a traditional pool burns through — a 20,000 gallon chlorine pool easily uses $300–$600 of chlorine, shock and stabilizer a season — and the salt system's running cost is a fraction of the old way. The real cost of a salt system is the cell, which lasts roughly 3–7 years and costs several hundred dollars to replace, not the salt. Salt is the cheapest part of owning a saltwater pool, which is exactly why it is worth dosing carefully: the money at stake is small, but the hassle of an overshoot — a drained pool, a faulted cell, a week of rebalancing — is not.

What ppm should my saltwater pool be?

How long after adding salt can I swim?

Can I use table salt or water softener salt in my pool?
