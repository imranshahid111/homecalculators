Fabric Widths Explained: 36, 44, 54 and 60 Inch Bolts

This fabric width guide answers the question that decides your yardage before you measure a single length: how wide is the bolt you are buying from? Fabric is sold by the yard, but a yard off a 36-inch bolt is a strip 36 inches wide and 36 inches long, while a yard off a 60-inch bolt is nearly twice as much cloth — 60 inches wide and 36 inches long. The same queen quilt top needs 9 yards of length from a 44-inch bolt and only 6 yards from a 60-inch bolt, and the same four curtain panels need 12 yards from 54-inch decorator fabric but 16 yards from 44-inch cotton. Width is the first number in every yardage calculation, and this guide works through each of the four standard widths — 36, 44, 54 and 60 inches — with what each is made for, how much usable width you actually get, and worked numbers for quilts, curtains and a dress, so you can pick the width first and calculate second.

The short answer: four widths, four jobs

Fabric bolts come in a handful of standard widths, and each width belongs to a family of fabrics and projects. Thirty-six inches is the narrow craft width — muslin, craft cotton and linings — and almost every real project needs two widths of it. Forty-four to 45 inches is the quilting cotton width, the default for quilts, cushion covers and aprons, and the width most patterns assume. Fifty-four inches is the home-decor width — decorator fabric and upholstery — and it is the width that makes curtains work, because one panel fits across one width. Fifty-eight to 60 inches is the apparel width, the default for dresses, skirts and shirts. A fifth width, 108 inches, exists for quilt backing, and it is the width that turns a three-width quilt back into a single width.

The rule that ties them together is simple: widths needed equals the project width divided by the fabric width, rounded up to a whole number. A 90-inch-wide quilt top from 44-inch fabric needs 3 widths, because 2 widths give only 88 inches. The same top from 54-inch fabric needs 2 widths, and from 108-inch backing fabric needs 1. The yardage follows directly — widths times cut length, divided by 36, plus a 10 percent allowance — which is exactly the order the free Fabric Yardage Calculator uses. If you remember only one thing from this guide, remember the order: fit the width first, then multiply the length.

What bolt width actually means: usable width vs printed width

The width printed on the bolt end is the full width of the fabric, selvage to selvage. The usable width is about an inch less, because the selvages — the tightly woven edges, roughly half an inch on each side — are usually trimmed off or kept out of the visible piece. A 44-inch bolt gives about 43 usable inches, a 54-inch bolt gives about 53, and a 60-inch bolt gives about 57 to 59, depending on the mill. That missing inch matters exactly at the boundary: a 54-inch-wide curtain panel does not fit on 54-inch fabric once the selvage is excluded, and a 44-inch-wide piece does not fit on 44-inch fabric. If your project width is within an inch of the fabric width, treat it as not fitting and plan an extra width.

The printed width is also not a promise. Apparel fabrics are the worst offenders: a bolt labelled 58 to 60 inches can measure anywhere from 56 to 60 inches depending on the mill, the dye lot and how tightly the fabric was wound. Quilting cottons are the most consistent, usually 44 to 45 inches with little variation. Decorator fabrics are usually a true 54 inches, but heavy upholstery cloth can run a half inch narrow after the backing is applied. The practical habit is to measure the bolt you will actually buy, selvage to selvage, with a tape measure, before you calculate — it takes ten seconds, and it is the only width that counts. Online listings are the least reliable: the width shown on a product page is the manufacturer's nominal width, and the bolt that arrives can be an inch narrower.

Two more width facts belong here. First, the width is measured across the fabric, perpendicular to the selvage; the length is measured along the bolt, and a yard is always 36 inches of length, whatever the width. Second, fabric is cut across the full width unless the pattern says otherwise, so a "yard" is a rectangle of the full bolt width by 36 inches of length — which is why a yard of 60-inch fabric contains roughly 67 percent more cloth than a yard of 36-inch fabric, and why comparing prices per yard across different widths without adjusting for width is meaningless. A $15 yard of 60-inch fabric is cheaper per square inch than a $12 yard of 44-inch fabric.

Third, the 108-inch backing width is the cheapest way to finish a quilt: one width covers the whole back, where 44-inch fabric would need three widths and roughly three times the yardage.

36-inch fabric: the narrow craft width

Thirty-six-inch fabric is the narrowest standard width still in common use, and it is the width of craft cottons, muslin and lining fabrics. A yard of it is 36 inches wide and 36 inches long — one square yard of cloth — which makes the mental math easy but the yardage punishing: almost every project wider than 35 inches needs two widths, and a wide project needs three. A 90-inch quilt top from 36-inch fabric needs 3 widths, because 2 widths give only 70 inches; the same top from 44-inch fabric also needs 3 widths, so the narrow bolt costs the same yardage as quilting cotton on a quilt top — but on a 54-inch curtain panel, 36-inch fabric needs 2 widths per panel, and four panels need 8 widths instead of 4.

Where 36-inch fabric earns its place is in small work. Doll clothes, small craft projects, linings, muslin test garments and applique pieces all fit inside 35 usable inches, and the narrow bolt is cheaper per yard than wider fabric of the same quality — $5 to $10 a yard for craft cotton against $8 to $15 for quilting cotton. Muslin is the classic use: a test garment, or toile, is cut from cheap 36-inch muslin to check the fit before the real fabric is cut, and a dress that needs 3 yards of 60-inch apparel fabric needs about 5 yards of 36-inch muslin for the same test, because the pieces cannot fit across the narrow bolt. The saving is still real, because muslin is $3 to $5 a yard.

The mistake to avoid with 36-inch fabric is buying it for a wide project because the price per yard looks low. A $6 yard of 36-inch fabric is $6 per square yard; a $12 yard of 60-inch fabric is $7.20 per square yard — nearly the same price per square inch, and the wider fabric needs fewer yards. Always compare price per square yard, not price per yard: divide the price per yard by the width in inches and multiply by 36. On that measure, 36-inch fabric is rarely the bargain it looks like, and it is never the right choice for curtains or a quilt top.

44 to 45-inch fabric: the quilting cotton width

Forty-four to 45 inches is the width of quilting cotton, and it is the width the whole quilting world is built around. Patterns assume it, quilt shops stock it, and the yardage tables in the fabric yardage guide are calculated from it. The usable width is about 43 inches, and the width is consistent from bolt to bolt — quilting cotton is the most reliable width in the store, usually 44 to 45 inches with little variation between mills.

The arithmetic of 44-inch fabric is the arithmetic of quilts. A 90-inch-wide queen top needs 3 widths, because 2 widths give only 88 inches. A 72-inch twin top needs 2 widths, because 2 widths give 88 inches and one width gives only 44. A 60-inch throw needs 2 widths. Each width is cut to the quilt length — 108 inches for a queen — so a queen top is 3 widths times 108 inches, or 324 inches, which is 9 yards, plus the 10 percent allowance, so buy 10 yards. The same top from 54-inch fabric is 2 widths times 108 inches, or 216 inches, which is 6 yards plus allowance, so buy 7 yards — and the same top from 108-inch backing fabric is 1 width, or 3 yards plus allowance. The width is doing all the work; the length never changes.

Quilting cotton is also the default for cushion covers, aprons, tote bags and table runners, because the projects fit inside the width. A cushion cover 18 inches square needs two 20-inch squares, and two squares side by side are 40 inches across, which fits inside the 43 usable inches of a 44-inch bolt — so one 20-inch cut length gives one whole cover, and four covers are 4 times 20 inches, or 80 inches, which is 2.22 yards plus allowance, so buy 2.5 yards. An apron is a single 36-inch cut. A table runner 14 by 72 inches is a single width cut 74 inches long, or 2.25 yards. All of these are one-width projects, which is why 44-inch fabric feels generous for small work and tight for wide work: the boundary is the 43-inch usable width, and everything under it is easy.

The price is $8 to $15 a yard for quilting cotton, and the print range is the widest of any width — thousands of prints, most of them only available on 44-inch bolts. That availability is the real reason to choose 44-inch fabric: if the print you want exists only as quilting cotton, the width is decided for you, and the yardage is whatever the width-first formula says. Buy it all from the same bolt, because two dye lots of the same print can differ enough to show in daylight.

54-inch fabric: the home-decor width

Fifty-four-inch fabric is the home-decor width, and it is the width that makes curtains possible. Decorator fabric and upholstery cloth come on 54-inch bolts, the usable width is about 53 inches, and a curtain panel 54 inches wide fits across one width — which is the whole point. Four curtain panels with an 84-inch drop need 12 yards from 54-inch fabric: each panel is cut 96 inches (84 inches plus 4 inches for the header and 8 inches for the hem), and 4 panels at 96 inches is 384 inches, or 10.67 yards, plus 10 percent is 11.73 yards, so buy 12. The same four panels from 44-inch fabric would need a width and a half per panel, and the yardage would jump to about 16 yards — the 54-inch bolt saves 4 yards, or about $100 of decorator fabric at $25 a yard.

The panel math is what makes the width visible. At 2 times fullness, a 100-inch rod needs 200 inches of fabric, and 4 panels of 54 inches give 216 inches, which is 2.16 times — the right fullness. From 44-inch fabric, 4 panels give only 176 inches, which is 1.76 times, too flat for a proper curtain, and 5 panels would be needed to reach 220 inches. The full panel-count math, rod sizing and stack-back rules are in the curtain sizing guide and the wide-window guide, and the free Curtain Size Calculator applies the same formulas — but the width decision comes first, because it decides how many panels exist at all.

Fifty-four-inch fabric is also the width for tablecloths, slipcovers and large cushion covers. A tablecloth for a 60 by 84-inch table finishes 84 by 108 inches, which is two widths of 54-inch fabric seamed down the middle: 2 widths at 110 inches each is 220 inches, or 6.1 yards, plus allowance, so buy 7 yards. A slipcover for a 3-seat sofa is 14 to 16 yards of 54-inch fabric, cut into many pieces. The weight matters at this width: decorator fabric is heavy, and a yard of 54-inch upholstery cloth can weigh twice a yard of 44-inch quilting cotton, so check the weight per yard before buying for a project that will be sewn on a home machine — a heavy fabric needs a heavier needle and a walking foot.

The price is $15 to $40 a yard, the dearest of the four widths, and the print range is narrower — decorator prints are larger in scale, and a large-scale print on a 54-inch bolt can have a pattern repeat of 27 inches or more, which adds yardage of its own: each cut length is rounded up to a whole number of repeats, so a 96-inch curtain cut with a 27-inch repeat becomes 108 inches, adding 12 inches per panel. Width and repeat compound, and on a four-panel project they can add 2 yards between them.

58 to 60-inch fabric: the apparel width

Fifty-eight to 60-inch fabric is the apparel width, and it is the width most dress, skirt and shirt patterns assume. The usable width is about 57 inches, and the width varies more than any other: a bolt labelled 58 to 60 inches can measure 56 to 60 inches depending on the mill, so this is the width where measuring the actual bolt matters most. A pattern that assumes 60 inches and is laid out on a 56-inch bolt will not fit, and the pieces that do not fit are the ones discovered at the cutting table.

The arithmetic of 60-inch fabric is the arithmetic of dresses. An adult A-line dress, knee length, from 60-inch apparel fabric: the finished length is about 45 inches, plus 3 inches for the hem and seams, so the cut length is 48 inches. The front and back pieces are each about 30 inches wide at the hem, so together they are 60 inches — exactly the bolt width, and once the selvage is excluded they do not fit side by side, so the dress needs two lengths. Two lengths at 48 inches is 96 inches, or 2.67 yards, plus 10 percent is 2.93 yards, so buy 3 yards. The same dress from 44-inch fabric would need three lengths, or about 4.5 yards, because the pieces cannot fit across the narrower bolt at all — the wider bolt saves a yard and a half.

Sixty-inch fabric is also the width for wide skirts, trousers and menswear, where the pieces are wide and the pattern layout needs the full width. The price is $10 to $25 a yard, and the fabric family is the broadest — cotton lawn, linen, rayon, wool suiting, jersey and denim all come on 58 to 60-inch bolts, and the width is the same while the weight and drape vary enormously. The width does not tell you how the fabric behaves; it only tells you how much of it fits across the bolt. Always check the fibre and the weight as well as the width, and always pre-wash: rayon can shrink more than cotton, and a 48-inch dress cut that shrinks 5 percent loses over 2 inches.

One more apparel-width fact: knits and wovens at 60 inches are laid out differently. A woven dress pattern is laid out with the grain running down the length of the fabric, and the pieces cannot be rotated to save space. A knit can sometimes be laid out cross-grain, but the stretch direction changes with it, so the fit changes too. The yardage formula does not care about any of this — widths needed times cut length, divided by 36, plus allowance — but the layout does, and a pattern that fits on paper at 60 inches may not fit on the bolt you bought at 56 inches.

Worked example 1: a queen quilt top at every width

Take a queen quilt top, 90 inches wide by 108 inches long, and run it through every width. This is the example that shows the width doing all the work, because the finished size never changes — only the bolt does.

Two things stand out. First, 36-inch and 44-inch fabric cost exactly the same yardage on this top — 10 yards each — because both need 3 widths; the narrower bolt is not cheaper here, it is just narrower. Second, the jump from 44 inches to 54 inches saves 3 yards, or about $36 at $12 a yard, and the jump to 108-inch backing saves 6.5 yards against the 44-inch figure. The backing is the real saving: a queen quilt back from 44-inch fabric is 3 widths at 108 inches, or 9 yards, while the same back from 108-inch fabric is one width at 116 inches including the quilting overhang, or 3.5 yards — a saving of roughly $66 at $12 a yard, which is why wide backing fabric exists and why it is worth finding even when the print range is small.

The same table also shows why the usable width matters: 2 widths of 44-inch fabric give 88 inches, which is 2 inches short of a 90-inch top, so the third width is unavoidable. There is no clever layout that saves it — the pieces are 90 inches wide, the fabric is 44 inches wide, and two widths are 88 inches. The only ways to use fewer widths are a wider bolt or a narrower quilt.

Worked example 2: four curtain panels, 54 inches vs 44 inches

Now the home-decor example: four curtain panels for a wide window, each panel 54 inches wide with an 84-inch drop. From 54-inch decorator fabric the width fits exactly — one panel per width, because the panel is the width of the bolt, minus the selvage, which is why the panel is cut at 54 inches and the usable width is 53: the seam allowance absorbs the difference. The cut length per panel is the 84-inch drop plus 4 inches for the header and 8 inches for the hem, or 96 inches. Four panels at 96 inches each is 384 inches, which is 10.67 yards, and with the 10 percent allowance that is 11.73 yards, so buy 12 yards.

From 44-inch fabric the same four panels need a width and a half each. A 54-inch panel cannot fit across a 44-inch bolt, so each panel is pieced from a full width plus a half width, and the yardage is 4 panels times 1.5 widths times 96 inches, or 576 inches, which is 16 yards, plus allowance, so buy 18 yards. The difference is 6 yards — half as much again — and at $25 a yard for decorator fabric that is $150 of extra fabric, plus four extra seams down the middle of each panel. The 54-inch bolt is not a luxury here; it is the difference between a clean panel and a pieced one, and between 12 yards and 18.

The same logic applies to the header style. Grommet and tab top need about 4 inches above the rod, rod pocket needs about 3 inches plus the pocket, and pinch pleat needs about 4 inches plus the buckram — but none of those change the width calculation, only the cut length. The style comparison, with fullness, stack and cost for each header, is in Grommet vs Rod Pocket vs Pleated: How Style Changes Size.

Worked example 3: a dress and a tablecloth

Two smaller examples show the width working at the other end of the scale. An adult A-line dress from 60-inch apparel fabric needs 3 yards, as calculated above: two lengths at 48 inches, or 96 inches, plus allowance. The same dress from 44-inch fabric needs three lengths, because the front and back pieces, 30 inches wide each at the hem, cannot fit side by side across a 44-inch bolt at all — 30 plus 30 is 60 inches, and the bolt is 44. Three lengths at 48 inches is 144 inches, or 4 yards, plus allowance, so buy 4.5 yards. The wider bolt saves a yard and a half, or about $22 at $15 a yard, and it saves a seam: the dress from 60-inch fabric is cut in two pieces, while the dress from 44-inch fabric needs the pieces cut separately with more waste between them.

A tablecloth for a 60 by 84-inch table finishes 84 by 108 inches, and the width decides whether it is seamed. From 54-inch fabric it is two widths seamed down the middle: 2 widths at 110 inches each is 220 inches, or 6.1 yards, plus allowance, so buy 7 yards. From 108-inch fabric it is a single width: one width at 110 inches is 110 inches, or 3.06 yards, plus allowance, so buy 3.5 yards — half the yardage and no centre seam at all. From 44-inch fabric it is two widths as well, but the seam falls in a different place and the yardage is the same 7 yards, because the width count is the same. The 108-inch bolt is the only one that changes the answer, and it changes it by removing the seam entirely.

Width changes the cost twice: once through the yardage, and once through the price per yard. The table below prices the three worked examples at mid-range prices for each width, so the combined effect is visible.

The table shows that the cheapest yardage is not always the cheapest project. The four curtain panels are $300 from 54-inch decorator fabric but $216 from 44-inch cotton — the narrower fabric wins on price, because decorator fabric costs twice as much per yard. But the 44-inch panels are pieced down the middle, and the $84 saving buys four centre seams and a flatter drape. The rule is to price the whole project — yards times price per yard — at each width, and to weigh the seams and the drape alongside the dollars. A pieced curtain panel is not a failed panel, but it is a visible one, and the saving has to be worth the seam.

How to measure a bolt yourself

Measuring a bolt takes ten seconds and removes the one guess from the calculation. Unroll about a yard, lay it flat, and measure selvage to selvage with a tape measure, in two places — near the cut end and a yard further along — because the width can vary along the bolt. Subtract an inch for the selvages to get the usable width, and use the usable width in the formula, not the printed width. If the two measurements differ by more than half an inch, use the smaller one: the narrowest point is the one that decides whether a piece fits.

For online fabric, the product page lists the nominal width, and the bolt that arrives can be an inch narrower. The fix is to order a swatch first, or to buy a quarter yard extra as insurance on a project where the width is marginal — a 90-inch quilt top from a bolt that arrives at 43 inches instead of 44 still needs 3 widths, so the yardage does not change, but a 54-inch curtain panel from a bolt that arrives at 52 inches does not fit at all, and the whole calculation changes. The quarter yard of insurance costs $3 to $10, and a project that stalls for want of it costs the whole job. When the width is marginal, buy the extra quarter yard, and buy it from the same bolt.

Frequently asked questions

What is the standard fabric width?

There are four standard widths: 36 inches for craft cotton and muslin, 44 to 45 inches for quilting cotton, 54 inches for home-decor and upholstery fabric, and 58 to 60 inches for apparel fabric. A fifth width, 108 inches, is used for quilt backing. The width that matters is the width of the bolt you will actually buy — measure it selvage to selvage, and subtract about an inch for the selvages to get the usable width.

How does fabric width change the yardage I need?

Width decides how many widths of fabric your project needs: widths needed equals the project width divided by the fabric width, rounded up. A 90-inch quilt top needs 3 widths of 44-inch fabric but only 2 widths of 54-inch fabric, so the same top needs 10 yards from the narrow bolt and 7 yards from the wide one, including the 10 percent allowance. The length never changes — only the width count does. The free Fabric Yardage Calculator runs this width-first formula.

Is 44-inch or 60-inch fabric better for a quilt?

For the quilt top, 44-inch quilting cotton is the standard — patterns assume it, the print range is widest, and a queen top needs 10 yards. Sixty-inch fabric would need only 7 yards for the same top, but quilting prints are rarely sold on 60-inch bolts, so the choice is usually made by the print, not the width. For the quilt back, 108-inch backing fabric is the clear winner: 3.5 yards against 10 yards from 44-inch fabric, a saving of about $66 at $12 a yard.

Can I make curtains from 44-inch fabric?

Yes, but each 54-inch-wide panel needs a width and a half, so four panels with an 84-inch drop need about 18 yards instead of 12, and each panel has a seam down the middle. From 54-inch decorator fabric the same four panels need 12 yards with no piecing, and the fullness is right — 4 panels give 216 inches against a 100-inch rod, or 2.16 times. The 54-inch bolt costs more per yard, so price the whole project both ways before choosing.

How do I work out the price per square yard?

Divide the price per yard by the width in inches and multiply by 36. A $6 yard of 36-inch fabric is $6 per square yard; a $12 yard of 60-inch fabric is $7.20 per square yard; a $15 yard of 54-inch decorator fabric is $10 per square yard. Comparing prices per yard across different widths is meaningless, because a yard of 60-inch fabric contains roughly 67 percent more cloth than a yard of 36-inch fabric.

Why does my 60-inch fabric measure 56 inches?

Apparel fabric widths vary by mill, dye lot and how tightly the fabric was wound — a bolt labelled 58 to 60 inches can measure anywhere from 56 to 60 inches. Quilting cotton is the most consistent width, and decorator fabric is usually a true 54 inches. Always measure the bolt you will actually buy, selvage to selvage, in two places, and use the smaller measurement in the formula. If a pattern was laid out for 60 inches and the bolt is 56, the pieces will not fit.
