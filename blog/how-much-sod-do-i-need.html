How Much Sod Do I Need? Measuring Any Yard in 10 Minutes

Wondering how much sod do I need for my yard? Take your total lawn area in square feet, subtract everything sod won't cover (house, driveway, patios, beds), add 5–10% for cuts and waste, then divide by roll or pallet coverage. A yard of 2,500 square feet of plantable lawn needs about 2,650 square feet of sod — roughly 265 standard rolls or 6 pallets. This guide shows the 10-minute measuring method that works on rectangles, L-shapes and curved beds alike, with worked numbers for three real yards and 2026 sod pricing so you can order once, correctly.

The short answer: the sod formula

Every sod order boils down to one calculation:

Sod to order (sq ft) = (lawn area − areas sod won't cover) × 1.05 to 1.10

The first half is measurement — walking the yard with a tape and turning shapes into square feet. The second half is conversion: sod is sold by the roll (usually 10 sq ft each) or by the pallet (usually 500 sq ft), so you divide your square footage by those coverages and round up. Sections below give you the exact measuring method, the roll/pallet math, and ready-made totals for common yard sizes.

The 10-minute measuring method

You need three things: a tape measure (a 25-footer is fine), a sheet of paper, and a rough sketch of the yard. Draw the property outline, mark off the lawn areas, then break them into simple shapes:

  • Rectangles: length × width in feet. This covers 90% of most yards once you split them up.
  • Right triangles: (base × height) ÷ 2. Handy for corner wedges where the lot line runs diagonal.
  • Circles and curves: for a round bed, radius × radius × 3.14. For gentle curves, measure as a rectangle to the curve's widest point — the extra you "overmeasure" roughly equals what curved cutting will waste.
  • L-shapes: split into two rectangles, measure each, add them up.

Pace-and-estimate works for a rough quote, but sod farms charge by the pallet — a 10% measuring error on a 5,000 sq ft yard is a whole extra pallet or a shortage that stops the job. Walking it with a tape is the cheapest part of the project.

Subtract what sod won't cover

The measuring mistake almost everyone makes is measuring the lot and calling it the lawn. Walk the sketch and subtract, in square feet:

  • The house footprint (including garage)
  • Driveways and walkways
  • Patios, decks and pool surrounds
  • Flower beds, tree rings and mulched areas
  • Strips narrower than about 2 feet along fences or curbs — sod can't bend into them, and they dry out first; plan them as gravel or plants instead

Rule of thumb: measure each subtraction as the rectangle that fully contains it. Being slightly generous here is good, because the shapes you're removing usually have their own waste factor when you cut sod around them.

Worked example: a real suburban backyard

A backyard is 45 feet wide and 60 feet deep: 45 × 60 = 2,700 sq ft. Now the subtractions:

  • Garage overhang and side setback intrude 3 ft along one 60-ft side: 3 × 60 = 180 sq ft
  • Patio: 12 × 14 = 168 sq ft
  • Shed: 8 × 10 = 80 sq ft
  • Curved flower bed along the back fence, measured as a 20 × 6 rectangle: 120 sq ft

Plantable lawn = 2,700 − 180 − 168 − 80 − 120 = 2,152 sq ft. The yard has curves and a few obstacles, so waste at 7%: 2,152 × 1.07 = 2,303 sq ft to order.

Now convert. At the standard 10 sq ft per roll: 2,303 ÷ 10 = 230.3 → 231 rolls. At 500 sq ft per pallet: 2,303 ÷ 500 = 4.6 → 5 pallets (pallets must be ordered whole, so you land slightly over — fine, since sod is sold pallet-sized by most farms).

Rolls and pallets: what you're actually buying

Sod sizing is not universal, so confirm with your supplier — but these are the US standards:

  • Standard roll: 2 ft × 5 ft = 10 sq ft per roll. This is the small-roll cut most home centers and sod farms sell to homeowners.
  • Slab roll: 2 ft × 8 ft (or 18 in × 8 ft) = 16 sq ft. Common with professional installers.
  • Big roll: 42 in × 105+ ft off a roll, installed by machine. Only practical for huge, open areas.
  • Pallet: usually 50 standard rolls = 500 sq ft, though some farms pack 450 sq ft pallets — always ask. Pallets weigh 1,500–3,000 lb, so delivery is a flatbed or forklift affair, not a pickup trunk load.

The waste percentage depends on shape complexity. A clean rectangle at 5% is honest. Anything with curves, trees, bed edges or diagonal property lines deserves 10% — cut-off triangles from curved edges can't be reused. The 7% in the example above is the middle ground for a yard with one curve and a few obstacles.

Sod cost per square foot in 2026

Material prices vary by grass type and distance from the farm:

  • Bermuda / zoysia (warm season): $0.35–$0.55/sq ft delivered
  • Kentucky bluegrass / tall fescue (cool season): $0.40–$0.65/sq ft delivered
  • St. Augustine: $0.45–$0.70/sq ft delivered (heavier rolls, often sold by the pallet only)

Delivery fees run $50–$150 per load inside metro areas. Professional installation — grading, the sod itself, and laying — lands around $1.00–$2.00/sq ft all-in for a straightforward yard. So the 2,500 sq ft example costs about $1,200 in sod plus $1,500–$3,000 if you hire the whole job out. DIY saves the labor half; the prep half (grading, a 2–3 inch topsoil layer, starter fertilizer) is where most DIY budgets get ambushed, so budget 20% of the sod cost for soil amendments and rental equipment.

Mistakes that waste sod (and money)

Measuring the lot, not the lawn. A 5,000 sq ft lot is often 3,000 sq ft of lawn. Ordering for the lot buys you two pallets of grass you'll never lay.

Forgetting the waste factor. Cutting sod around beds and curves leaves triangular scraps. Skip the 5–10% and you'll run out one row short — with a delivery fee on the small make-up order.

Assuming every pallet is 500 sq ft. Some farms pack 450. If you divide by 500 but receive 450, a 10-pallet order comes up 500 sq ft short. Confirm the number with your supplier before you divide.

Measuring once, ordering immediately. Soil prep eats a week; sod is perishable. Measure now, but order for delivery the day before (or the morning of) install day. A pallet baking in the sun for three days can be half-dead before it touches soil.

Ignoring strips under 2 feet wide. That 18-inch strip along the driveway will brown out within a season and can't be cut cleanly from rolls. Redesign it before you buy grass for it.

Frequently asked questions

How much sod do I need for 1,000 square feet of lawn?

Order about 1,050–1,100 square feet of sod — 105–110 standard rolls, or 3 pallets (most farms' pallets are 500 sq ft, so you will take a partial third pallet or order by the roll). Budget around $480 for sod only at 2026 prices.

How many square feet are in a pallet of sod?

Usually 500 square feet (50 standard 2x5 ft rolls), though some farms pack 450 square feet per pallet. Always confirm the pallet size with your supplier before converting your square footage into pallets.

How much sod waste should I add to my order?

Add 5% for a simple rectangular yard and 10% for yards with curves, trees, beds or diagonal edges. The waste covers triangular cut-offs around curves and beds that cannot be reused.

How much does sod cost per square foot in 2026?

Sod alone runs about $0.35–$0.70 per square foot delivered, depending on grass type. Professionally installed, expect $1.00–$2.00 per square foot all-in. DIY saves the labor portion but budget about 20% extra for soil prep and amendments.

Can I lay sod on top of an old lawn?

Not directly — sod needs soil contact to root. Mow the old lawn as short as possible, till or aerate the top few inches, add 2–3 inches of topsoil, grade, and lay the sod on the prepared surface. Skipping prep is the main reason new sod fails.

How long can sod sit on the pallet before laying?

Lay it the same day, within 24 hours at most. Sod starts heating and yellowing fast in the roll. Order for delivery on install day, and keep the rolls shaded and lightly misted if installation gets delayed.
