How Much Fabric Do I Need? A Yardage Guide by Project

How much fabric do I need is the question that decides whether a sewing project ends with a finished quilt on the bed or a half-finished top and a fabric store that no longer stocks your print. Buy too little and the project stalls — a discontinued print cannot be reordered, and a dye lot that does not match shows in daylight. Buy too much and you have paid for fabric that sits in a drawer, which matters when decorator fabric is $15 to $40 a yard. The answer is not a single number, because the same project can need 2 yards from a 60-inch bolt or 4 yards from a 36-inch bolt. Yardage is driven by the width of the fabric first and the length of the project second, and this guide works through the formula in that order — fit the width, then multiply the length — with worked numbers for a queen quilt, four curtain panels, a simple dress, cushion covers and a tablecloth, so you can buy the right amount the first time.

The short answer: fit the width first, then multiply the length

Every yardage calculation follows the same four steps, and the order matters. First, work out how many widths of fabric your project needs: divide the project width by the fabric width and round up to a whole number. A 90-inch-wide quilt top from 44-inch quilting cotton needs 3 widths, because 2 widths give only 88 inches. Second, work out the cut length of one piece: the finished length plus hem and seam allowances, rounded up to a whole number of pattern repeats if the fabric is patterned. Third, multiply: widths needed times pieces times cut length gives the total inches of fabric. Fourth, divide by 36 to get yards, add a 10 percent allowance for shrinkage and cutting mistakes, and round up to the next quarter or half yard — fabric is sold by the yard, and a quarter yard short is the same as a yard short when the bolt runs out.

That width-first order is the whole secret, and it is the order the free Fabric Yardage Calculator uses: project width and fabric width in, widths needed out, then length. Most people do it backwards — they measure the length of the project, guess a yardage, and discover at the cutting table that the piece does not fit across the fabric. A curtain panel 84 inches long needs about 2.7 yards of length, but if the panel is wider than the bolt, the same panel needs two widths, and the yardage doubles to 5.4 yards before the hem is even added. Width is what trips people up; length is the easy part.

The allowance is not optional padding. Cotton shrinks 3 to 5 percent on the first wash, linen shrinks more, and a miscut piece, a seam ripped out and re-sewn, or a pattern piece laid the wrong way all consume fabric that the raw calculation does not include. Ten percent covers normal cases; for bold patterns on big projects, where every piece must line up, 15 to 20 percent is safer. The cost of the allowance is small — a half yard of quilting cotton is $4 to $8 — and the cost of being short is the whole project.

The formula, step by step

Step one is the widths. Widths needed equals the project width divided by the fabric width, rounded up. The fabric width is the width of the bolt you will actually buy, not the width printed on a pattern envelope, and the usable width is about an inch less than the bolt width, because the selvage edges — roughly half an inch on each side — are usually trimmed or kept out of the visible piece. If your project width is within an inch of the fabric width, treat it as not fitting and plan two widths. A 54-inch decorator fabric gives about 53 usable inches; a 53-inch-wide panel fits, a 54-inch panel does not, and the difference is a whole extra width of fabric.

Step two is the cut length. Cut length equals the finished length plus hem and seam allowances, and then rounded up to a whole number of pattern repeats. A curtain panel with an 84-inch drop needs about 4 inches for the header and 8 inches for the hem, so the cut length is 96 inches. A cushion cover 18 inches square needs two 20-inch squares, with a 1-inch seam allowance on each side. If the fabric has a pattern repeat — the distance after which the print repeats, printed on the bolt end or the product page — each cut length is rounded up to a whole number of repeats so the pattern lines up across pieces. A 90-inch cut with a 27-inch repeat becomes 108 inches, four repeats, and the extra 18 inches is the price of a matched pattern. Plain fabric has a repeat of zero, and the cut length stays as measured.

Step three is the multiplication. Total inches equals widths needed times number of pieces times cut length. Four curtain panels at one width each and 96 inches per cut is 4 times 96, or 384 inches. A queen quilt top at 3 widths and a 108-inch cut is 3 times 108, or 324 inches. Step four is the conversion: divide by 36 to get yards, add 10 percent, and round up. The four curtain panels are 384 divided by 36, or 10.67 yards, plus 10 percent is 11.73 yards, so buy 12 yards. The quilt top is 324 divided by 36, or 9 yards exactly, plus 10 percent is 9.9 yards, so buy 10 yards for the top alone.

One more rule belongs in the formula: directional prints and nap fabrics such as velvet must all be cut facing the same way. You cannot flip a piece upside down to save space, the way you can with a plain solid, so the yardage for a directional print is always the full calculation with no nesting tricks. Stripes that must line up across a seam have the same constraint, and it is the pattern repeat rounding that accounts for it.

Fabric widths: why the bolt width changes everything

Fabric is sold by the yard, but it is sold off bolts of different widths, and the width is what decides the yardage. The common widths are 36 inches for craft cottons and some narrow cottons, 44 to 45 inches for quilting cottons, 54 inches for home-decor fabrics, 58 to 60 inches for apparel fabrics, and 108 inches for quilt backing fabric. The same 90-inch-wide quilt top needs 3 widths of 44-inch fabric, 2 widths of 54-inch fabric, and 1 width of 108-inch backing fabric — and the yardage follows: 9 yards, 6 yards, or 3 yards of length for the same finished top, before the repeat and allowance are added.

The practical consequence is that you should choose the fabric width before you calculate the yardage, not after. If you are making curtains, 54-inch decorator fabric gives one panel per width, and the yardage is simply the number of panels times the cut length. If you are making a quilt, 44-inch cotton means three widths for a queen top, and the yardage is three times the cut length. And if you are choosing between two prints, one on a 44-inch bolt and one on a 60-inch bolt, the wider bolt can save a third of the yardage on a wide project — which, at $12 a yard, is real money on a 10-yard project. Always ask the width of the bolt you will actually buy, because the same print is sometimes sold on different widths by different stores.

Three patterns fall out of the table. Third, the big projects — queen quilts and slipcovers — are 14 yards and up, and on those the pattern repeat and the allowance are worth calculating properly, because a 10 percent error is a yard and a half of fabric.

Worked example 1: a queen quilt, top to binding

Take a queen quilt, 90 inches wide by 108 inches long, made from 44-inch quilting cotton for the top, 108-inch backing fabric for the back, and a 2.5-inch binding. The top is the width problem: 90 divided by 44 is 2.05 widths, so 3 widths are needed, because 2 widths give only 88 inches. Each width is cut 108 inches long, plus a small seam allowance that is already inside the piecing, so the top is 3 times 108, or 324 inches, which is 9 yards. With the 10 percent allowance that is 9.9 yards, so buy 10 yards of the top fabric — and buy it all from the same bolt, because two dye lots of the same print can differ enough to show in daylight.

The backing is the width solution: 108-inch backing fabric is wide enough that one width covers the whole 90-inch quilt back, with about 9 inches to spare on each side for the quilting overhang. The cut length is the quilt length plus 4 inches of overhang on each end, or 116 inches, which is 3.2 yards — buy 3.5 yards. If 108-inch backing is not available in the print you want, the backing from 44-inch fabric is 3 widths times 108 inches, or 9 yards, which is why wide backing fabric exists and why it is worth finding.

The binding is the perimeter: 2 times 90 plus 108 is 396 inches of binding. Binding strips are cut 2.5 inches wide across the 44-inch fabric, so one strip gives 44 inches of binding, and 396 divided by 44 is 9 strips. Nine strips at 2.5 inches each is 22.5 inches of fabric, or 0.63 yards — buy 0.75 yards. The total is 10 yards for the top, 3.5 yards for the backing, and 0.75 yards for the binding, or 14.25 yards, and with the allowance folded in, buy about 14.5 yards across the three fabrics. The same quilt from 44-inch fabric for the backing as well would be 10 plus 9.9 plus 0.75, or about 20.5 yards — the 108-inch backing saves roughly 6 yards, or about $72 at $12 a yard.

Worked example 2: four curtain panels, 84-inch drop

Now the most common home-decor project: four curtain panels for a wide window, each panel 54 inches wide with an 84-inch drop, made from 54-inch decorator fabric. The width fits exactly: one panel per width, because the panel is the width of the bolt. The cut length per panel is the 84-inch drop plus 4 inches for the header and 8 inches for the hem, or 96 inches. Four panels at 96 inches each is 384 inches, which is 10.67 yards, and with the 10 percent allowance that is 11.73 yards, so buy 12 yards.

The panel count itself comes from the curtain math: at 2 times fullness, a 100-inch rod needs 200 inches of fabric, and 4 panels of 54 inches give 216 inches, which is 2.16 times — the right fullness. If the same four panels were made from 44-inch fabric, each panel would need a width and a half, and the yardage would jump to about 16 yards, which is why decorator fabric is sold on 54-inch bolts. The header style changes the cut length, not the panel count: grommet and tab top need about 4 inches above the rod, rod pocket needs about 3 inches plus the pocket, and pinch pleat needs about 4 inches plus the buckram. The style comparison — fullness, stack and cost for each header — is in Grommet vs Rod Pocket vs Pleated: How Style Changes Size.

If the fabric is patterned with a 27-inch repeat, the cut length changes: 96 inches rounded up to a whole number of 27-inch repeats is 4 repeats, or 108 inches per panel, which adds 12 inches per panel and 48 inches — 1.33 yards — across the four panels. With the allowance, the buy becomes 13.5 yards instead of 12. That is the pattern-repeat section below, worked out in full, and it is the number most curtain makers forget until the second panel does not match the first.

Worked example 3: a simple A-line dress and four cushion covers

The small projects follow the same formula, and they show how the width decides the answer. An adult A-line dress, knee length, from 60-inch apparel fabric: the finished length is about 45 inches, plus 3 inches for the hem and seams, so the cut length is 48 inches. The front and back pieces are each about 30 inches wide at the hem, so together they are 60 inches — exactly the bolt width, and once the selvage is excluded they do not fit side by side, so the dress needs two lengths. Two lengths at 48 inches is 96 inches, or 2.67 yards, plus 10 percent is 2.93 yards, so buy 3 yards. The same dress from 44-inch fabric would need three lengths, or about 4.5 yards, because the pieces cannot fit across the narrower bolt at all.

Four cushion covers, 18 inches square, from 44-inch quilting cotton: each cover needs two 20-inch squares, one for the front and one for the back. Two squares side by side are 40 inches across, which fits inside the 44-inch bolt, so one 20-inch cut length gives one whole cover. Four covers are 4 times 20 inches, or 80 inches, which is 2.22 yards, plus 10 percent is 2.44 yards — buy 2.5 yards, or 3 yards if the fabric is patterned and the print must be centred on each cover. A single cover is the same calculation with one cut: 20 inches is 0.56 yards, plus the allowance is 0.61, so buy 0.75 yards. The lesson is that cushion covers are the cheapest project to over-buy on, and the most forgiving: a quarter yard extra costs $2 to $4, and a centred print is worth it.

Pattern repeat: the yardage most people forget

A pattern repeat is the distance after which the print starts over — a large floral might repeat every 27 inches, a stripe every 9 inches, a geometric every 18 inches — and it matters because every piece of a project must start at the same point in the repeat, or the pattern will not line up across seams. The rule is simple: each cut length is rounded up to a whole number of repeats. A 90-inch curtain cut with a 27-inch repeat becomes 4 repeats, or 108 inches. A 48-inch dress cut with an 18-inch repeat becomes 3 repeats, or 54 inches. A 20-inch cushion cut with a 12-inch repeat becomes 2 repeats, or 24 inches.

The cost of the rounding grows with the number of pieces. Four curtain panels at a 96-inch cut and a 27-inch repeat each become 108 inches, which adds 12 inches per panel and 48 inches in total — 1.33 yards, or about $33 of decorator fabric at $25 a yard. A queen quilt top with a 24-inch repeat and a 108-inch cut is already 4.5 repeats, so it rounds up to 5 repeats, or 120 inches per width, adding 12 inches per width and 36 inches — a full yard — across the three widths. A small repeat is cheap: a 9-inch stripe on a 96-inch cut rounds up to 99 inches, adding 3 inches per panel. A large repeat is expensive, and it is the reason bold large-scale prints cost more in yardage than small prints of the same fabric.

Two practical rules follow. First, always find the repeat before you buy — it is printed on the bolt end or the product page, and if it is not listed, ask, because guessing it wrong by 6 inches changes the yardage by a yard on a four-panel project. Second, for bold patterns on big projects, raise the allowance from 10 percent to 15 or 20 percent: the repeat rounding and the centring of the motif on each piece both consume fabric, and a half yard of over-buying is a pleasant spare for test seams and future repairs, while a half yard short on a discontinued print ends the project.

Shrinkage and pre-washing: measure after the wash, not before

Cotton shrinks 3 to 5 percent on the first wash, linen shrinks more, and rayon can shrink more still — and the shrinkage happens in both directions, so a 108-inch quilt top can lose 3 to 5 inches of length and width. The 10 percent buying allowance covers one wash for most cottons, but it does not cover cutting the fabric first and washing the finished project later: if the pieces shrink after they are sewn, the seams pull and the finished size comes up short. The fix is to pre-wash the fabric the way the finished project will be washed, dry it the same way, and then measure and cut. Pre-washing also removes the sizing and softens the fabric, which makes it easier to cut accurately.

Pre-washing changes the yardage calculation in one way: measure the cut length after the wash, not before. A 96-inch curtain cut that shrinks 4 percent becomes 92 inches after washing, so if you cut 96 inches before washing, the finished panel is 4 inches short. Buy the yardage, wash it, press it, and then measure the pieces — the allowance you bought covers the shrinkage, and the cutting is done on the fabric at its final size. For fabrics that cannot be washed, such as silk and some decorator fabrics, skip the pre-wash and rely on the allowance instead, and dry-clean the finished project.

What it costs: yardage times price per yard

Fabric is priced by the yard, and the price range is wide enough that the same project can cost three times as much in one fabric as in another. USA fabric stores price quilting cotton around $8 to $15 per yard, decorator fabric $15 to $40 per yard, and apparel fabrics anywhere in between, roughly $10 to $25 per yard. Frequently asked questions

How much fabric do I need for a queen quilt?

About 14.5 yards in total: 10 yards of 44-inch quilting cotton for the 90 by 108-inch top (3 widths at 108 inches each, plus the 10 percent allowance), 3.5 yards of 108-inch backing fabric (one width at 116 inches including overhang), and 0.75 yards for the binding (9 strips of 2.5 inches to cover the 396-inch perimeter). If 108-inch backing is not available, the backing from 44-inch fabric is 9 yards, and the total becomes about 20.5 yards.

How much fabric do I need for curtains?

Four panels with an 84-inch drop from 54-inch decorator fabric need 12 yards: each panel is cut 96 inches (84 inches plus 4 inches for the header and 8 inches for the hem), and 4 panels at 96 inches is 384 inches, or 10.67 yards, plus 10 percent is 11.73 yards, so buy 12. Two panels need 6 yards. If the fabric has a 27-inch pattern repeat, each cut rounds up to 108 inches and the four panels need 13.5 yards.

How do I calculate yardage from 36-inch fabric versus 60-inch fabric?

Divide the project width by the fabric width and round up to whole widths — that is the number that changes. A 90-inch quilt top needs 3 widths of 36-inch fabric but only 2 widths of 60-inch fabric, so the same top needs 9 yards of length from the narrow bolt and 6 yards from the wide one, before the repeat and allowance. Always calculate from the width of the bolt you will actually buy, and remember the usable width is about an inch less than the bolt width because of the selvage.

Does a pattern repeat really add that much fabric?

Yes, on multi-piece projects. A 27-inch repeat on a 96-inch curtain cut rounds each cut up to 108 inches, adding 12 inches per panel and 48 inches — 1.33 yards — across four panels. A 24-inch repeat on a 108-inch quilt cut rounds each width up to 120 inches, adding a full yard across three widths. Small repeats are cheap — a 9-inch stripe adds 3 inches per cut — and large repeats are expensive, which is why bold large-scale prints cost more in yardage than small prints of the same fabric.

Should I pre-wash fabric before cutting?

Yes, for cotton, linen and rayon — cotton shrinks 3 to 5 percent on the first wash, linen more, and the shrinkage happens in both directions. Wash the fabric the way the finished project will be washed, dry it the same way, press it, and then measure and cut, so the pieces are cut at their final size. The 10 percent buying allowance covers the shrinkage. For fabrics that cannot be washed, such as silk and some decorator fabrics, skip the pre-wash, rely on the allowance, and dry-clean the finished project.

How much extra fabric should I buy?

Ten percent over the calculated yardage for normal projects, and 15 to 20 percent for bold patterns on big projects, where the repeat rounding and the centring of the motif both consume fabric. A half yard of quilting cotton costs $4 to $8, and a half yard short on a discontinued print ends the project — the allowance is the cheapest insurance on the whole job. Buy the extra from the same bolt, because two dye lots of the same print can differ enough to show in daylight.
