Curtain Length Guide: Sill, Apron, Floor or Puddle?

This curtain length guide answers the question that decides whether a curtain looks finished or looks like a mistake: where, exactly, should the hem stop? A curtain that ends in the wrong place is visible from the doorway — too short and it looks like it shrank, too long and it drags on the floor collecting dust, and the difference between the right length and the wrong one is often a single inch. There are four standard lengths — sill, apron, floor and puddle — and each one has a room it belongs in, a measurement rule, and a price. This guide works through all four with the exact drop to measure for each, a length table for 8, 9 and 10 foot ceilings, three worked examples with real windows, and the cost of hemming a panel versus buying the right length in the first place.

The short answer: four lengths, one measurement each

Every curtain length is a drop measured from the rod down to a fixed point, and the four lengths differ only in where that fixed point is. Sill length ends at the windowsill, minus half an inch so the hem clears it. Apron length ends 4 to 6 inches below the sill. Floor length ends at the floor, minus half an inch to one inch so the hem just kisses it. Puddle length ends past the floor, with 2 to 8 inches of extra fabric pooling on it. The measurement is always taken from the same starting point — the top of the rod, or the bottom of the ring if the curtain hangs on rings, which adds about 1.5 inches of drop — and it is always taken at three points across the window, because floors and sills are rarely level and the shortest of the three measurements is the one to use.

The choice between the four is mostly a room choice, not a taste choice. Sill and apron lengths belong where a long curtain would be in the way: kitchens, bathrooms, and any window above a radiator, a counter or a piece of furniture. Floor length is the default for living rooms, bedrooms and dining rooms, and it is the length that makes a room look finished. Puddle length belongs in formal rooms where the curtain is decoration as much as window covering — a formal living room or a master bedroom — and it is the only length that is genuinely impractical anywhere else. Pick the length for the room first, then measure the drop for that length, and never buy a panel in the length that matches the window height instead of the length that matches the wall.

Sill length: short, practical, and only for the right rooms

Sill length is the shortest standard length, and it is measured from the rod to the windowsill, minus half an inch. That half inch matters: a curtain that touches the sill will catch on it every time it moves, and the hem will wear through where it rubs. On a standard window with the rod mounted 6 inches above the frame, the sill drop is usually 36 to 48 inches — a 36-inch-wide, 48-inch-tall window in a room with an 8-foot ceiling has its sill at about 36 inches from the floor, and the rod at 84 inches, so the sill drop is 48 inches minus half an inch. That is far shorter than any standard ready-made panel, which is why sill-length curtains are almost always hemmed or custom-made: the standard lengths are 63, 84, 95 and 108 inches, and cutting a 63-inch panel down to 48 inches wastes 15 inches of fabric and costs $15 to $25 per panel in hemming.

Sill length belongs in exactly three places. First, kitchens, where a long curtain would hang into the sink, the stove or the counter and pick up steam, grease and water. Second, bathrooms, where a long curtain would sit in the damp and go mouldy at the hem. Third, any window with a radiator underneath it, where a floor-length curtain would block the heat and could scorch or, with a very hot radiator, be a fire risk. In those three rooms sill length is not a compromise, it is the correct answer, and a floor-length curtain in a kitchen looks as wrong as a sill-length curtain in a living room.

The cost of getting sill length wrong is small in money and large in appearance. A sill-length curtain in a living room makes the wall below the window look bare, and the eye reads the curtain as too small for the room — the same effect as a rug that is too small for the furniture. The fix is to measure the sill drop properly and accept the hemming cost: a 48-inch sill drop on a $30 panel plus a $20 hem is still cheaper than a custom panel at $150, and it is the right length for the room. If the window is in a kitchen and the sill drop is under 40 inches, look for cafe curtains or a valance instead — a full panel hemmed that short loses its proportions, and a cafe curtain is designed for exactly that height.

Apron length: the in-between that works in casual rooms

Apron length ends 4 to 6 inches below the sill, and it is measured the same way as sill length — rod to sill, plus 4 to 6 inches, minus nothing, because the hem hangs free below the sill and does not rub on anything. The name comes from the apron, the trim board under the sill, and the length is meant to cover the sill and the apron together. On the standard window above, with the sill at 36 inches from the floor and the rod at 84 inches, the apron drop is 52 to 54 inches — still well short of the 63-inch standard panel, so apron length, like sill length, usually means hemming a ready-made panel or ordering custom.

Apron length is the right choice for casual rooms where a sill-length curtain looks too abrupt but a floor-length curtain would be in the way: a kitchen with a window over the sink where the sill is high, a laundry room, a home office with a desk under the window, or a child's bedroom where a long curtain would be pulled and climbed on. It covers the sill, which makes the window look finished, and it stays clear of the counter or the furniture below. It is also the length to use when the window sits above a piece of furniture that cannot move — a radiator cover, a built-in bench, a window seat — because a floor-length curtain behind furniture is invisible anyway and a sill-length curtain leaves the apron exposed.

The risk with apron length is that it can look indecisive in a formal room. A curtain that ends 5 inches below the sill in a living room reads as a curtain that was meant to reach the floor and did not, and guests will assume it shrank. The rule is simple: if the room has no furniture or radiator under the window, do not use apron length. If something sits under the window, apron length is honest and sill length is abrupt, and the 4 to 6 inches of extra fabric is the difference between a curtain that looks deliberate and one that looks cut short. Measure the sill drop first, add 4 to 6 inches, and write the number down — apron length is the easiest length to get wrong by guessing, because the difference between a good apron length and a bad one is two inches.

Floor length: the default, and the measurement that matters most

Floor length is the length most curtains should be, and it is measured from the rod to the floor, minus half an inch to one inch. That gap is the whole secret of floor length: the hem should just kiss the floor, not drag on it and not hover above it. Half an inch is the safe number on a hard floor; one inch is better on carpet, because a thick-pile carpet compresses under the hem and a curtain that measures exactly to the top of the carpet will drag once the carpet is walked on. Measure at three points across the window and use the shortest, because a floor that slopes by an inch — which most floors do — will make the curtain drag on one side if you use the longest measurement.

The rod height decides the floor length before you ever measure the drop. The standard is to mount the rod 4 to 6 inches above the window frame, and on an 8-foot ceiling with a standard window that puts the rod about 84 inches from the floor — which is exactly why 84-inch panels are the best-selling ready-made length in the US. The ready-made length ladder is really a ceiling-height ladder, and the table below maps each standard panel length to the rod height and ceiling it fits.

Three measuring traps catch people on floor length. The first is measuring on the subfloor instead of the finished floor — if the room is being renovated and the flooring is not in yet, add the thickness of the finished floor to the measurement, or the curtain will be an inch short when the floor goes in. The second is measuring without the rings: rings add about 1.5 inches of drop, and a panel that is perfect without rings is 1.5 inches short with them, so measure with the hardware you will actually use. The third is measuring once, in the middle: floors slope, sills are rarely level, and a single measurement in the middle of the window will be wrong on one side. Three measurements, shortest wins, and if the difference between the three is more than an inch, the floor has a real slope and the curtain should be hemmed to the shortest measurement — a curtain that is half an inch short on one side is invisible, and a curtain that drags on one side is obvious.

Floor length is also the length where the "buy long and hem" rule pays off most. If your rod height falls between two standard lengths — say the rod is at 90 inches, between the 84 and 95-inch panels — buy the 95-inch panel and hem it to 90 inches. A 95-inch panel hemmed to 90 looks identical to a 90-inch panel, and the hemming costs $15 to $25. An 84-inch panel on a 90-inch rod is 6 inches short, and it cannot be fixed at any price. Always round length up, never down.

Puddle length: the formal look, and the price of it

Puddle length is floor length plus 2 to 8 inches of extra fabric that pools on the floor, and it is measured the same way as floor length — rod to floor, then add the puddle on top. The amount of puddle is a choice with three named levels: a soft break is 1 to 2 inches past the floor, just enough for the hem to bend slightly where it meets the floor; a medium puddle is 3 to 4 inches, with a visible fold of fabric on the floor; a full puddle is 6 to 8 inches, with a generous pool that spreads out from the hem. The soft break is the safest puddle for most rooms; the full puddle belongs only in a formal living room or a master bedroom where the curtain is never walked past and the floor is cleaned often.

The puddle is bought, not guessed: measure the floor length first, then add the puddle amount to it, and buy the standard length at or above that total. A rod at 84 inches with a 4-inch medium puddle needs an 88-inch drop, so the 95-inch panel is the buy, hemmed to 88 inches. A rod at 84 inches with a full 8-inch puddle needs a 92-inch drop, and the same 95-inch panel covers it with 3 inches to spare. The mistake is to buy the 84-inch panel and hope it puddles — it will not, because 84 inches is the floor length and there is nothing extra to pool. A puddle that is too small looks like a mistake; a puddle that is too large looks like a laundry pile.

Puddle length has real costs that floor length does not. The hem sits on the floor, so it collects dust, pet hair and dirt, and it needs vacuuming or shaking out far more often than a floor-length hem. It is a trip hazard in any room children walk through, and it is a problem for anyone with a vacuum cleaner or a robot vacuum, which will catch the hem. It also uses more fabric: a full puddle on a 96-inch sliding door needs 8 extra inches on each of 4 panels, which is 32 inches of fabric, or roughly half a panel more than floor length. That is why puddle length is a formal-room length — in a formal room the curtain is decoration, the traffic is light, and the extra fabric is the point. In a kitchen, a child's bedroom or a hallway, a puddle is a hazard and a cleaning chore, and floor length is the right answer.

Worked example 1: a kitchen window, sill length

Take the kitchen window over the sink: 36 inches wide, 36 inches tall, with the sill at 42 inches from the floor and the rod mounted 6 inches above the frame at 84 inches from the floor. The window is too short for a floor-length curtain — the sink and the counter sit directly below it — so the length is sill. The sill drop is the rod height minus the sill height: 84 minus 42 is 42 inches, minus half an inch for clearance, so the finished drop is 41½ inches. No standard panel comes in 41½ inches, so the buy is a 63-inch panel hemmed down to 41½ inches, which wastes 21½ inches of fabric and costs $15 to $25 per panel in hemming — or a cafe curtain, which is made for exactly this height and costs $20 to $40 for the pair with no hemming at all.

The width math is the same as any window: the rod is 48 inches wide (36 plus 6 on each side), 2 times fullness is 96 inches of fabric, and 2 standard 54-inch panels give 108 inches, which is 2.25 times — the right fullness for a kitchen, where the curtain is mostly open and the folds matter less. The shopping list is 2 panels, 54 inches wide, hemmed to 41½ inches, or a cafe pair if the window is over the sink and the top half of the window is left bare for light. Cost it out: 2 budget polyester panels at $25 to $40 for the pair, plus $30 to $50 in hemming for both, is $55 to $90 all in — and the cafe alternative is $20 to $40 with no hemming, which is why cafe curtains win on short kitchen windows.

One detail that catches people on kitchen windows: the curtain must clear the faucet. A sill-length curtain that hangs to 41½ inches on a window with a tall faucet will brush the faucet every time it is opened, and the hem will get wet. Measure the faucet height before you measure the curtain, and if the faucet rises above the sill, use apron length or a cafe curtain that ends above the faucet. The curtain is there to dress the window, not to mop the sink.

Worked example 2: a living room window, floor length

Now the living room picture window: 60 inches wide, 60 inches tall, 8-foot ceiling, hardwood floor, and this is the room where the curtains are part of the furniture, so the length has to be right. The rod is mounted 6 inches above the frame and 6 inches past it on each side, so the rod is 72 inches wide and sits 84 inches from the floor. The length is floor: the drop is measured at three points — 84 inches on the left, 83¾ inches in the middle, 84¼ inches on the right — and the shortest is 83¾ inches, minus half an inch, so the finished drop is 83¼ inches. The buy is the 84-inch standard panel, which hangs with its hem half an inch off the floor at the shortest point and three-quarters of an inch off at the longest — invisible from the sofa.

The width math: 72-inch rod times 2 times fullness is 144 inches of fabric, and 3 standard 54-inch panels give 162 inches, which is 2.25 times — the right look for a living room. The shopping list is 3 panels, 54 by 84 inches, in a mid-range linen at $45 each, which is $135, plus a 72 to 96-inch rod at about $35 and rings and finials at $20 — about $190 all in, with no hemming, because the standard length matches the rod height exactly. That is the advantage of floor length on an 8-foot ceiling: the ready-made ladder was built for exactly this room, and the 84-inch panel is the best-selling length in the US for exactly this reason.

If the same window were in a 9-foot-ceiling room, the rod would sit at 95 inches and the buy would be the 95-inch panel, and the cost would be the same — the length ladder scales with the ceiling, and the price of a 95-inch panel is usually within a few dollars of the 84-inch one. The expensive version of this window is the one where the rod is mounted at 90 inches, between the two standard lengths, and the buy is a 95-inch panel hemmed to 90 inches — the hemming adds $15 to $25 per panel, or $45 to $75 for three panels, and it is still the right buy, because an 84-inch panel would be 6 inches short and no amount of money fixes a curtain that is too short.

Worked example 3: a formal bedroom, full puddle

Finally, the formal master bedroom: a 72-inch window, 60 inches tall, 9-foot ceiling, carpeted floor, and the curtain is meant to be the room's main decoration, so the length is a full puddle. The rod is mounted 6 inches above the frame at 95 inches from the floor, and the floor drop is measured at three points — 95, 94¾ and 95¼ inches — with the shortest at 94¾ inches. The puddle is 6 inches, so the finished drop is 94¾ plus 6, which is 100¾ inches. The buy is the 108-inch standard panel, hemmed to 100¾ inches, which wastes 7¼ inches and costs $15 to $25 per panel in hemming.

The width math: the rod is 84 inches wide (72 plus 6 on each side), 2 times fullness is 168 inches of fabric, and 4 standard 54-inch panels give 216 inches, which is 2.57 times — fuller than the standard, and right for a puddle, because the extra fabric in the width makes the puddle on the floor look generous rather than thin. The shopping list is 4 panels, 54 by 108 inches, in a premium velvet at $90 to $100 per panel, which is $360 to $400, plus hemming at $60 to $100 for four panels, plus the rod and hardware at $70 to $150 — about $500 to $650 all in. That is the most expensive curtain in this guide, and it should be: a full puddle in velvet is a luxury choice, and it is priced like one.

The care cost is part of the price. The hem sits on the carpet, so it collects dust and pet hair, and it needs vacuuming with a brush attachment once a week and shaking out once a month. The carpet under the puddle will also wear differently from the carpet around it, because the fabric traps moisture and blocks light, so the puddle should be lifted and the carpet aired every few months. If that maintenance sounds like too much, the soft break — 1 to 2 inches past the floor — gives most of the formal look for a fraction of the care, and it is the puddle most people should actually buy.

The six length mistakes that make curtains look wrong

The first two mistakes are room mistakes, and they are the ones that show up in almost every "why do my curtains look wrong" photo: the length was chosen for the window instead of the room. The next four are measurement mistakes, and they are all avoidable with the same discipline — measure the rod, not the window; measure at three points; measure with the hardware on; and measure the finished floor, not the subfloor. The hemming rule covers the rest: always round length up, never down, because a panel that is too long can be hemmed and a panel that is too short is a loss.

How to measure the drop, step by step

Do the measuring in this order, with a steel tape, a pencil and a notepad — not a cloth tape, which stretches, and not from memory. First, mount the rod, or decide its height: 4 to 6 inches above the frame is the standard, and the rod height is the starting point for every length. Second, decide the length for the room — sill, apron, floor or puddle — using the table above, not the panel lengths in the shop. Third, measure the drop from the rod to the ending point — the sill, the sill plus 4 to 6 inches, the floor, or the floor plus the puddle — at three points across the window: left, middle and right. Fourth, take the shortest of the three, and subtract half an inch for sill and floor length, or add the puddle for puddle length. Fifth, match the finished drop to the nearest standard length at or above it: 63, 84, 95 or 108 inches. Sixth, write all six numbers down before you shop, and take the notepad with you — the aisle is where the numbers get forgotten.

Two final traps deserve a note. The first is measuring the drop from the window frame instead of the rod: the rod sits 4 to 6 inches above the frame, and a drop measured from the frame is 4 to 6 inches short, which is exactly the difference between a floor-length curtain and a curtain that hovers above the floor. The second is buying the length that matches the window height instead of the wall height: a 60-inch window does not take a 60-inch curtain, it takes the curtain that reaches from the rod to the floor, which on an 8-foot ceiling is 84 inches. The window height is irrelevant to the length; the rod height and the floor are everything. The width and panel-count math for the same window is covered in What Size Curtains Do I Need?, and the two guides together give the full size: width from the rod and the fullness, length from the rod and the floor.

Frequently asked questions

How long should curtains be — should they touch the floor?

Floor-length curtains should end half an inch to one inch above the floor, so the hem just kisses the floor without dragging or collecting dust. A puddle length deliberately adds 2 to 8 inches past the floor for a formal look, but it collects dust and pet hair and is a trip hazard, so it belongs in low-traffic formal rooms, not in a kitchen or a child's bedroom.

What is the standard curtain length for an 8-foot ceiling?

84 inches. With the rod mounted 4 to 6 inches above the window frame, the rod sits about 84 inches from the floor on an 8-foot ceiling, and an 84-inch panel hangs floor length with its hem half an inch off the floor. For a 9-foot ceiling the standard length is 95 inches, and for a 10-foot ceiling it is 108 inches.

Should curtains be sill length or floor length?

Floor length for living rooms, bedrooms and dining rooms; sill length only for kitchens, bathrooms and windows above radiators, where a long curtain would be in the way or a hazard. A sill-length curtain in a living room looks shrunk and leaves the wall below the window bare, while a floor-length curtain in a kitchen hangs into the sink or the stove.

How much extra length do I need for a puddle?

Add 2 to 8 inches to the floor-length drop: 1 to 2 inches for a soft break, 3 to 4 inches for a medium puddle, and 6 to 8 inches for a full puddle. Measure the floor length first, then add the puddle on top, and buy the standard length at or above the total — a rod at 84 inches with a 4-inch puddle needs an 88-inch drop, so the 95-inch panel is the buy, hemmed to 88 inches.

How do you measure curtain length?

Measure from the top of the rod — or the bottom of the ring if you use rings, which adds about 1.5 inches — down to the sill, the floor or the puddle point, at three points across the window, and use the shortest measurement. Subtract half an inch for sill and floor length so the hem clears the sill or just kisses the floor, and add 2 to 8 inches for a puddle. Measure on the finished floor, not the subfloor.

What length curtains do I need for a sliding door?

Floor length, always — a sliding door never takes a sill-length curtain. The drop is the rod height to the floor, minus half an inch, which on an 8-foot ceiling is an 84-inch panel. If the door is used daily, consider a ceiling-mounted track instead of a rod, so the panels glide clear of the door handle, and keep the hem off the floor so it does not catch on the track.
