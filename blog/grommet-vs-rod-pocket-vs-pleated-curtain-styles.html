Curtain Size

Grommet vs Rod Pocket vs Pleated: How Style Changes Size

Curtain styles sizing is the part of buying curtains that most people skip — and it is the reason two panels that are both labelled 54 by 84 inches can hang completely differently on the same window. The header style — grommet, rod pocket, pinch pleat, tab top or back tab — decides how much fabric you need, how many panels to buy, how wide the stack is when the curtains are open, what diameter rod they fit, and even how long the panel should be. A grommet panel at 1.5 times fullness and a rod pocket panel at 2.5 times fullness are not the same purchase even though they come off the same shelf in the same size. This guide compares all five styles head to head with the same 60-inch window, so you can see the panel count, the stack-back width and the cost for each, and pick the style that fits your window, your rod and your budget.

The short answer: the header sets the fullness, and the fullness sets everything else

Every curtain style is a different way of attaching fabric to a rod, and each way gathers the fabric differently. Fullness is the ratio of flat fabric width to the rod width it covers when closed: a 54-inch panel on a 27-inch stretch of rod is 2 times fullness. Grommet and tab top styles hang in wide, soft folds and look right at 1.5 to 2 times fullness. Rod pocket and pinch pleat styles gather the fabric tightly and need 2 to 2.5 times fullness to look full instead of flat. Back tab sits in the middle at about 2 times. That one number — the fullness the style needs — drives the panel count, because the total fabric is the rod length times the fullness, divided by the panel width, rounded up to whole panels. It drives the cost, because more panels mean more money. And it drives the stack-back, because a panel that is gathered tighter takes up less rod when it is open.

The second number the style sets is the stack-back: the width a panel takes up on the rod when the curtain is open and pushed to the side. A 54-inch panel gathered at 2 times fullness takes up about 10 to 12 inches of rod when it is open, but the exact number depends on the fold. Grommet folds are wide and soft, so a grommet panel stacks to about 12 to 14 inches. Rod pocket gathers are tight, so a rod pocket panel stacks to about 9 to 11 inches. Pinch pleats fold flat against each other, so a pleated panel stacks to about 8 to 10 inches — the narrowest of the five. Tab top stacks to about 11 to 13 inches, and back tab to about 10 to 12 inches. The stack-back matters because it is what the rod extension has to hold: the rod extends 8 to 12 inches past the frame on each side, and the stack sits on that extension. A style with a wide stack needs the wide end of that range, and a style with a narrow stack is happy at the narrow end. The placement rules themselves — how high the rod goes and how far it extends — are covered in How High to Hang Curtains (Decorator Rules), and the panel-count math is worked out in What Size Curtains Do I Need?. This guide is about what changes when the style changes.

If you know your window size and the style you want, run it before you buy — it applies the right fullness for the style and rounds the same way this guide does.

Two things jump out of that table. First, the fullness range is the whole story for cost: a style that needs 2.5 times fullness needs about two-thirds more fabric than a style that needs 1.5 times, on the same rod. Second, the stack-back range is the whole story for how much glass stays visible when the curtains are open: a pleated panel stacks narrowest, a grommet panel stacks widest, and the difference is about 4 inches per panel — which is 8 inches of glass on a two-panel window.

Grommet: the fewest panels, the widest stack

Grommet curtains — also called eyelet curtains — have metal rings punched through the header, and the rod threads through the rings. The rings force the fabric into wide, even, soft folds, which is why grommet is the most forgiving style: the folds form themselves, and the curtain looks neat even if you never touch it. Because the folds are wide, grommet looks right at 1.5 to 2 times fullness, which is the lowest fabric requirement of any style. On a 60-inch window with an 84-inch rod (the 60-inch window plus trim plus 10 inches of extension on each side, from the placement guide), 1.75 times fullness is 147 inches of fabric, and 3 standard 54-inch panels give 162 inches, which is 1.93 times — full enough for a living room, and one panel fewer than the styles that need 2.5 times.

The trade is the stack. A grommet panel stacks to 12 to 14 inches of rod when it is open, because the wide folds do not compress. Three panels split across two sides is 1.5 panels per side, so the stack on each side is about 19 to 21 inches — roughly double the 10-inch rod extension. That means about 9 to 11 inches of gathered panel sits on the glass on each side when the curtains are open, and the visible glass is about 42 inches of the 60-inch window. That is normal for grommet, and it is the price of the wide folds: the window looks wider when the curtains are closed and a little narrower when they are open. If the window is small — a 36-inch bedroom window, for example — the same math gives a 61-inch rod, 2 panels, and a stack of about 13 inches per side against a 10-inch extension, so only 3 inches of glass is covered on each side. Grommet is at its best on small to medium windows, where the stack is a small share of the window.

Grommet has two hardware rules. The rod diameter must be smaller than the grommet hole: standard grommets have a 1.6-inch hole and fit rods up to 1.5 inches in diameter, and a rod thinner than 1 inch looks lost in the rings and lets the panel sag between them — 1 to 1.25 inches is the sweet spot. And the panel length is measured from the top of the rod to the floor plus about 2 inches, because the top 2 inches of the panel sits above the rod: the grommets hold the fabric so the header stands above the rod line. Buy the rod-to-floor length and the panel will hang 2 inches short of the floor; buy 2 inches longer and it will just kiss the floor. Ready-made grommet panels run $35 to $55 each in a mid-range fabric, so the 3-panel set for the 60-inch window is $105 to $165, plus a 1 to 1.25-inch rod at $30 to $60.

Rod pocket: the most fabric, the easiest install

Rod pocket curtains have a sewn channel at the top — the pocket — and the rod slides through it. There are no rings, no hooks and nothing to buy separately, which makes rod pocket the cheapest and simplest style to install: the rod goes through the pocket and the curtain is up. The pocket gathers the fabric into tight, even ruffles, and those ruffles only look right when there is a lot of fabric — 2 to 2.5 times fullness. On the same 84-inch rod, 2.25 times fullness is 189 inches of fabric, and 4 standard 54-inch panels give 216 inches, which is 2.57 times. That is one panel more than grommet on the same window, and at $25 to $40 per ready-made panel the 4-panel set is $100 to $160 — about the same total as the grommet set, because rod pocket panels are cheaper per panel even though you need more of them.

The tight gathers are also why rod pocket stacks narrow: 9 to 11 inches per 54-inch panel, the second-narrowest of the five. Four panels split two per side gives a stack of about 20 inches per side, which is almost the same as grommet's 19 to 21 inches — because rod pocket needs more panels, and the extra panel cancels out the narrower stack. The real difference between the styles is not the stack on a big window, it is the look: rod pocket gathers are tight and traditional, grommet folds are wide and modern, and the two read as completely different rooms even on the same rod.

Rod pocket has two limits. The pocket only fits a rod up to about 1.25 inches in diameter — a thicker rod will not go through, and forcing it splits the seam. And the curtain does not slide easily: the pocket grips the rod, so opening and closing a rod pocket curtain means walking the fabric along the rod by hand, which is fine on a bedroom window that opens once a day and a nuisance on a sliding door that opens twenty times a day. Rod pocket is at its best on windows that stay mostly closed or mostly open, on valances and tiers, and in rooms where the traditional gathered look is the point. It is the wrong choice for a door or a window that is opened and closed all day.

Pinch pleat: the narrowest stack, the highest cost

Pinch pleat curtains have the pleats sewn into the header — three folds pinched together at regular intervals — and they hang from hooks that clip into the pleats and slide on rings or a traverse rod. Because the pleats are sewn in, the folds are fixed: the curtain hangs in the same even pleats every time, open or closed, and it never needs arranging. Pinch pleat needs 2 to 2.5 times fullness, the same as rod pocket, so on the 84-inch rod it is again 4 panels and 216 inches of fabric. But pinch pleat panels are rarely sold ready-made in standard sizes: the pleats have to be sewn to the width of the window, so most pinch pleat curtains are custom-made, at $80 to $150 per panel. Four custom panels is $320 to $600 — three to four times the cost of the ready-made grommet or rod pocket set for the same window.

What that money buys is the narrowest stack of any style: 8 to 10 inches per 54-inch panel, because the pleats fold flat against each other instead of bunching. Four panels split two per side gives a stack of about 18 inches per side — the smallest stack on the 84-inch rod — so the visible glass when the curtains are open is about 48 inches of the 60-inch window, the most of any style. Pinch pleat also hangs the straightest: the sewn pleats hold the fabric in line from rod to floor, so the curtain never billows or twists, and it is the style decorators choose for formal living rooms, dining rooms and tall windows, where the drape is part of the furniture. The cost is the reason it is not the default: on a bedroom window, a $400 custom set does the same job as a $120 ready-made set, and the difference is only visible to someone who looks at curtains for a living.

Pinch pleat has one hardware requirement that the other styles do not: hooks and rings. The hooks clip into the back of the pleats, the rings slide on the rod, and the hook position sets the drop — moving the hook up or down by one pleat changes the length by about an inch, which is the easiest length adjustment of any style. A traverse rod, which opens and closes the curtains with a cord, is the other option, and it is the right one for a wide window or a sliding door, because the cord pulls both panels evenly and the pleats stay in line. Rings and hooks add $10 to $20 to the job, and a traverse rod adds $40 to $90, which goes on top of the custom panel cost.

Tab top and back tab: the casual middle ground

Tab top curtains have fabric loops sewn to the top of the panel, and the rod threads through the loops. The loops hold the fabric in wide, soft folds like grommet, and tab top needs the same 1.5 to 2 times fullness — 3 panels and 162 inches on the 84-inch rod, the same as grommet, at $30 to $50 per ready-made panel, or $90 to $150 for the set. The stack is 11 to 13 inches per panel, so 1.5 panels per side stacks to about 18 inches per side, a touch narrower than grommet. The difference is the look: the loops are visible above the rod, which reads as casual and handmade, and the panels do not slide easily, because the loops grip the rod the same way a rod pocket does. Tab top is at its best in bedrooms, kitchens and casual rooms, where the visible loops are part of the charm, and it is the wrong choice for a window that is opened and closed all day.

Back tab curtains are tab top with the loops hidden on the back of the panel, so the front of the curtain looks like a plain, flat header with soft folds — the tailored look of pinch pleat without the custom price. Back tab needs 2 times fullness, which is 168 inches of fabric on the 84-inch rod, and 3 standard 54-inch panels give 162 inches, which is 1.93 times — close enough, and the set is $105 to $180 at $35 to $60 per ready-made panel. The stack is 10 to 12 inches per panel, so 1.5 panels per side stacks to about 17 inches per side — the second-narrowest stack of the ready-made styles. Back tab slides better than tab top, because the loops are on the back and the fabric does not bunch on the rod, and it is the style to pick when you want the clean look of pleats on a ready-made budget. Its one weakness is the loops themselves: they are sewn to the back of the header, and on a heavy fabric they can pull and pucker, so back tab is best in light to mid-weight fabrics.

Worked example 1: a 36-inch bedroom window, three styles

Take the standard bedroom window: 36 inches wide, 48 inches tall, 8-foot ceiling. The rod is 61 inches — the 36-inch window plus 5 inches of trim plus 10 inches of extension on each side. Grommet at 1.75 times fullness needs 107 inches of fabric, and 2 standard 54-inch panels give 108 inches, which is 1.77 times — exactly right. The set is 2 panels at $35 to $55 each, $70 to $110, plus a 1-inch rod at $20 to $35, for $90 to $145 all in. The stack is one panel per side at 12 to 14 inches, against a 10-inch extension, so about 3 inches of panel sits on the glass on each side — barely noticeable, and the visible glass is about 30 inches of the 36-inch window.

Rod pocket at 2.25 times fullness needs 137 inches of fabric on the same 61-inch rod, and 3 panels give 162 inches, which is 2.66 times — fuller than the minimum, and right for a bedroom, where the curtain is closed at night and the tight gathers matter. The set is 3 panels at $25 to $40 each, $75 to $120, plus the rod at $20 to $35, for $95 to $155 all in — within $10 of the grommet set, for one more panel. The stack is 1.5 panels per side at 10 inches, about 15 inches per side, so about 5 inches of panel sits on the glass on each side — a little more than grommet, and the visible glass is about 26 inches. Pinch pleat at 2.25 times is also 3 panels, but custom-made at $80 to $150 each, $240 to $450, plus rings and hooks at $10 to $20, for $250 to $470 all in — two and a half times the ready-made sets, for a stack of about 13 inches per side and 30 inches of visible glass. On a bedroom window, the custom cost buys almost nothing the ready-made styles do not already give, and grommet or rod pocket is the better spend.

Worked example 2: a 96-inch sliding door, and why the style matters more

Now the wide case, where the style choice has real consequences: a 96-inch sliding glass door, 80 inches tall, 9-foot ceiling. The rod is 125 inches — the 96-inch door plus trim plus 12 inches of extension on each side, the top of the range, because the stack is large. Grommet at 1.75 times needs 219 inches of fabric, and 5 standard 54-inch panels give 270 inches, which is 2.16 times — the right fullness, and the set is 5 panels at $35 to $55 each, $175 to $275, plus a 1.25-inch rod at $50 to $80 and two centre brackets, for $240 to $375 all in. The stack is 2.5 panels per side at 13 inches, about 32 inches per side, so about 20 inches of panel sits on the glass on each side when the curtains are open — a fifth of the door on each side, which is the price of the wide folds on a wide window.

Rod pocket on the same door needs 2.25 times, which is 281 inches of fabric, and 6 panels give 324 inches, which is 2.59 times. The set is 6 panels at $25 to $40 each, $150 to $240, plus the rod at $50 to $80, for $200 to $320 all in — cheaper than grommet, but with a fatal problem: a rod pocket curtain does not slide. A sliding door is opened and closed all day, and a rod pocket panel has to be walked along the rod by hand every time, which means it is either left closed, left open, or pulled off the rod within a month. On a sliding door, the style choice is not about cost or stack, it is about sliding: grommet and back tab slide freely on the rod, pinch pleat on a traverse rod glides on a cord, and rod pocket and tab top do not slide at all. That one fact rules out two of the five styles for any door, whatever the table says about price.

How style changes the panel length

The panel length is measured from the rod to the floor in every style, but the header sits differently on the rod in each one, and that changes what length to buy. Grommet panels have about 2 inches of fabric above the rod — the header stands above the grommet line — so the finished drop is about 2 inches less than the labelled length: buy the rod-to-floor length plus 2 inches, or hang the rod 2 inches lower than the placement guide says. Rod pocket panels have a ruffle, usually 1 to 3 inches, above the pocket, and the same rule applies: the drop is the labelled length minus the ruffle, so buy 2 inches longer than the rod-to-floor distance, or the panel will hang 2 inches short. Tab top panels have loops 2 to 3 inches tall above the rod, and the drop is the labelled length minus the loop height — again, buy 2 inches longer. Back tab is the same: the loops are hidden, but they still hold the panel 2 inches above the rod line, so buy 2 inches longer.

Pinch pleat is the exception, and it is the easiest style to size for length: the hooks clip into the pleats, and moving the hook up or down by one pleat changes the drop by about an inch, so the panel can be adjusted after it is hung. Buy the rod-to-floor length, hang the panel, and move the hooks until the hem sits where you want it — no hemming, no rehanging the rod. That adjustability is part of what the custom price buys, and it is the reason pinch pleat is the style to pick when the ceiling is not level or the floor is not flat: a rod-to-floor measurement that is off by an inch in the middle of a wide window can be corrected hook by hook, while a grommet or rod pocket panel that is an inch short has to be hemmed or replaced. The length rules for each drop — sill, apron, floor and puddle — are covered in Curtain Length Guide: Sill, Apron, Floor or Puddle?, and the same rules apply to every style; only the 2-inch header adjustment changes.

The first two mistakes are the same mistake in opposite directions: using one style's fullness for another style's panels. The fullness is not a property of the window, it is a property of the header, and the panel count follows the header. The third mistake is the one that sends people back to the store: the rod and the header have to fit each other, and the pocket and the grommet hole are both fixed sizes. Measure the rod before you buy the panels, not after. Which style for which room

For a living room, grommet is the default: the wide folds look modern, the panels slide easily, and 3 panels on a 60-inch window is the cheapest full look. Back tab is the second choice for a living room, for the same reasons, with a cleaner header. For a formal living room or dining room, pinch pleat is the right spend: the sewn pleats hang straight, the stack is the narrowest, and the length adjusts hook by hook. For a bedroom, rod pocket is the best value: the tight gathers look right closed at night, the panels are the cheapest, and the window is opened once a day, so the sliding does not matter. Tab top is the bedroom's casual alternative, and it is the style to pick when the loops are part of the look. For a kitchen, tab top or grommet on a short rod, at sill or apron length, because both slide easily and both come off the rod for washing without unhooking anything. For a sliding door, grommet or pinch pleat on a traverse rod, and never rod pocket or tab top — the door is opened all day, and the curtain has to slide. And for sheers, which are the one case where the fullness rule changes, use 2.5 to 3 times fullness in any style, because sheer fabric is thin and needs more of it to read as a curtain instead of a veil.

Frequently asked questions

Which curtain style needs the least fabric?

Grommet and tab top, at 1.5 to 2 times fullness. On an 84-inch rod that is 3 standard 54-inch panels, against 4 panels for rod pocket and pinch pleat at 2 to 2.5 times fullness. Back tab sits in the middle at 2 times, which is also 3 panels on the same rod.

Do grommet curtains need a bigger rod?

They need a rod in the 1 to 1.25 inch range. Standard grommets have a 1.6-inch hole and fit rods up to 1.5 inches, but a rod thinner than 1 inch looks lost in the rings and lets the panel sag between them. Rod pocket panels only fit rods up to about 1.25 inches, so check the rod diameter against the header before you buy either one.

Can I change a rod pocket curtain to grommet?

No — the header is sewn in, and the pocket cannot be converted to rings. The panel has to be replaced, or re-sewn by a workroom, which costs about as much as a new ready-made panel. Pick the style before you buy the panels, not after.

Which curtain style is best for a sliding door?

Grommet, back tab, or pinch pleat on a traverse rod — all three slide freely. Rod pocket and tab top grip the rod and have to be walked along by hand, which is a nuisance on a door that is opened all day. The rod for a sliding door also needs a 3 to 4 inch projection so the curtain clears the handle.

Do pleated curtains cost more?

Yes — pinch pleat panels are usually custom-made, at $80 to $150 per panel, against $25 to $55 per ready-made panel for the other styles. Four custom panels for a 60-inch window is $320 to $600, three to four times the ready-made set. What the money buys is sewn-in pleats that hang straight, the narrowest stack, and a length that adjusts hook by hook.

What fullness should sheer curtains have?

2.5 to 3 times fullness, in any style, because sheer fabric is thin and needs more of it to read as a curtain instead of a veil. On an 84-inch rod that is 4 to 5 standard 54-inch panels. Sheers are the one case where the style's normal fullness does not apply — the fabric, not the header, sets the number.
