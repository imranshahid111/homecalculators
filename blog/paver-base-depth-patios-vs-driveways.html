How Deep Should a Paver Base Be? Patios vs Driveways

Paver base depth is the single number that decides whether a patio stays flat or starts sinking — and it is not the same number for every project. A patio that only carries foot traffic needs 4–6 inches of compacted gravel base. A driveway that carries cars needs 6–8 inches, and a driveway that carries heavy trucks or sits on clay can need 10–12 inches. The difference is not guesswork: it follows from the load the surface must carry, the soil underneath, and how deep frost reaches in winter. Get the depth right and the pavers above it have a quiet, uneventful life. Shave it thin to save a load of gravel and you buy a repair job — lifting every paver, regrading the base, and laying the whole surface again.

The short answer, by project

If you only need one table from this guide, it is this one. Depths below are compacted gravel base — the finished, compacted thickness, not the loose pile you order — and they sit under 1 inch of bedding sand plus the pavers themselves.

Why depth changes with the load

A gravel base does two jobs: it spreads concentrated loads over a wider area of soil, and it drains water away from that soil so it stays firm. Both jobs get harder as the load grows, and depth is what buys capacity. Think of it like snowshoes: a wider, thicker base spreads the same weight over more soil, so the pressure on any one spot drops. A person standing on a patio presses down with roughly 150–250 pounds spread over two feet — trivial for compacted gravel. A car presses down with about 1,000 pounds per tire on a contact patch the size of a hand. Without enough base, that pressure reaches the soil in a concentrated spike, the soil yields a fraction of an inch at a time, and the pavers above it follow the soil down. The ruts you see in a failed driveway are not the pavers failing — they are the soil failing under a base too thin to spread the load.

This is also why driveways need thicker pavers as well as a thicker base: driveway pavers are typically 3⅛ inches thick versus 2⅜ inches for patios, because the paver itself must bridge and distribute wheel loads without cracking. A patio paver on a driveway will crack even over a good base, and a driveway paver over a thin base will sink even though it does not crack. Base depth and paver thickness are a pair — get both right for the load.

Worked quantities: what depth means in gravel and dollars

Depth only becomes real when you convert it to tons and money. The conversion is straightforward: volume in cubic feet = area in square feet × depth in feet, and compacted crushed gravel weighs roughly 100 pounds per cubic foot (about 1.35–1.5 tons per cubic yard). Because compaction squeezes loose gravel down by 15–20%, order about 20% more loose material than the compacted volume — your supplier can confirm the conversion for their product.

Take a 10×10 patio (100 square feet). At a 4-inch compacted base, the compacted volume is 100 × ⅓ = 33 cubic feet, or about 1.25 cubic yards — roughly 1.7 tons of gravel. Bump that patio to a 6-inch base and you need 50 cubic feet, about 1.85 cubic yards, or roughly 2.5 tons. The difference between the minimum and the generous patio base is under a ton of gravel — at 2026 delivered prices of roughly $40–$70 per ton, that is about $35–$60. On a project that will cost thousands all-in, the thicker base is the cheapest insurance available, and it is the upgrade most worth taking if your soil is anything less than firm.

Now a driveway: a single-car drive of 10×20 feet (200 square feet) at an 8-inch base needs 200 × ⅔ = 133 cubic feet, about 5 cubic yards, or roughly 6.5–7 tons. At 6 inches the same drive needs about 5 tons. The 2-inch upgrade costs roughly $60–$140 of gravel — again, a small fraction of a driveway budget, and the difference between a drive that stays flat under daily car traffic and one that ruts in the wheel tracks within a few years. When contractors quote a driveway, ask what base depth is in the quote; a quote that is a few hundred dollars cheaper because it hides a 4-inch base is not a bargain.

The three adjustments that change the standard depth

The table above is the starting point. Three site conditions push the number up, and ignoring any of them is how a "by the book" base still fails.

1. Soil type: add 2 inches on clay, subtract nothing on gravel

Clay is the problem soil. It swells when wet, shrinks when dry, and loses bearing strength dramatically when saturated — a base that is fine on sandy or gravelly soil can be marginal on clay. On clay, take the top of the patio range (6 inches) as your minimum, and consider 8 inches for a driveway that would otherwise get 6. On the other end, if you excavate down to firm gravel or bedrock, the standard depth is plenty; the base is spreading load onto ground that barely moves. The quick field test: dig a test hole after rain. If the soil at base depth is sticky, grey and plastic, you have clay and you should add depth. If it crumbles and drains, you are on good ground.

2. Frost: depth is not the same as frost protection

A common misconception is that a deeper base protects against frost heave. It helps, but the real protection is drainage: frost heave happens when water in the soil freezes and expands, lifting the surface. A thick base of free-draining crushed gravel with a geotextile separator keeps water out of the fine soil below and gives freezing water somewhere to go, which matters more than an extra inch of depth. In cold climates (frost depths of 24 inches or more), the practical rules are: never build on topsoil or organic soil, always use a geotextile separator so fines cannot pump up into the base, and slope the base so water drains out rather than pooling under the pavers. If your area freezes hard, the 6-inch patio base is the safer default even for light use.

3. Drainage and slope: depth without drainage is a bathtub

A deep base that holds water is worse than a shallow base that drains. Water trapped in the base softens the subgrade, and a saturated subgrade fails under loads it would carry easily when dry. The base must slope away from the house at 1/8 to 1/4 inch per foot, the same slope as the finished surface, and the low edge must have somewhere for water to go — a lawn, a swale, a drain. If your patio sits in a low spot where water collects, fix the drainage before you argue about base depth: regrade the yard, add a drain, or raise the patio. No depth of gravel rescues a base that sits in standing water.

How the base is built: lifts, not one deep dump

Depth is only half the specification — the other half is compaction. A 6-inch base dumped in one layer and compacted only from the top is loose in the middle, and that loose middle settles during the first rainy season. The correct method is lifts of 2–3 inches: spread a lift, wet it lightly, run a plate compactor over it until it stops settling, then repeat. A 6-inch base is two or three lifts; an 8-inch driveway base is three or four. A plate compactor rents for roughly $50–$75 a day in 2026 and is non-negotiable for anything bigger than a stepping-stone path. When you measure depth, measure the compacted thickness after each lift — a base that measures 6 inches loose will measure closer to 5 inches compacted, and ordering or specifying by loose depth is how projects quietly end up an inch thin.

Too thin vs too thick: both have a cost

Too thin is the expensive mistake: rutting, sinking, puddling, and a lift-and-relay repair that runs roughly $10–$20 per square foot at contractor rates in 2026. Too thick is a much smaller problem — it costs a bit more gravel and a bit more digging, and on a patio it can complicate the finished height relative to doors and siding. There is one real "too thick" trap: using extra base depth to compensate for a base that was never compacted, or to bury a drainage problem instead of fixing it. Depth is not a substitute for compaction or drainage. Within reason, though, erring one inch deeper on a patio base is the rare construction decision where the downside is a few dozen dollars and the upside is a decade of flat surface.

Checking a contractor's base depth

If you are hiring the work, base depth is the easiest thing to verify and the most common place for quiet corner-cutting, because it is buried before you ever see the finished patio. Three checks work: first, ask for the depth in writing in the quote, as a compacted depth, not a loose depth. Second, be on site when the base goes in and measure it yourself with a tape — measure in several spots, not just at the edge where it is easy to fake. Third, ask how many lifts they compact; "one pass and done" on a 6-inch base is a red flag regardless of what the quote says. A contractor who welcomes these questions is a contractor whose base you can trust; one who waves them away is telling you something too.

Frequently asked questions

How deep should a paver base be for a patio?

4–6 inches of compacted crushed gravel, plus 1 inch of bedding sand. Use 4 inches on firm, well-drained soil and 6 inches on clay, wet ground, or in freeze-thaw climates. Anything under 4 inches on a patio is a future repair, not a saving.

How deep should a paver base be for a driveway?

6–8 inches of compacted gravel base for a residential car driveway, with 3⅛-inch driveway pavers (not 2⅜-inch patio pavers) on top. Driveways for heavy vehicles or RVs need 8–12 inches. The extra depth spreads wheel loads that are far heavier and more concentrated than foot traffic.

Does paver base depth include the bedding sand?

No. When a guide says "4–6 inch base," it means the compacted gravel layer only. The full stack is gravel base + 1 inch of bedding sand + the paver thickness, so a 6-inch patio base means roughly 9 inches of total excavation below the finished surface.

Should I add extra base depth on clay soil?

Yes — add about 2 inches over the standard depth, and always use a woven geotextile separator between the soil and the gravel. Clay swells when wet and loses strength when saturated, so it needs a thicker load-spreading layer, and the fabric stops the gravel from sinking into the clay over wet seasons.

Is a deeper base better in cold, frosty climates?

Depth helps, but drainage matters more. Frost heave is caused by water in the soil freezing and expanding, so a free-draining gravel base, a geotextile separator, and a base sloped to drain water away protect better than an extra inch of gravel alone. In hard-freeze areas, default to the 6-inch patio base even for light use.

How do I measure paver base depth correctly?

Measure the compacted thickness, not the loose pile: after each 2–3 inch lift is compacted, measure from the top of the compacted gravel to the subgrade in several spots with a tape. Loose gravel compacts down 15–20%, so a base that measures 6 inches loose is only about 5 inches compacted.
