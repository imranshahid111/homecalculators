How Many Tons of Asphalt for a Driveway? (With Examples)

Figuring out how many tons of asphalt for a driveway is a 30-second calculation, not a contractor's secret: measure the driveway in feet, decide the compacted thickness in inches, and run one formula — length × width × depth ÷ 166. A single-car 10×40 driveway at 3 inches needs about 8 tons; a standard two-car 20×40 needs about 16. This guide works through the formula, the worked tonnage for five real driveway sizes, the thickness rules that decide whether you order 2 or 3 inches, why you always round UP, and what a ton of asphalt costs in 2026.

The short answer: the ÷166 formula

Asphalt is sold by the ton, and the conversion from driveway dimensions to tons is one division. Hot mix asphalt weighs about 145 pounds per cubic foot, compacted. Multiply length × width × depth in feet, multiply by 145, divide by 2,000 pounds per ton — and that collapses to the shortcut every paving crew uses:

Tons = length (ft) × width (ft) × depth (in) ÷ 166

Depth here means compacted inches — the finished thickness after the roller does its work, not the loose pile the truck dumps. A quick first example: a 10×40 single-car driveway at 3 inches compacted is 10 × 40 × 3 ÷ 166 = 7.2 tons. You cannot order 7.2 tons from most plants, so the order-up rule (covered below) takes you to 8 tons. That is the entire method. The rest of this guide is about the details that keep that number honest.

Where the 166 comes from (the one-minute version)

If the number 166 looks arbitrary, here is the whole derivation, because trusting a formula you cannot check is how people order two tons short. One square foot of asphalt at one inch of compacted depth is a volume of 1/12 of a cubic foot. At roughly 145 pounds per cubic foot, that slice weighs about 12.1 pounds. Multiply by 2,000 pounds per ton and divide: 2,000 ÷ 12.1 ≈ 165.5, conventionally rounded to 166. So each square foot-inch of driveway eats 1/166 of a ton, or about 12 pounds per inch.

Two related rules fall out of the same math and are worth memorizing. First: one square yard of asphalt at one inch deep weighs about 110 pounds — handy when a contractor quotes in square yards. Second: one cubic yard of compacted asphalt weighs about 2 tons (27 cubic feet × 145 pounds = 3,915 pounds). Contractors who order by the cubic yard and plants that sell by the ton are doing the same arithmetic; the cubic-yard version is how you cross-check a quote that does not add up.

The honest caveat: 145 pounds per cubic foot is the standard compacted density, but mixes vary. A dense-graded highway mix can run a little heavier; an open-graded or high-recycled mix can run a little lighter. For residential estimating, 145 is the number everyone works to, and the order-up rule absorbs the spread.

The 12×200 example is worth a second look: at 43.4 exact tons, the 10% allowance puts you at 47.7 — and you order 48, not 47, because the 10% rule protects against the real world, not the arithmetic. Feathered edges along the sides, a slightly soft subgrade that takes extra mix in low spots, and the mix that sticks to the truck bed all consume the margin. On small jobs the same logic applies in reverse: the 10×10 patch only needs 1.3 tons, but no plant will sell you 1.3 tons with a smile — minimums of 1 to 2 tons are the norm, and short-load fees (covered below) mean the minimum is often the cheapest option anyway.

How thick should driveway asphalt be?

Tonnage is half the question; the other half is thickness, because one inch of extra depth adds a third to your tonnage. Thickness is chosen by load, not by aesthetics:

UseCompacted thicknessNotes
Resurfacing over sound asphalt (overlay)1.5–2 inFine for a cosmetic refresh; the old pavement is your base
Residential driveway, cars only2.5–3 inThe standard: 3 inches over 4–6 in of compacted gravel base
Driveway with delivery trucks, RVs, boats3–4 inHeavy, infrequent loads — thickness carries the point load
Commercial / parking areas4–6 inDifferent world: engineered base and commercial mixes

Two inches of compacted asphalt is the minimum for a real residential driveway — thinner than that and the layer cannot resist cracking from thermal movement and edge loads. If a quote specifies a single 2-inch lift on new gravel, that is acceptable only on a well-compacted 6-inch aggregate base in mild climates; in freeze-thaw country, ask for 3 inches, ideally placed in two lifts (a 2-inch base course plus a 1-inch surface course) for density you can actually compact.

And the layer math matters: each inch of thickness on the same 20×40 driveway adds 800 ÷ 166 ≈ 4.8 tons. The difference between a 2-inch job and a 3-inch job on a two-car driveway is roughly 5 tons of mix — about $400–$600 in material — for a driveway that lasts meaningfully longer. That is the cheapest durability money in the whole project.

The order-up rule: always round up, never down

The formula gives you the exact compacted tonnage. Nobody orders the exact number, for four reasons that compound:

1. Waste is real. Feathered edges, hand-worked transitions at the garage apron, and the inevitable low spots in the base each take mix. Ten percent is the industry-standard allowance for residential paving.

2. Plants sell in rounded increments. Many plants round to the nearest half ton or ton. Ordering "8 tons" when you need 7.2 is not a choice — it is how the transaction works.

3. Minimums and short-load fees. Most plants have a 1- to 2-ton minimum; under about 5 tons you may pay a short-load fee ($50–$150 is typical in 2026) for the truck time. A second trip for "the 1 ton we were short" costs far more than the spare ton would have.

4. Hot mix waits for no one. Asphalt cools and stiffens in the truck. Running short mid-pave means a cold joint — a visible seam and a weak line — or a stalled crew waiting on a second load. The extra ton is insurance against the most expensive problem on the job.

The rule of thumb professionals use: order the formula's exact tonnage + 10%, then round UP to the nearest whole or half ton. The 20×40 at 3 inches: 14.5 × 1.10 = 15.95 → order 16 tons. The leftover half ton, if any, goes to a thickened apron edge or a patch at the street — it is never truly wasted.

The mistakes that blow the tonnage estimate

Measuring the wrong rectangle. Driveways flare at the street, widen at the garage, and curve. Measure each section separately and add the areas — a single generous "about 20×50" can be off by 15%, which is 2.5 tons you either over-order or run short on.

Using loose depth instead of compacted depth. Hot mix compacts about 20%: 2.5 inches of loose mix becomes 2 inches finished. The formula works in compacted inches because density is quoted compacted. If a contractor says "we'll lay it 3 inches," confirm that means 3 inches after rolling — a 2.4-inch finished job billed as 3 is 20% less pavement.

Forgetting the base. New asphalt on soft or thin gravel will flex, crack and consume extra mix as the base deforms under the roller. A 4- to 6-inch compacted aggregate base is part of the job, not an optional extra — it is also the cheaper material, so skimping on base to buy extra asphalt is the most expensive mistake on this list.

Ordering before measuring twice. This one costs a truck fee every time. Measure the driveway, sketch it with dimensions, run the formula, add 10% — then re-measure the one section you guessed at. The second measurement is free; the second truck is not.

Frequently asked questions

What is the formula for asphalt tonnage?

Tons = length (ft) × width (ft) × depth in inches ÷ 166. Depth means compacted inches — the finished thickness after rolling. For example, a 10×40 driveway at 3 inches is 10 × 40 × 3 ÷ 166 = 7.2 tons; add 10% and round up to order 8 tons.

How many tons of asphalt do I need for a 2-car driveway?

A standard 20×40 two-car driveway at 3 inches compacted needs 14.5 tons exactly — order 16 tons with the 10% allowance and rounding up. At 2.5 inches the same driveway needs 15.1 tons with the allowance; order 16 or 17 tons.

How thick should a residential asphalt driveway be?

The standard is 2.5 to 3 inches of compacted asphalt over 4 to 6 inches of compacted gravel base. Two inches is the minimum for cars only. Driveways that carry delivery trucks, RVs or boat trailers should get 3 to 4 inches. Overlays on sound existing asphalt run 1.5 to 2 inches.

Can you buy less than a ton of asphalt?

Usually not directly from a plant — most have a 1- to 2-ton minimum, and orders under about 5 tons often carry a short-load fee of $50–$150. For very small patches, some plants sell by the ton with pickup only, or you can use bagged cold-patch asphalt from a hardware store.

How much does a cubic yard of asphalt weigh?

About 2 tons. One cubic yard (27 cubic feet) of compacted hot mix asphalt weighs roughly 3,915 pounds at standard density. This is the cross-check for contractors who order by the cubic yard: tons ≈ cubic yards × 2.

How many square feet does a ton of asphalt cover?

About 55 square feet at 3 inches compacted, 83 square feet at 2 inches, or 166 square feet at 1 inch — each square foot-inch of driveway takes 1/166 of a ton, so the thinner the layer, the further one ton goes.

Is it cheaper to buy asphalt by the ton and pave it yourself?

The material is the cheapest part: 8 tons for a single-car driveway runs roughly $440–$880 picked up. But DIY paving needs a compacted base, a way to spread and roll hot mix fast before it cools, and a minimum order that may exceed what you need. Small patches are genuinely DIY-friendly; full driveways are a job where the crew's equipment and speed usually pay for themselves.
