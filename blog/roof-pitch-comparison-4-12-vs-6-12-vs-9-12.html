4/12 vs 6/12 vs 9/12: Which Pitch Is Right for Your Climate?

This roof pitch comparison settles the three pitches almost every American house is built with: 4/12, the gentle slope that is the lowest pitch asphalt shingles are rated for; 6/12, the middle-of-the-road pitch that most production builders default to; and 9/12, the steep pitch that sheds snow and rain fast and opens up a real attic room. They look similar from the street — a few degrees apart — but they behave very differently in snow, in wind, in cost, and in how much of your attic you can actually stand up in. A 4/12 roof holds snow that a 9/12 roof sheds, a 9/12 roof costs roughly $2,500–$3,500 more to shingle than the same footprint at 4/12, and the same 30-foot-wide house has zero standing-height attic at 4/12 and an 11-foot-wide usable room at 9/12. This guide compares all three on the numbers that matter — degrees, roof area, snow load, wind, cost and attic space — and then matches each pitch to the climate it suits best, so you can tell whether the pitch on your plans (or on your existing house) is right for where you live.

Read the table in two directions. Going from 4/12 to 9/12, the roof gets steeper by about 18 degrees, the surface area grows by roughly 19 percent (1,054 to 1,250 sq ft per 1,000 sq ft of footprint), and the ridge rises by more than six feet. That extra area and height is the whole story: more surface means more material and more labor, and more height means more attic volume — but also a roof that sheds weather instead of holding it. Every section below prices one side of that trade-off.

If you have not measured your own roof yet, do that before reading the climate advice as a prescription — a roof you think is 6/12 is often 5/12 or 7/12, and a single pitch step changes the snow and cost math. What each pitch looks and feels like

4/12 — the low, wide look. From the curb, a 4/12 roof reads as a low, horizontal roofline with a shallow gable. It is the lowest pitch that takes a standard shingle install — below it, shingles need the special low-slope method, and below 2/12 they are not allowed at all, as explained in our guide to the minimum pitch for shingles, metal and tile. Inside, the attic is storage-only: on a 30-foot span the ridge is just 5 feet above the ceiling joists, so nobody stands up anywhere. Builders like 4/12 because it uses the least material, needs no steep-slope labor surcharge, and keeps the house under height limits on tight lots. Homeowners notice it most in snow country, where a 4/12 roof holds its snow load until it melts or slides in one heavy sheet.

6/12 — the default. A 6/12 roof is the pitch most people picture when they picture a house: a clear, readable gable, steep enough to look like a roof and gentle enough to walk on carefully with a harness. It is the most common pitch on production homes for a reason — it clears the shingle minimum with margin, sheds rain well, holds a moderate snow load, and gives a ridge 7 feet 6 inches high on a 30-foot span, which is just enough for a narrow strip of standing room in the middle of the attic. If you have no strong climate reason to go steeper or shallower, 6/12 is the safe default, and it is the pitch most roofers quote fastest because they build it every week.

9/12 — the steep statement. A 9/12 roof is unmistakably steep: the gable is tall, the roofline dominates the front of the house, and the attic becomes a real room. On a 30-foot span the ridge sits 11 feet 3 inches above the ceiling joists. Crews charge a steep-slope surcharge from around 7/12 or 8/12 upward, because the work is slower and needs staging and safety lines, and the roof surface is a quarter larger than the same footprint at 4/12. What you buy for that money is weather performance — snow and rain leave fast — and living space: a finished attic bedroom or bonus room is realistic at 9/12 in a way it simply is not at 4/12.

Snow: where the pitches really separate

Snow is the climate factor that most clearly separates these three pitches, because the building code itself treats them differently. The roof snow load is the ground snow load multiplied by a slope factor, and that factor stays at 1.0 up to 30 degrees — which covers both 4/12 (18.4°) and 6/12 (26.6°) — then drops as the roof gets steeper. For a warm roof under the ASCE 7 formula, the slope factor at 9/12 (36.9°) is about 0.83, meaning a 9/12 roof is designed for roughly 17 percent less snow load than a 4/12 or 6/12 roof in the same town.

Worked example: 30 psf ground snow load. Take a town with a 30 psf ground snow load — typical of the upper Midwest and New England foothills. The flat-roof snow load is 0.7 × 30 = 21 psf. At 4/12 and 6/12, the slope factor is 1.0, so the design roof snow load stays 21 psf. At 9/12, the factor is about 0.83, so the design load drops to about 17.4 psf. On a 1,500 sq ft roof surface that is a difference of roughly 5,400 lbs of design snow load — about the weight of a large SUV — that the structure does not have to carry. That is not a reason to undersize the framing (the code still sets minimums), but it is a real structural margin, and it is why steep roofs in snow country have fewer ice-dam and sagging problems.

The lived experience matches the math. A 4/12 roof holds snow until a warm spell or a slide, and the snow that sits there feeds ice dams at the eaves: meltwater runs down to the cold overhang, refreezes, and backs up under the shingles. A 6/12 roof holds less and slides sooner, so ice dams are less common but not rare. A 9/12 roof sheds snow in smaller, more frequent slides — you will hear it come off — and ice dams are rare because the snow does not sit long enough to melt and refreeze at the eave. In heavy-snow regions (ground snow loads of 50 psf and up), the honest advice is 9/12 or steeper, or a metal roof on 6/12, because metal's smooth surface sheds snow that shingles grip. In places that see snow a few times a year, 6/12 is plenty, and 4/12 is fine if the attic is well insulated and ventilated so the roof deck stays cold.

Rain and wind: steep sheds, shallow resists

Rain is simpler than snow: steeper is better, almost without exception. Water runs off a 9/12 roof roughly twice as fast as off a 4/12 roof, so wind-driven rain has less time to find a gap under a shingle lap, and the roof dries faster between storms — which matters for moss and algae in wet climates. A 4/12 roof in a rainy climate is legal and common, but it is the pitch where a sloppy flashing job or a worn shingle shows up first, because the water moves slowly and sits on every seam longer. If you live somewhere with 50-plus inches of annual rain, 6/12 is the practical minimum most roofers recommend, and 9/12 is the pitch that forgives the most sins.

Wind cuts the other way, and this is the one place 4/12 wins. A low roof presents less surface to the wind, and the uplift forces on the windward slope are lower — which is why the low, hipped roofs of hurricane country are often 4/12 or 5/12. A 9/12 roof catches more wind, and the windward slope sees higher uplift pressure, so in hurricane and high-wind zones a steep roof needs the enhanced fastening schedule to match: six nails per shingle instead of four, a sealed roof deck, and hip construction rather than gable ends where possible. The pitch itself is not the problem — a properly fastened 9/12 roof survives hurricanes — but the fastening is not optional, and it costs more. The rule of thumb: in high-wind zones, pick the pitch for the rain and snow, then pay for the fastening the wind demands. Never pick a shallow pitch just to dodge the fastening bill, because a 4/12 roof with four nails per shingle in a 130 mph zone fails just as surely as a steep one.

Cost compared: the same house at three pitches

Pitch moves the price two ways: it changes the roof's surface area, and above about 7/12 or 8/12 it triggers a steep-slope labor surcharge. Here is the full comparison for the same house — a 1,500 sq ft footprint, architectural shingles, 10 percent waste allowance — so the difference is the pitch alone, not the house.

The gap from 4/12 to 6/12 is small — about $550, or 6 percent — because the area grows only slightly and there is no labor surcharge. The gap from 6/12 to 9/12 is the real jump: about $2,100–$2,600, or 20–25 percent, split between the extra 3 squares of material and the steep-slope surcharge, which crews typically price at $0.50–$1.00 per sq ft of roof surface for pitches above 7/12 or 8/12. The surcharge is labor only — the crew works slower, stages more, and runs safety lines — so it does not buy you better shingles, just the same shingles installed on a steeper surface.

Worked example: price per year of service. The cost gap looks different when you spread it over the roof's life. Architectural shingles last about 25–30 years. The 4/12 roof at $9,900 costs about $330–$396 per year of service; the 9/12 roof at $12,550–$13,050 costs about $418–$522 per year — roughly $90–$125 more per year. Against that, the 9/12 roof in snow country avoids the ice-dam repairs and the shortened shingle life that a snow-loaded 4/12 roof suffers, and it adds a finished attic room that a 4/12 roof cannot offer at any price. In a mild climate with little snow, the 4/12 roof's lower annual cost is the better deal, and the money is better spent on the shingle quality or the underlayment. Attic space: the hidden value of a steep pitch

The most underrated difference between these pitches is what happens inside the roof. A roof is a triangle, and the pitch sets how much of that triangle is tall enough to stand in. The rule for usable space is simple: measure the width where the ceiling height is at least 7 feet, which is the usual minimum for a habitable room.

Worked example: 30-foot span, ceiling joists as the attic floor. The ridge height above the joists is half the span times the pitch: 15 ft × 4/12 = 5 ft at 4/12, 15 ft × 6/12 = 7 ft 6 in at 6/12, and 15 ft × 9/12 = 11 ft 3 in at 9/12. The width with at least 7 feet of headroom is zero at 4/12 (the ridge itself is only 5 feet high), about 2 feet at 6/12 (a narrow strip right under the ridge), and about 11 feet 4 inches at 9/12 (the roof is 7 feet high at 9 ft 4 in out from the ridge on each side, so 2 × (15 − 9.33) = 11.3 ft of standing width). That 11-foot width is a real room: a bedroom, an office, or a bonus room with a dormer or two, adding 300-plus sq ft of living space without changing the footprint. A 6/12 attic is storage with a crouch; a 4/12 attic is storage with a crawl.

This is why the pitch decision is also a floor-plan decision. If you plan to finish the attic — now or in ten years — 9/12 pays for itself in a way the cost table above does not capture, because adding 300 sq ft inside the existing footprint costs far less per square foot than an addition. If the attic will never be more than insulation and Christmas boxes, the 4/12 or 6/12 roof is the cheaper roof, and the steep pitch is money spent on a room you will never use. Decide this before the framing goes up: changing pitch after the roof is built means new rafters and a new roof together.

Two cautions before you treat the table as a prescription. First, local code and the neighborhood matter: some jurisdictions and HOAs set a minimum or maximum pitch, and a 9/12 roof on a street of 4/12 ranches will look out of place and may trip a height limit. Second, the material interacts with the pitch — metal sheds snow on a 6/12 roof about as well as shingles do on a 9/12 roof, because the surface is smooth, so a metal roof can buy you a shallower pitch in snow country. Check the material minimums in our guide to the minimum pitch for every material before you lock the pitch in, and if your roof is already built, the pitch you have is the pitch you design around — the climate advice above tells you what to watch for (ice dams at 4/12, wind fastening at 9/12), not what to rebuild.

Can you change the pitch of an existing roof?

Yes, but it is a structural job, not a roofing job, and it is priced like one. Re-pitching means removing the roof covering and decking, cutting or replacing the rafters or trusses, raising the ridge, and re-roofing — typically $15,000–$40,000 for a 1,500 sq ft house, depending on the span, the framing, and whether the interior ceilings are affected. It is worth it when the pitch is causing a real problem — chronic ice dams at 4/12 in snow country, or a finished-attic plan that needs 9/12 headroom — and it is not worth it to save a few hundred dollars on a re-roof. Most of the time, the better move is to keep the pitch and fix the problem at the pitch you have: better underlayment and ice-and-water shield at the eaves for a 4/12 roof in snow country, enhanced fastening for a 9/12 roof in wind country, and a metal roof if you want snow shedding without the re-pitch bill. Get two quotes before you commit — one for the re-pitch and one for the fix at the existing pitch — because the fix is usually a third of the price.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common roof pitch?

6/12 is the most common pitch on American production homes. It clears the 4/12 shingle minimum with margin, sheds rain well, holds a moderate snow load, and costs only about 6 percent more to roof than the same footprint at 4/12, with no steep-slope labor surcharge.

Is a 4/12 roof pitch good?

Yes, in mild climates. 4/12 is the lowest pitch asphalt shingles are rated for, it is the cheapest of the three to build, and it has the lowest wind uplift. It is a poor choice in heavy snow country, where it holds snow and feeds ice dams, and its attic is storage-only — the ridge is just 5 feet high on a 30-foot span.

Is 9/12 too steep for a roof?

No, 9/12 is a normal steep pitch, but it costs about 20-25 percent more to roof than the same house at 4/12, because the surface is a quarter larger and crews charge a steep-slope surcharge above about 7/12 or 8/12. What you get for it is fast snow and rain shedding, a lower design snow load, and a real finished-attic room.

Which roof pitch is best for snow?

9/12 is the best of the three for snow. Its slope factor is about 0.83 against 1.0 for 4/12 and 6/12, so the design snow load is roughly 17 percent lower, and it sheds snow in frequent small slides instead of holding it. In heavy-snow regions, 9/12 or steeper — or a metal roof on 6/12 — is the honest recommendation.

Does a steeper roof cost more?

Yes. For a 1,500 sq ft footprint, architectural shingles run about $9,900 at 4/12, $10,450 at 6/12, and $12,550-$13,050 at 9/12. The 9/12 premium comes from the larger surface (1,875 vs 1,581 sq ft) plus a steep-slope labor surcharge of roughly $1,000-$1,500.

Can I finish an attic under a 6/12 roof?

Not as a proper room. On a 30-foot span, a 6/12 roof gives only about 2 feet of width with 7 feet of headroom — a narrow strip under the ridge. A 9/12 roof on the same span gives about 11 feet 4 inches of standing-height width, which is a real bedroom or office. A 4/12 roof gives none at all.
