Upholstery Yardage: Chairs, Sofas and Cushions Estimated

Upholstery fabric yardage is the number that decides whether a reupholstery project comes in on budget or stalls one panel short — and it is the number most people guess at. A three-seat sofa needs 14 to 18 yards of plain 54-inch fabric, an armchair needs 5 to 7, and a single dining chair seat needs just three-quarters of a yard, but those figures only hold if you measure every panel, add the seam and tuck-in allowances, and charge the pattern repeat on patterned fabric. Buy too little and the project stops while the dye lot sells out; buy too much and you have paid $30 a yard for fabric that sits in a cupboard. This guide gives the yardage for every common piece, the measure-every-panel formula that produces an exact number for your furniture, four worked examples with real costs, and the pattern, width and waste rules that turn an estimate into an order.

The formula: measure every panel, then add it up

Upholstery yardage has no single shortcut formula the way a curtain does, because a sofa is not one rectangle — it is fifteen or twenty panels of different sizes. The method professionals use is to measure every panel, add a cutting allowance to each one, lay the panels out mentally on the fabric width, and add the lengths. The steps are simple, and the only tool you need is a tape measure.

Step one: list the panels. For a dining chair, that is the seat top, the seat underside or the back front and back. For an armchair, it is the inside back, outside back, seat, two inside arms, two outside arms, two front panels and the front rail. For a sofa, add the outside front, the deck under the cushions, the seat cushions (top, bottom and boxing strip each), the back cushions, and the skirt if it has one. Write the list down — the list is the estimate, and a missing panel is the reason most DIY estimates come up short.

Step two: measure each panel at its widest and longest point, following the curve, not the straight line. A seat that is 20 inches wide and 18 inches deep at the edges but 22 inches across the middle is measured as 22 by 18, because the fabric has to cover the widest point. Measure over the padding and around the roll of an arm — a rolled arm that looks 8 inches wide from the front can be 14 inches of fabric once the tape follows the curve over the top and down the outside.

Step three: add the allowance to every panel. Add 2 inches to each dimension for seam allowances (1 inch per side), and add a further 2 to 4 inches on any edge that tucks into the frame — the seat deck, the inside back and the inside arms all tuck in, and the tuck-in is what holds the fabric tight. A panel that measures 20 by 18 inches becomes 24 by 22 inches after a 2-inch seam allowance and a 2-inch tuck-in on two sides, and that extra fabric is not waste: it is the fabric the upholsterer pulls on to get the tension right.

Step four: convert the panels to yards. Lay the panels across the fabric width — 54 inches for standard upholstery fabric, 44 inches for dress-weight fabric — and add up the lengths. The shortcut for a rough total is to add up the area of all panels in square inches and divide by the area of one yard of fabric: one yard of 54-inch fabric is 54 by 36 inches, or 1,944 square inches, and one yard of 44-inch fabric is 1,584 square inches. Then add 10 percent for cutting waste, and 15 to 20 percent more if the fabric is patterned. The worked examples below show the method on real pieces, panel by panel.

Worked example 1: six dining chairs, seat and back

A set of six dining chairs with upholstered seats and backs is the most common first upholstery project, and it is the one where the table estimate and the panel method agree nicely — a good check that the method works. Each chair has two panels: a seat and a back. The seat measures 18 inches wide by 17 inches deep, and the back measures 18 inches wide by 20 inches tall. Adding 2 inches of seam allowance and 2 inches of tuck-in to each dimension gives a seat panel of 22 by 21 inches and a back panel of 22 by 24 inches. The two panels together are 462 plus 528 square inches, or 990 square inches per chair. Six chairs need 5,940 square inches, and dividing by 1,944 square inches per yard of 54-inch fabric gives 3.06 yards. Add 10 percent cutting waste and the order is 3.4 yards — buy 3.5 yards of plain fabric.

The table estimate says 1 to 1.5 yards per chair, or 6 to 9 yards for the set, which looks like a contradiction — and the difference is worth understanding, because it is the difference between fabric for the panels and fabric as it comes off the bolt. The panel method gives the area of the panels, but panels cannot be cut edge to edge: each panel needs a margin around it on the bolt, the panels are different shapes that do not tile perfectly, and a cut that starts mid-bolt leaves an offcut that is too small for the next panel. The trade estimate of 1 to 1.5 yards per chair already includes that layout waste, which is why it is the number to order by. The panel method is the check that the estimate is sane — if the panel area says 3 yards and the shop quotes 12, something is wrong; if it says 3 and the shop quotes 7.5, the difference is layout waste, and the shop is right. For this set, order 7.5 yards of plain fabric, or 9 yards if it is patterned with a repeat over 12 inches.

The cost follows. At $30 a yard, 7.5 yards of plain fabric is $225. Add new foam for six seats at about $8 a seat, or $48, batting at about $20, and staples, welt cord and thread at about $25, and the materials total is roughly $318. A professional would charge $75 to $150 per chair in labor for seats and backs, or $450 to $900 for the set, so the DIY saving on this project is the whole labor bill — the reason dining chairs are the standard first project. The fabric width guide explains why the 54-inch width matters here: on 44-inch fabric the same six chairs need about 20 percent more yardage, because each yard of fabric covers 1,584 square inches instead of 1,944.

Worked example 2: an armchair, plain and patterned

An armchair is the project where the panel method earns its keep, because the panels are large, curved and easy to underestimate. Take a standard club armchair: 34 inches wide, 36 inches deep, 34 inches tall, with a loose seat cushion. The panel list is the inside back (34 by 28 inches after allowances), the outside back (34 by 32), the seat deck (30 by 24), the two inside arms (24 by 20 each), the two outside arms (24 by 26 each), the two front arm panels (10 by 24 each), the front rail (34 by 8), and the seat cushion (top and bottom at 26 by 24 each, plus a boxing strip 4 inches wide by 96 inches around). Adding the areas: inside back 952, outside back 1,088, seat deck 720, inside arms 960, outside arms 1,248, front panels 480, front rail 272, cushion top and bottom 1,248, boxing 384 — a total of 7,352 square inches. Dividing by 1,944 gives 3.78 yards, and with 10 percent waste the panel area says 4.2 yards.

The trade estimate for an armchair is 5 to 7 yards, and again the gap is layout waste: the inside back and outside back are each 34 inches wide, which leaves a 20-inch strip beside them on a 54-inch bolt, and that strip is only wide enough for the front panels and the boxing, not for a full arm panel. The big panels each take a full width of the bolt for their length, so the chair consumes about 6 yards of bolt length even though its panels only cover 4.2 yards of area. Order 6 yards of plain fabric, and 7 yards if it is patterned — the pattern repeat is charged on the bolt length, not on the panel area, because every panel has to be cut at the right point in the repeat.

The patterned version shows the repeat charge clearly. A 27-inch repeat on 6 yards of bolt length means each panel's cut length is rounded up to a whole number of repeats, and the rounding is paid on every panel, not once per chair. The inside back, at 28 inches after allowances, rounds up to 54 inches — two repeats — and the outside back at 32 inches rounds up to 54 as well. The two back panels alone consume 108 inches of bolt length, or 3 yards, against 60 inches, or 1.67 yards, without the repeat. Across the whole chair the repeat adds about 1 to 1.5 yards, which is 15 to 25 percent, and at $35 a yard that is $35 to $53 extra. The pattern repeat guide works through the round-up formula on curtains, quilts and dresses, and the same rule applies here: a repeat under 12 inches adds about 10 percent, a repeat over 12 inches adds 15 to 20 percent, and a half-drop or directional print adds up to 25 percent.

The cost of the chair at $35 a yard is $210 for 6 yards of plain fabric, or $245 for 7 yards of patterned fabric. Add foam and batting at about $40, supplies at $30, and the materials total is roughly $280 to $315. Professional labor for an armchair runs $300 to $600, so a DIY reupholstery lands at about $300 against a $600 to $900 professional job — and against a new armchair of similar quality at $800 to $1,500, which is the comparison that decides whether the project is worth doing at all.

Worked example 3: a three-seat sofa, plain vs patterned

A three-seat sofa, 84 inches wide, is the biggest single-piece project most people attempt, and it is where an accurate estimate matters most, because the fabric bill alone is $400 to $600. The panel list is long: inside back (84 by 30 inches after allowances), outside back (84 by 34), seat deck (76 by 26), two inside arms (26 by 22 each), two outside arms (26 by 30 each), two front arm panels (12 by 26 each), front rail (84 by 10), three seat cushions (top and bottom at 28 by 26 each, boxing 4 by 104 each), and three back cushions (28 by 24 each, top and bottom). Adding the areas: inside back 2,520, outside back 2,856, seat deck 1,976, inside arms 1,144, outside arms 1,560, front panels 624, front rail 840, seat cushion tops and bottoms 4,368, seat boxing 1,248, back cushion tops and bottoms 4,032 — a total of 21,168 square inches. Dividing by 1,944 gives 10.9 yards of panel area, and with 10 percent waste that is 12 yards.

The trade estimate is 14 to 18 yards, and the layout waste is even larger here than on the chair, because the sofa's widest panels are 84 inches long — they take a full width of the bolt for 84 inches of length, and the 54-inch width leaves nothing beside them except narrow strips. The inside back, outside back and front rail are each a full-width cut; the seat deck and the cushion panels fill the next widths; and the arms, boxing and front panels are cut from the strips and offcuts. A plain sofa orders 16 yards, which is the middle of the trade range, and a patterned sofa with a 24-inch repeat orders 19 yards — the repeat adds about 3 yards, or just under 20 percent.

At $28 a yard for a mid-range upholstery fabric, 16 yards of plain fabric costs $448, and 19 yards of patterned fabric costs $532 — an $84 difference for the pattern. Add foam for three seat cushions at about $25 each, or $75, batting at $30, and supplies at $50, and the materials total is roughly $603 for the plain version and $687 for the patterned version. Professional labor for a three-seat sofa runs $800 to $1,500, so the all-in professional job is $1,400 to $2,200, and the DIY version is $600 to $700. The comparison that matters is against a new sofa: a new three-seat sofa of similar frame quality costs $1,500 to $3,000, so reupholstering a good frame — hardwood, eight-way hand-tied springs, no sag — saves real money, while reupholstering a cheap frame with a sagging deck does not, because the frame is the part you cannot fix with fabric.

Worked example 4: a set of four loose cushions

Loose cushions are the smallest upholstery project and the best way to learn the panel method, because every panel is a rectangle and the math is exact. Take four seat cushions, each 22 inches square and 5 inches thick, with a boxing strip around the edge and a zipper in the back. Each cushion has three panels: a top (22 by 22 inches), a bottom (22 by 22), and a boxing strip 6 inches wide — 5 inches of thickness plus a 1-inch seam allowance — by 88 inches around, which is four sides of 22 inches. After a 2-inch seam allowance on the top and bottom, each is 24 by 24 inches, or 576 square inches, so the top and bottom together are 1,152 square inches, and the boxing is 6 by 88, or 528 square inches. Each cushion is 1,680 square inches, and four cushions are 6,720 square inches. Dividing by 1,944 gives 3.46 yards, and with 10 percent waste the order is 3.8 yards — buy 4 yards of plain fabric, or 4.5 yards if it is patterned.

The table estimate says 0.75 yards per cushion, or 3 yards for four, and here the two methods nearly agree, because cushions tile well: two 24-inch panels fit side by side across a 54-inch width with only a 6-inch strip left over, and the boxing strips fill that strip exactly. That is the rule in miniature — square panels waste little, long panels waste a lot, and the more a piece looks like a rectangle, the closer the panel method lands to the trade estimate. At $30 a yard, four yards of fabric is $120, foam inserts at $20 each add $80, zippers and thread add about $20, and the set costs roughly $220 in materials — against $400 to $800 for four new custom cushions, and against $25 to $50 per cushion for a professional to make them from your fabric.

A useful rule is that the fabric should cost no more than the frame is worth: spending $900 on fabric for a $200 frame is money you will never see again, while spending $450 on fabric for a solid hardwood frame is an investment that lasts 15 years. And always ask the shop for the double-rub rating — the number of times the fabric can be rubbed before it wears through. A dining chair needs 15,000 double rubs, a family sofa needs 30,000, and a commercial seat needs 50,000 or more; a fabric that fails the rub test is the most expensive fabric you can buy, because you will buy it twice.

Fabric width: why 54 inches is the upholstery standard

Upholstery fabric is sold in 54-inch widths for a reason: most furniture panels are between 20 and 36 inches wide, so two panels fit side by side across a 54-inch bolt, and the widest common panel — a sofa back at 84 inches long — is cut along the length, not the width. Dress-weight fabric at 44 inches breaks that arithmetic. Two 24-inch cushion panels fit across 54 inches with 6 inches to spare; across 44 inches they do not fit at all, so each panel takes its own width of fabric, and the yardage jumps by roughly a quarter. The conversion is exact: multiply the 54-inch yardage by 54 divided by 44, or 1.23. A sofa that needs 16 yards of 54-inch fabric needs about 19.7 yards of 44-inch fabric — 3.7 extra yards, or about $110 at $30 a yard — and that is before the pattern repeat, which is charged on the longer bolt length as well. The fabric width guide works through the same 36, 44, 54 and 60 inch bolt widths on quilts, curtains and dresses, and the upholstery conclusion is the same: the wider the bolt, the less yardage you buy.

There is one more width rule that catches people out. Upholstery fabric is usually cut with the pattern running down the length of the bolt, and on a directional print — a stripe, a landscape, a row of animals — the panels must all run the same way up. That means a panel that would fit sideways across the bolt cannot be rotated to save space, and the yardage goes up by 15 to 25 percent on top of the repeat allowance. If you are choosing between two fabrics and one is directional, the non-directional one is cheaper to cut even at the same price per yard. And check the usable width, not the bolt width: a 54-inch bolt has about 53 usable inches after the selvage, and a 60-inch bolt has about 59. A panel that is 53 inches wide fits on a 54-inch bolt; a panel that is 54 inches wide does not, and it takes a whole extra width of fabric.

Pattern repeat on upholstery: the 15 to 20 percent rule

Patterned upholstery fabric pays the same repeat charge as patterned curtain or quilt fabric, and the charge is larger here because the panels are bigger and there are more of them. The rule from the pattern repeat guide carries over unchanged: round every cut length up to a whole number of repeats, add 15 to 20 percent to the plain-fabric estimate for a repeat over 12 inches, 10 percent for a repeat under 12 inches, and up to 25 percent for a half-drop or directional print. The repeat is printed on the end of the bolt and in the online listing, written as something like “V 24 in. / H 13.5 in.”, and it is worth reading before you fall in love with the print — a 27-inch repeat on a sofa adds 3 yards, or about $84 at $28 a yard, while a 12-inch repeat on the same sofa adds barely a yard.

One upholstery-specific detail: the repeat matters most on the panels people look at — the inside back, the inside arms and the seat cushions — and least on the panels nobody sees, which is why professionals cut the outside back, the deck and the dust cover from plain fabric even on a patterned job. The outside back of a sofa is 84 by 34 inches, or nearly 2 yards of fabric, and cutting it from the same patterned fabric as the front means paying the repeat charge on 2 yards that face the wall. Use a plain fabric in a matching color for the outside back, the bottom and the deck, and the patterned fabric goes only where the pattern shows. The saving on a sofa is 3 to 4 yards, or $84 to $112 at $28 a yard, and the finished piece looks identical from the front.

Frequently asked questions

How much fabric do I need to reupholster a sofa?

A three-seat sofa about 84 inches wide needs 14 to 18 yards of plain 54-inch upholstery fabric, and 17 to 21.5 yards if the fabric is patterned — add 15 to 20 percent for a repeat over 12 inches. A loveseat needs 10 to 12 yards plain, and a sectional needs 22 to 30 yards plain. Measure every panel and add 2 inches of seam allowance plus 2 to 4 inches of tuck-in per panel, or use the yardage table in this guide for a first estimate. The free Fabric Yardage Calculator runs the width-first math on your measurements.

How much fabric do I need for a dining chair?

A dining chair seat alone needs 0.5 to 0.75 yards, and a seat plus back needs 1 to 1.5 yards of plain 54-inch fabric. A set of six chairs with seats and backs orders about 7.5 yards plain, or 9 yards patterned. Fully upholstered side chairs need 2 to 3 yards each. The seat panel is measured at its widest point, with 2 inches of seam allowance and 2 inches of tuck-in added to each dimension.

How do you calculate upholstery yardage?

List every panel, measure each one at its widest and longest point following the curve, add 2 inches of seam allowance and 2 to 4 inches of tuck-in to each dimension, then add up the panel areas in square inches and divide by 1,944 — the area of one yard of 54-inch fabric. Add 10 percent for cutting waste and 15 to 20 percent more for a patterned fabric. The trade estimates in the yardage table already include layout waste, so order by those, and use the panel method as a check.

Is it cheaper to reupholster or buy new?

It depends on the frame. Reupholstering a three-seat sofa costs about $600 in materials DIY, or $1,400 to $2,100 with professional labor, against $1,500 to $3,000 for a new sofa of similar quality. Reupholstering wins when the frame is solid hardwood with good springs and no sag — that frame will last another 15 years. It loses on a cheap frame with a sagging deck, because the frame is the part fabric cannot fix.

How much extra fabric do I need for pattern repeat on upholstery?

Add 15 to 20 percent to the plain-fabric estimate for a pattern repeat over 12 inches, 10 percent for a smaller repeat, and up to 25 percent for a half-drop or directional print. The repeat is charged on every cut length separately, so a sofa with a 24-inch repeat needs about 3 extra yards over its 16-yard plain estimate. Use plain matching fabric for the outside back, deck and dust cover to avoid paying the repeat charge on fabric nobody sees.

What fabric width is best for upholstery?

54 inches is the upholstery standard, because most furniture panels fit two across a 54-inch bolt. Dress-weight fabric at 44 inches needs about 23 percent more yardage for the same piece — multiply the 54-inch estimate by 1.23. Check the usable width as well: a 54-inch bolt has about 53 usable inches after the selvage, so a panel wider than 53 inches takes a whole extra width of fabric.
