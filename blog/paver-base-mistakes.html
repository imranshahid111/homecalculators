The 7 Paver Base Mistakes That Make Patios Sink

Paver base mistakes are the reason most sinking patios fail — not bad pavers, not bad luck. A paver patio is only as good as what sits underneath it: the base carries every chair, grill and footstep, spreads the load so the ground does not rut, and drains water away before it can soften the soil. Get the base right and a patio stays flat for decades. Get any one of the seven mistakes below wrong and the surface starts to tell on you within a year or two — a low spot where the chairs wobble, a puddle that never drains, a corner that creeps outward a little every summer. The good news: every one of these mistakes is cheap to prevent and expensive to fix, which makes the base the highest-value part of the whole project to get right the first time.

The standard base, in 30 seconds

Before the mistakes, the baseline. A residential paver patio base is built in layers, from the ground up: excavated and compacted soil (the subgrade), a geotextile fabric separator, 4–6 inches of compacted crushed gravel laid in thin lifts, 1 inch of bedding sand screeded flat, then the pavers, then joint sand swept in and the whole surface compacted once more. Driveways need 6–8 inches of base because cars are heavier. Every mistake below is a deviation from this stack — and each one has a specific failure it produces, so you can diagnose an existing patio by its symptoms as well as prevent the problem on a new one.

Mistake 1: A base that is too thin

This is the single most common cause of a sinking patio, and it is almost always a money decision: gravel is heavy, hauling it costs money, and shaving an inch or two off the base looks like free savings. It is not. A 2-inch base over soft soil flexes under foot traffic and furniture; the gravel migrates sideways under load, and the pavers above follow it down. The rule is simple: 4 inches of compacted gravel is the minimum for a patio, 6 inches if your soil is clay or the patio sits in a low, wet spot, and driveways need 6–8 inches. For a 10×10 patio, the difference between a 2-inch base and a 4-inch base is roughly 0.6 cubic yards of gravel — about $30–$50 of material. That is the cheapest insurance in the project, and the fix for a thin base after the fact is lifting every paver and starting over.

Mistake 2: Dumping the gravel in one deep layer and never compacting it

Gravel does not compact itself, and a 6-inch pile compacted only from the top is loose in the middle. The correct method is lifts: spread no more than 2–3 inches at a time, wet it lightly, and run a plate compactor over it until it stops settling, then repeat. A plate compactor rents for roughly $50–$75 a day in 2026 and is the single most important tool on the job — hand tamping a patio base is a fantasy for anything bigger than a stepping-stone path. Skipping compaction shows up fast: the first rainy season settles the loose middle of the base, and the patio develops a shallow bowl right where people walk most. If you are hiring the work, ask the contractor how many lifts they run; "one and done" is a red flag worth walking away from.

Mistake 3: Using the wrong gravel

Not all gravel locks together. The base needs angular crushed stone with fines — typically called 3/4-inch minus or crusher run — because the sharp edges and the dust-sized fines interlock into a rigid slab when compacted. Rounded river rock or pea gravel does the opposite: smooth stones roll against each other, never lock, and the base shifts under load like a bag of marbles. The same goes for recycled concrete that is mostly dust with no stone in it — it turns to mud in a wet winter. Order by the right name at the yard ("3/4-inch minus crushed stone" or "crusher run"), and if the pile looks like beach pebbles, it is the wrong pile. For a 10×10 patio at 4 inches of compacted base, order about 1.7 tons of loose gravel — compaction eats 15–20% of loose volume, so never order the exact compacted number.

Mistake 4: Skipping the geotextile fabric

Geotextile fabric is a $30–$60 roll that most DIYers have never heard of, and it is the difference between a base that lasts and a base that slowly disappears. Without a separator, the gravel presses down into soft or clay soil over successive wet seasons, and soil pumps up into the gravel from below. Five years later the base is thinner than the day it was laid, and nobody can explain why the patio sank when the gravel depth was "right." The fabric goes directly on the compacted subgrade, under the gravel, with overlaps of at least 12 inches at seams. It is not landscape weed fabric — that thinner material tears under gravel. Buy woven geotextile rated for driveways and patios; it is sold at the same yards that sell the gravel.

Mistake 5: No slope, or slope toward the house

A patio must shed water, and the base — not just the pavers — sets the grade. The standard is a slope of 1/8 to 1/4 inch per foot away from the house: a 10-foot patio drops 1.25 to 2.5 inches from the house to the outer edge. A flat base ponds water, and standing water is what softens the subgrade and starts the sinking cycle; a base sloped toward the house sends that water at your foundation, which is worse. Screed the bedding sand to the same slope, and check it with a long level or a string line before a single paver goes down — regrading after the pavers are laid means lifting them all. While you are at it, never let the finished patio sit above the house's siding or below the weep holes; keep the surface at least 4 inches below the siding line.

Mistake 6: No edge restraint

Pavers are held in place by their neighbors and by the edge. Without a rigid edge restraint — plastic or aluminum paver edging spiked into the base, or a concrete haunch — the outer rows creep outward under foot traffic and thermal expansion, joints widen, and the whole field loosens from the edges inward. This is the mistake behind the classic symptom of a patio that looks fine in the middle but ragged at the borders. Edge restraint goes in before the final compaction, spiked every 12 inches or so into compacted base (not into loose soil), and it costs roughly $1–$2 per linear foot. A 10×10 patio needs 40 feet of it — under $80 — to lock the entire field. Skipping it saves almost nothing and guarantees the slow unraveling that makes patios look ten years old in three.

Mistake 7: Bedding sand too thick, or the wrong sand

The bedding layer is exactly 1 inch — no more. Thick bedding sand (2 inches or more) feels forgiving when you are leveling pavers, but it is the layer that ruts: foot traffic and furniture legs punch through soft, deep sand, and the pavers above dip one by one. Use coarse concrete sand, not fine mason sand and never stone dust for bedding; fine sand holds water and migrates, stone dust turns to cement-like paste when wet and traps water under the pavers. Screed the 1-inch layer with pipes or rails, lay the pavers without walking on the screeded sand, and never use sand to "fix" a low spot in the base — fix the base, then screed. The same discipline applies to joint sand: sweep polymeric sand in dry, compact, sweep again, and mist it lightly to activate — flooding it washes the binder out before it sets.

What a sinking patio costs to fix

Prevention is measured in tens of dollars; repair is measured in days. Lifting and relaying a sunken section means pulling the pavers, regrading and recompacting the base, re-screeding and relaying — at contractor rates of roughly $10–$20 per square foot for relay work in 2026, a 100-square-foot repair runs $1,000–$2,000, and a full relay approaches the cost of a new patio. Compare that to the prevention budget for the same 10×10 patio:

Frequently asked questions

Why do paver patios sink?

Almost always because of the base, not the pavers: a base that is too thin, never compacted in lifts, built from rounded gravel that cannot lock together, or laid without geotextile fabric so it slowly sinks into soft soil. Standing water from a flat or wrongly sloped base accelerates all of it. The pavers themselves rarely fail.

How thick should a paver base be?

4–6 inches of compacted crushed gravel for a residential patio (6 inches on clay or wet soil), plus 1 inch of bedding sand. Driveways need 6–8 inches of base because vehicles are far heavier than foot traffic. Anything under 4 inches on a patio is a future repair bill, not a saving.

Can you fix a sinking paver patio without removing all the pavers?

Only for small, isolated dips: pull the affected pavers, add and compact base material in the low spot, re-screed and relay. But if the whole patio is sinking, the base itself is the problem — spot fixes will keep sinking — and a full lift-and-relay is the honest repair.

Do I need geotextile fabric under a paver patio?

Yes, on any soil softer than firm gravel — especially clay. The fabric separates the gravel base from the soil so the base cannot sink into mud over wet seasons. It costs $30–$60 for a 10×10 patio and is the cheapest long-term protection in the project. Use woven geotextile, not thin weed fabric.

What gravel is best for a paver base?

3/4-inch minus crushed stone (crusher run): angular stone with fines that interlock into a rigid layer when compacted in 2–3 inch lifts. Avoid rounded river rock and pea gravel — smooth stones roll and never lock — and avoid material that is mostly dust, which turns to mud when wet.

How much slope should a paver patio have?

1/8 to 1/4 inch per foot away from the house — a 10-foot patio drops 1.25 to 2.5 inches from house to edge. The base sets the grade, so slope the gravel and screed the bedding sand to the same fall before laying any pavers, and keep the finished surface at least 4 inches below the siding.
