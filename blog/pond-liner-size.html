Pond Liner Size: The Formula and the Overlap Rule

Pond liner size is the one measurement you cannot fix after the fact. Order a liner too small and the pond is unbuildable — there is no stretching a sheet of EPDM, no seam you can add in the middle of a hole, and no refund that covers the weekend you lose re-digging. Order too big and you pay for rubber that ends up folded under the edging stones, which is money wasted but at least a pond that holds water. This guide gives you the formula that gets the size right the first time, the overlap rule that keeps the edge dry, worked examples for the pond sizes people actually build, and the cost of the liner and the underlayment underneath it.

The short answer: the liner formula

The formula for pond liner size is the same for every rectangular pond, and it has only three terms:

Liner length = pond length + (2 × maximum depth) + (2 × overlap)
Liner width = pond width + (2 × maximum depth) + (2 × overlap)

Overlap is the extra liner that runs past the pond's edge and lies flat on the ground, where it is held down by edging stones or buried in a shallow trench. The standard overlap is 12 inches — one foot — on every side, which means 2 feet added to each dimension. With the standard 12-inch overlap, the formula shortens to the version most builders carry in their heads: liner length = pond length + (2 × depth) + 2 feet, and liner width = pond width + (2 × depth) + 2 feet.

Run it once for a common pond and the numbers stop feeling abstract. A pond 12 feet long, 8 feet wide and 3 feet deep needs a liner 12 + 6 + 2 = 20 feet long and 8 + 6 + 2 = 16 feet wide — a 20 × 16 foot liner, 320 square feet of rubber. A small pond 8 feet by 6 feet and 2 feet deep needs 8 + 4 + 2 = 14 feet by 6 + 4 + 2 = 12 feet — a 14 × 12 foot liner, 168 square feet. And a large pond 16 feet by 12 feet and 4 feet deep needs 16 + 8 + 2 = 26 feet by 12 + 8 + 2 = 22 feet — a 26 × 22 foot liner, 572 square feet. Depth is the term that surprises people: every extra foot of depth adds 2 feet to both dimensions, so a pond that is one foot deeper needs a liner that is 2 feet longer and 2 feet wider, and the square footage climbs fast.

Before you measure anything, settle the pond's dimensions with honest numbers. The length and width are the maximum measurements at the waterline, not the average and not the size of the hole at the bottom, which will be smaller once the sides slope in. The depth is the maximum depth, measured from the waterline to the deepest point, not the average depth you would use for a volume calculation. If you have not worked out the volume yet — and the volume decides the pump, the fish and the treatment dose — use the free Pond Volume Calculator to get the gallons from length, width and average depth; the measuring method for irregular shapes is worked through in How Many Gallons Is My Pond? Why each part of the formula exists

Every term in the formula earns its place, and understanding why makes the common mistakes obvious. The pond length and width are the span the liner has to cover across the bottom. The two depth terms are the liner climbing up each side of the pond: from the bottom to the waterline on the left, and from the bottom to the waterline on the right. A 3-foot-deep pond needs 6 feet of liner just to go down one wall and up the other, before a single inch of flat bottom is covered. People who forget one of the depth terms — who add the depth once instead of twice — end up with a liner that reaches the edge on one side and falls a foot short on the other, and the pond is unusable.

The overlap terms are the insurance. A liner that ends exactly at the waterline has nothing holding it in place and nothing stopping water from wicking over the edge: rain running off the lawn, a wave from a waterfall, or a heavy-footed heron can all push water behind the liner, and once water gets behind it the liner floats, the edge collapses, and the pond loses level. Twelve inches of flat liner under the edging stones gives the liner enough grip to stay put and enough margin that a small shift in the ground does not expose the edge. The overlap is also what the edging hides: the raw cut edge of a liner is never meant to be seen, and 12 inches gives you room to tuck it under the first course of stones and still have liner left if a stone settles.

The third reason for the overlap is that ponds are never exactly the shape you drew. The hole ends up a few inches wider here, the shelf a few inches deeper there, and a liner ordered to the exact formula with no overlap has zero tolerance for any of it. The 2 feet the overlap adds to each dimension is the only slack in the whole build, and experienced builders treat it as the minimum, not a luxury. If the site is uneven, the soil is loose, or the pond has a waterfall that runs into it — all of which push and pull on the liner — 18 inches of overlap instead of 12 is cheap protection, adding only 1 foot to each dimension.

Worked example 1: a small 8 × 6 ft garden pond, 2 ft deep

Take the most common first pond: 8 feet long, 6 feet wide, 2 feet deep, with a simple shelf around the edge for marginal plants. The formula gives a liner 8 + 4 + 2 = 14 feet long and 6 + 4 + 2 = 12 feet wide — 14 × 12 feet, 168 square feet.

The next question is what size to actually buy, because liners are sold in standard sizes and 14 × 12 is not one of them. The standard sizes run in steps — 10 × 10, 10 × 15, 15 × 15, 15 × 20, 20 × 20, 20 × 25 and so on — so the rule is to buy the next size up in each dimension: a 15 × 15 foot liner for this pond. The extra foot on the length and the extra 3 feet on the width cost a little more and fold neatly under the edging, and they are the difference between a liner that fits with slack and one that is a foot short because the hole came out 6 inches wider than planned. A 15 × 15 foot EPDM liner at 45 mil runs about $150 to $200, and the underlayment underneath it — a geotextile fabric that stops roots and sharp stones from puncturing the rubber — adds about $40 to $60 for the same size.

The shelf changes the math slightly, and it is worth understanding before you dig. A shelf is a ledge cut into the side of the pond, typically 12 inches wide and 12 inches below the waterline, where potted plants sit. The liner has to travel down to the shelf, across it, and down again to the bottom, which adds roughly 2 feet to the liner dimension on the side with the shelf — one foot down, one foot across — but only if the shelf runs the full length of that side. A shelf on one side of this pond adds about 1 foot to the liner width in practice, because the shelf's horizontal run is roughly cancelled by the shorter vertical drop on that side. The honest way to handle shelves is to add 1 foot to the liner dimension for each full-length shelf, measure after digging, and buy the next standard size up. Guessing at the shelf and buying exactly to the formula is how a liner ends up short by a foot on the shelf side.

Worked example 2: a 12 × 8 ft koi pond, 3 ft deep

Now the pond most koi keepers build: 12 feet by 8 feet, 3 feet deep, with a shelf on one long side and a waterfall at one end. The base formula gives 12 + 6 + 2 = 20 feet long and 8 + 6 + 2 = 16 feet wide — a 20 × 16 foot liner, 320 square feet. Add 1 foot to the width for the shelf and the target becomes 20 × 17 feet, and the next standard size up is a 20 × 20 foot liner, 400 square feet.

The waterfall deserves its own piece of liner, and it is the one most often forgotten. Water falling from a waterfall box into the pond runs over a stream or a rock slope, and every inch of that run needs liner underneath it, lapped over the pond liner so the water flows downhill from one sheet to the other. A waterfall that drops 2 feet over a 4-foot run needs roughly a 5 × 4 foot piece of liner — the run length plus the drop plus overlap — and that piece is usually cut from the spare liner left over after the main pond is lined, which is one more reason to buy the next size up rather than the exact formula size. If the main liner leaves no spare, a small offcut piece in the same material costs $30 to $60, and it should be ordered with the main liner so the materials match.

The cost for this pond breaks down into three lines. A 20 × 20 foot, 45-mil EPDM liner costs about $350 to $450. The geotextile underlayment in the same size costs about $80 to $120. Edging — the stones or pavers that hold the overlap down — runs about $150 to $300 depending on whether you use fieldstone you already have or buy cut stone. The total liner job lands between $580 and $870, and the liner itself is roughly 60% of it. The stocking that this pond supports — four adult koi by the 250-gallon rule, since a 12 × 8 × 3 foot pond holds about 2,150 gallons — is worked through in How Many Koi Can Your Pond Really Hold?, and the pump that turns it over once an hour is sized in Pond Pump Sizing: GPH, Head Height and Turnover Explained.

Worked example 3: a round pond and an irregular pond

Round ponds use the same formula with one substitution: the diameter replaces both the length and the width, and the liner is a square, not a circle. A round pond 10 feet across and 3 feet deep needs a liner 10 + 6 + 2 = 18 feet on each side — an 18 × 18 foot square, 324 square feet. The corners of the square fold into pleats around the pond's edge, and those pleats are normal: fold them flat, point them downhill away from the water, and bury them under the edging. A round pond wastes more liner in the corners than a rectangular pond of the same volume, which is one of the quiet costs of a round shape — about 15 to 20% more liner per gallon of water.

Irregular ponds — kidney shapes, freeform curves, ponds that follow a garden bed — use the maximum length and the maximum width as if the pond were a rectangle, then add the same depth and overlap terms. A kidney pond that measures 14 feet at its longest and 10 feet at its widest, 3 feet deep, needs a liner 14 + 6 + 2 = 22 feet by 10 + 6 + 2 = 18 feet — a 22 × 18 foot liner, and the next standard size up, 25 × 20 feet, 500 square feet. The rectangular-bounding-box method overestimates the liner for an irregular pond by design: the curves cut away corners of the bounding rectangle, so the spare liner folds into pleats at the tight curves. That spare is not waste — it is the slack that lets the liner follow a curve without pulling tight across it, and a liner pulled tight across a curve is a liner that will tear at the fold in the first freeze.

For any pond that is not a clean rectangle, measure twice: once with a tape across the longest points, and once by laying a rope along the ground following the pond's outline, then measuring the rope. The rope method catches the extra length a curve adds that a straight tape misses. If the two methods disagree by more than a foot, trust the rope, and buy the next size up. A liner that is a foot too big costs $30 or $40 extra. A liner that is a foot too small costs the whole pond.

Two patterns in the table are worth noticing. First, the buy size is always bigger than the formula size, and the gap grows with the pond: a small pond jumps one standard size, a large pond can jump two, because standard sizes come in 5-foot steps and the formula rarely lands on one. Second, the gallons column is the average-depth volume, not the maximum-depth volume — a pond's average depth is usually about 75% of its maximum depth once the sides slope, which is why a 12 × 8 × 3 foot pond holds about 2,150 gallons instead of the 2,880 a flat-bottomed box would hold. That distinction is exactly why the liner formula uses maximum depth and the volume calculation uses average depth, and mixing the two up is the most common arithmetic error in pond building.

The overlap rule: 12 inches, and when to go bigger

Twelve inches of overlap is the standard because it is the smallest amount that reliably holds a liner down under edging stones and survives a season of ground movement. The overlap works in two ways: the weight of the edging presses the liner flat against the ground, and the friction of 12 inches of rubber on soil resists the pull of the water inside the pond. Shorten the overlap to 6 inches and both effects weaken — the stones sit on less liner, the friction area halves, and a heavy rain or a settling stone can pull the edge free. The liner then slips toward the pond, the water level drops an inch, and the edge of the pond starts to show.

Three situations call for more than 12 inches. The first is a steep-sided pond, where the liner is pulled tight by the weight of the water and the slope gives the overlap less purchase — go to 18 inches. The second is loose or sandy soil, which shifts under the edging and lets the liner creep — go to 18 inches and bury the overlap in a shallow trench 6 inches deep rather than laying it flat. The third is a pond with a waterfall or a stream feeding into it, where the moving water constantly tugs at the liner near the inlet — go to 18 inches on the waterfall side, and lap the waterfall liner over the pond liner by at least 12 inches so the joint sheds water downhill instead of catching it.

The anchoring trench is the upgrade that makes a big overlap worth having. Instead of laying the overlap flat on the ground and piling stones on it, dig a trench 6 to 8 inches deep and 6 inches wide about 12 inches back from the pond's edge, run the liner down into the trench and back up the far side, then backfill with soil and set the edging stones on top. The trench locks the liner mechanically — it cannot slip without lifting the backfilled soil — and it hides the cut edge completely. A trench costs an hour of digging and changes the overlap from a friction hold to a buried anchor. On any pond over 2,000 gallons, or any pond with fish worth more than the liner, it is the right call.

Liner materials compared: EPDM, PVC, RPE and HDPE

The liner material decides the price per square foot, the lifespan, and how forgiving the liner is to install. The table below compares the four materials sold for garden ponds at typical US prices in 2026. EPDM is the default for a reason; the others are the right choice only in specific situations.

HDPE30 to 40 mil$0.50 to $0.8020+ yearsLarge, square ponds; very stiff, best where the shape is simple

EPDM's advantage is flexibility. A 45-mil EPDM liner drapes into corners, folds into neat pleats, stretches a little where the ground settles, and survives a freeze without cracking. It is also the easiest to repair: a puncture takes a patch and a tube of seam adhesive, and the repair lasts as long as the liner. Its disadvantages are weight — a 20 × 20 foot sheet weighs about 90 pounds and needs two people to lay — and price, roughly double PVC per square foot. For a pond you plan to keep for a decade or more, and especially for a pond with fish, EPDM is the material most builders recommend first, and the extra cost is small next to the cost of rebuilding a pond around a failed liner.

PVC is the budget option, and its limits are real. It is stiffer than EPDM, so it pleats poorly in tight curves and can crack in a hard freeze if the pond is drained. It is also vulnerable to UV damage if the edge is exposed to sun, which is another reason the overlap and the edging matter — exposed PVC degrades in a few seasons. PVC makes sense for a small ornamental pond under 500 gallons, a temporary pond, or a project where the liner will be replaced within a decade anyway. It does not make sense for a koi pond, where a liner failure costs the fish.

RPE and HDPE are the large-pond materials. RPE is a woven polyethylene with a coating, and it is remarkably strong for its weight — a 25 × 25 foot RPE liner weighs about half what the same size in EPDM weighs, which matters when two people are hauling it into a hole. Its stiffness is the trade-off: it does not drape, it pleats in wide, stubborn folds, and it suits large, simple shapes better than small, curvy ones. HDPE is similar but stiffer still, and it is mostly used for large commercial or farm ponds where the shape is a simple rectangle. For a backyard pond under 5,000 gallons, EPDM is almost always the better install; for a pond over 10,000 gallons, the weight saving of RPE starts to matter.

The comparison that matters is the cost of getting the size wrong. A liner one size too small cannot be used at all, and the replacement costs the full price of the right liner plus the return shipping on the wrong one — typically $60 to $120 in freight for a heavy EPDM sheet, and a week of delay. A liner one size too big costs the price difference between the two sizes, usually $40 to $100, and the extra rubber folds harmlessly under the edging. The asymmetric cost of the two errors is the whole argument for the next-size-up rule: overbuying is cheap, underbuying is expensive, and the formula plus one standard size up is the cheapest way to be sure.

The five liner-size mistakes, ranked

MistakeWhat happensHow to avoid it

Skipping the overlapEdge slips, water wicks behind the liner, level drops12 inches minimum on every side; 18 on steep or sandy sites

The first mistake deserves emphasis because it is the one people make even when they know the formula. The depth term feels like it should be added once — the pond is 3 feet deep, so add 3 feet — but the liner travels the depth twice, down and up, and the missing 3 feet shows up as a liner that reaches the waterline on one side and hangs 3 feet short of it on the other. The pond cannot be filled past the short side, which means the pond is effectively 3 feet narrower than planned and the deep end is unusable. There is no patch for a liner that is a whole depth short. Measure the depth twice, add it twice, and write the arithmetic down before ordering.

Frequently asked questions

What size pond liner do I need?

Use the formula: liner length = pond length + (2 × maximum depth) + 2 feet of overlap, and liner width = pond width + (2 × maximum depth) + 2 feet. A 12 × 8 ft pond that is 3 ft deep needs a 20 × 16 ft liner by the formula, and you should buy the next standard size up — 20 × 20 ft — so the hole has slack and the waterfall has spare liner.

How much overlap should a pond liner have?

Twelve inches on every side is the standard, giving 2 feet total added to each dimension. Go to 18 inches on steep-sided ponds, loose or sandy soil, or the waterfall side, and consider a shallow anchoring trench 6 to 8 inches deep to lock the overlap mechanically instead of relying on the weight of the edging stones alone.

Should I buy the exact liner size or the next size up?

Always the next standard size up in each dimension. Liners are sold in 5-foot steps, so the formula size almost never matches a stock size. The extra rubber costs $40 to $100 and folds under the edging; a liner one size too small cannot be used at all and costs the full replacement price plus return freight.

How do I size a liner for a round pond?

Use the diameter in place of both length and width: liner size = diameter + (2 × depth) + 2 feet, as a square. A 10 ft round pond 3 ft deep needs an 18 × 18 ft square liner; buy 20 × 20 ft. The corners fold into pleats around the edge — fold them flat, point them downhill, and bury them under the edging.

Do I need underlayment under a pond liner?

Yes, on almost every soil. A geotextile underlayment costs about $0.25 per square foot — roughly a quarter of the liner — and stops sharp stones, roots and burrowing animals from puncturing the rubber. The only time to skip it is a pond dug in clean, stone-free sand with no roots nearby, and even then the saving is small next to the cost of a puncture.

What is the best pond liner material?

EPDM rubber at 45 mil is the default for most ponds: flexible, UV-stable, fish-safe, repairable with a patch kit, and good for 20+ years at about $0.85 to $1.20 per square foot. PVC is cheaper but stiffer and shorter-lived; RPE is lighter and stronger for large ponds but harder to pleat. For a koi pond, EPDM is the material most builders recommend first.
