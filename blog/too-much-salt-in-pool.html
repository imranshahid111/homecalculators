Too Much Salt in the Pool? How to Fix It Safely

Too much salt in the pool is the one saltwater-pool mistake with no shortcut. Salt does not evaporate, no chemical neutralizes it, and no filter removes it — once it is dissolved in the water, the only way out is to take water out with it. The good news is that the fix is simple arithmetic and a garden hose: drain a calculated share of the pool, refill with fresh water, and the salt level drops in exact proportion. The bad news is that most owners discover the overshoot late, after the salt cell has already shut itself down on a high-salt fault, the water tastes like the sea, and the extra salt has been quietly working on metal fixtures. This guide covers how to confirm the overshoot before you drain a single gallon, the exact drain-and-refill math for your pool size, what the fix costs in water and time, and the three mistakes that turn a small overshoot into a big one.

How a pool ends up over-salted

Salt leaves a pool only with water — splash-out, backwash, leaks and overflow. Nothing else removes it, so the salt level is a one-way ratchet: every bag you add stays until water leaves. That is why overshooting is so common. The single biggest cause is dosing on a wrong number. A salt cell that reads low — because of scale on its plates, cold water, or a worn sensor — talks its owner into adding salt the pool never needed, and the same mistake repeated at the next "low" reading stacks the overshoot higher. The full story of that trap, including the 200 ppm rule for trusting the display, is in Why Your Salt Cell Reads Low (and When to Trust It).

The second cause is dosing before the last dose has mixed. Salt poured into a pool takes about 24 hours of circulation to fully dissolve and distribute, and a test taken too soon reads low — so the owner adds more, and a day later the pool is over target by the full second dose. The third cause is simple arithmetic done in the wrong direction: guessing the pool's volume instead of measuring it. A pool listed as 20,000 gallons that actually holds 16,000 gets 25% more salt than intended on every dose, and the error compounds across a season. The fourth cause is the honest accident — a bag tipped in by a helper, a child "helping" with the salt, or a delivery of the wrong product, such as water-softener salt with additives, dosed at pool-salt rates. And the fifth is a cell that was already over target when the owner took over: a new house, a new pool service, or a pool opened after a winter of nobody testing anything. Whatever the cause, the fix is the same, and it starts with a number you can trust.

First: confirm the overshoot with a real test

Do not drain water on the strength of a display reading. Salt cells measure salt by conductivity, not by a chemical test, and their readings drift — including upward. A display that says 4,500 ppm while the water tests at 3,200 ppm is a sensor problem, not an overshoot, and draining on that number wastes water for nothing. Test the water with a liquid drop test kit, which is accurate to about 200 ppm, or take a sample to a pool store for a free photometer reading. Take the sample from elbow depth, away from the return jets, and wait at least 24 hours after the last salt addition so the water is fully mixed. If the kit and the display disagree by more than 200 ppm, trust the kit.

While you are testing, read the other numbers too. High salt is rarely alone: check pH, calcium hardness and stabilizer in the same visit, because a pool that is over-salted after a season of guessing is often also out of balance in other ways, and you will be rebalancing everything after the refill anyway. Also check the cell itself. Most salt chlorinators shut down or flash a high-salt fault somewhere between 4,000 and 6,000 ppm, depending on the model — check your manual for the exact threshold. A cell that has shut down is not broken; it is protecting itself, and it will come back online once the salt drops back into range. Note the symptoms too, because they help you judge severity: water that tastes distinctly salty (most people notice it around 4,500–5,000 ppm), stinging eyes that were not a problem before, a white crust forming faster than usual on the waterline tile, and any metal — ladder rails, light niches, heater parts — showing fresh corrosion. None of these is an emergency on its own, but together they mean the overshoot has been there a while and the fix should not wait for the weekend.

Two caveats on this table. First, the target is not the same for every system: most residential cells want 3,200 ppm, but some ask for 3,000 and a few for 3,500 — always dose to your own manual's number, not to a generic one. Second, the "mildly high" band is the one owners most often ignore, and it is the one that costs the most over time: a pool sitting at 3,800 ppm for a whole season does not trigger any alarm, but the extra salt is quietly accelerating corrosion on every metal part in the water, and the next bag of salt — added because the display said "low" — pushes it over the cell's shutdown threshold without warning.

The drain-and-refill math (the only fix that works)

The math is a straight proportion, because salt mixes evenly through the water. If you drain a fraction of the pool and refill with fresh water (which has essentially zero salt), the salt level falls by that same fraction. The formula is: percentage to drain = (current ppm − target ppm) ÷ current ppm × 100. So a pool at 4,500 ppm with a 3,200 ppm target needs (4,500 − 3,200) ÷ 4,500 × 100 = about 29% drained and refilled. A pool at 3,800 ppm needs (3,800 − 3,200) ÷ 3,800 × 100 = about 16%. And a pool at 6,000 ppm needs (6,000 − 3,200) ÷ 6,000 × 100 = about 47% — nearly half the pool.

The table below runs that formula across the overshoot levels owners actually see, for a 3,200 ppm target. Read your current ppm from the test kit, find the row, and you have your drain percentage. One warning about the formula: it assumes the fresh water you refill with has no salt in it. Municipal tap water is effectively salt-free for this purpose, but well water can carry a few hundred ppm of dissolved salts — if you are on a well, test the fill water once; if it reads 300 ppm, your target after dilution is not 3,200 but the blend, and the drain percentage has to be a little larger.

If the overshoot is large — over about 40% — consider doing it in two rounds instead of one. Drain 25%, refill, circulate for 24 hours, retest, then drain the remainder. Two rounds use the same total amount of water, but the retest in the middle catches a wrong volume estimate or a test error before you have drained half the pool on a bad number. It also keeps the water level safely above the skimmer and the pump intake at every step, which matters for the next section.

Step by step: how to lower salt safely

Step 1 — Turn the salt cell off. Switch the chlorinator off at the control panel before you start. A cell running in over-salted water keeps producing chlorine while you are trying to fix the salt, and running a cell with the water level dropping risks exposing the plates to air, which damages them. Keep it off until the refill is complete and the water has circulated for a full day.

Step 2 — Drain to the calculated level. Use the pool pump on waste, a submersible pump, or a siphon — whichever you have. Measure the drop, don't guess it: mark the waterline with tape before you start and measure the drop in inches, then convert using your pool's gallons-per-inch (a 20,000 gallon pool that is 16 ft by 32 ft holds roughly 600 gallons per inch of depth). Stop at the calculated percentage. Never drain a pool below the skimmer opening, and never drain an inground pool more than about a third of its depth in one go without checking with a pool professional — hydrostatic pressure from groundwater can lift or crack an empty or nearly empty shell, and that risk is real in areas with a high water table. If your overshoot needs more than a 40% drain, do it in two rounds as described above, and if it needs more than half the pool, talk to a pool company first.

Step 3 — Refill with fresh water. Refill to the normal operating level with the garden hose or the auto-fill. Keep the pump running while you refill so the fresh water mixes instead of layering on top of the salty water — salt water is denser than fresh, so a still refill can leave the salty layer sitting at the bottom and your next test reading low at the surface. Let the pump run for a full 24 hours after the refill before you test.

Step 4 — Retest and restart. Test the salt with the kit again. If it is within 200 ppm of target, switch the cell back on and let it run its normal cycle. If it is still high, repeat the calculation with the new reading and drain the remaining percentage — the second round is usually small. Then rebalance the rest of the chemistry: the fresh water diluted everything, not just the salt, so check pH, alkalinity, calcium hardness and stabilizer, and adjust in that order. Finally, check the salt again a week later. A reading that has crept back up means salt is still being added somewhere — a salt feeder left on, a helper topping up, or a misread display — and the real fix is finding that source, not draining again.

Step 5 — Inspect what the salt may have damaged. While the water is down, look at the parts the salt was working on: ladder rails and handrails for pitting, the light niche screws for rust, the heater's heat exchanger if you can see it, and the cell plates themselves for scale. A short overshoot rarely damages anything permanently, but a season at 5,000 ppm can, and catching it now is cheaper than replacing a heater next year.

What the fix costs: worked examples

The cost of fixing an overshoot is mostly water, and water is cheap compared with the salt and the cell it protects. US municipal water typically costs $0.005–$0.01 per gallon, though some districts charge more, so the table below uses $0.008 per gallon as a middle figure and shows the same 20,000 gallon pool at four overshoot levels. The "salt wasted" column is the value of the salt that has to leave with the drained water — a 40-pound bag of pool salt costs roughly $7–$12 — and the "time" column assumes a garden hose filling at about 9 gallons per minute, which is typical for a standard outdoor spigot.

Compare those numbers with the cost of not fixing it. A salt cell that shuts down on high salt leaves the pool with no chlorine production, so the owner buys liquid chlorine at $4–$6 a gallon while the cell sits idle — $25–$40 a week for a typical pool. A heater heat exchanger corroded by a season of over-salted water costs $300–$800 to replace. And a cell that runs for months in water above its rated salt range scales faster and wears its plates out early, pulling a $500–$900 replacement forward by a year or two. The $18–$75 of water is the cheapest line in the whole comparison, which is why the right move is to drain promptly instead of waiting for splash-out and backwash to fix it slowly — that passive route takes months at the mild end and never catches up at the severe end, because backwash removes only a few hundred gallons at a time.

What does NOT lower salt (myths worth ignoring)

"Just add a chemical to neutralize it." There is no chemical that removes dissolved salt from pool water. Products sold as "salt reducers" are either clarifiers that do nothing to the salt level or, worse, add more dissolved solids to water that already has too many. The only removal mechanism is dilution.

"Run the filter longer — the filter will catch it." Salt is dissolved, not suspended. A sand, cartridge or DE filter catches particles; dissolved sodium chloride passes straight through, every time, no matter how long the pump runs. Running the pump longer only mixes the water — useful after a refill, useless as a fix on its own.

"Let the pool overflow in the rain." Rain dilutes the pool, and heavy rain followed by overflow does lower the salt — but by a trivial amount. An inch of rain on a 20,000 gallon pool adds about 1,250 gallons of fresh water, which is only a 6% dilution, and most of that rain runs off the deck rather than into the pool. Waiting for rain is not a plan.

"Backwash more often." A backwash dumps 200–500 gallons, which is a 1–2% dilution per backwash. You would need twenty backwashes to fix a moderate overshoot, and backwashing that often wastes water and throws the filter out of balance. Backwash on the pressure gauge, not on the salt reading.

"Drain it all and start over." A full drain is almost never needed and is the riskiest option on this list: an empty inground pool can float, crack or pop out of the ground in areas with high groundwater, and a full refill wastes the entire water cost plus a full rebalance. Partial dilution is safer, cheaper, and just as effective.

"Add more chlorine to balance it." Chlorine and salt are different chemicals that do different jobs. Adding chlorine to a high-salt pool does nothing to the salt level, and it can push the free chlorine far above target while the real problem — the salt — stays exactly where it was. Fix the salt first, then rebalance the chlorine.

How to stop it happening again

Prevention is a routine, not a purchase. First, dose only on a test-kit reading, never on the display alone, and wait 24 hours after adding salt before testing again — the two rules from How Much Salt to Add to a Pool: The PPM Guide that would have prevented most overshoots. For the exact pounds and bags for your pool, use the free Pool Salt Calculator: enter your gallons and your current and target ppm, and it gives you the dose before you open a bag. Second, know your pool's real volume. If you have never measured it, the volume is the first number to get right, because every dose is calculated from it — the free Pond Volume Calculator works from length, width and depth and is a quick way to check whether the number you have been using is anywhere near the truth. Third, write the numbers down: date, kit reading, bags added, display reading. A log is what turns "I think I added two bags last month" into a record that shows the overshoot building, and it is also the evidence a warranty claim needs if a drifting sensor is the cause. Fourth, clean the cell on schedule — scale is the main reason displays read low and owners over-dose, and the cleaning routine is in Saltwater Pool Maintenance Schedule: Weekly to Yearly. And fifth, check the salt monthly even when nothing seems wrong. A monthly test catches a 200 ppm drift while it is still a $10 fix; a test only when the cell alarms catches a 2,000 ppm overshoot when it is a $75 drain and a week of manual chlorination.

Frequently asked questions

Can too much salt in a pool damage the salt cell?

Yes, in two ways. Most cells shut down on a high-salt fault above about 4,000 to 6,000 ppm, so the pool loses its chlorine production until the salt comes down. And running for months in water above the rated range makes the cell scale faster and wears the plates out early, pulling a $500 to $900 replacement forward by a year or two.

How do I lower salt in my pool fast?

Drain and refill — it is the only method that works. Drain the percentage from the formula (current minus target, divided by current, times 100), refill with fresh water, circulate for 24 hours, and retest. Salt does not evaporate and no chemical removes it, so there is no faster route.

How much water do I need to drain to lower pool salt?

It depends on how high the salt is. For a 3,200 ppm target, a pool at 4,000 ppm needs about 20% drained, at 4,500 ppm about 29%, and at 6,000 ppm about 47%. For a 20,000 gallon pool that is roughly 4,000, 5,800 and 9,400 gallons. Always confirm the level with a test kit before draining.

Will rain lower the salt level in my pool?

Only by a trivial amount. An inch of rain adds about 1,250 gallons to a 20,000 gallon pool, roughly a 6% dilution, and much of it runs off the deck. Rain helps at the margins but cannot fix a real overshoot — drain and refill is the fix.

Is it safe to swim in a pool with too much salt?

At moderately high levels it is uncomfortable rather than dangerous — salty taste, stinging eyes, dry skin — but above about 5,500 ppm the water can irritate eyes and skin more noticeably, and metal fixtures corrode faster. The bigger risk is that the salt cell has shut down, so the pool may also be under-chlorinated; check the free chlorine before swimming.

Can I add salt right after draining to fix an overshoot?

No — never add salt until you have retested after the refill and 24 hours of circulation. The fresh water needs time to mix, and a test taken too soon reads low, which is exactly the mistake that caused the overshoot in the first place. Dose only on a settled test-kit reading.
