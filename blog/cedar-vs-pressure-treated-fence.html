Cedar vs Pressure-Treated Pine Fence: An Honest Comparison

Cedar vs pressure-treated fence is the first decision in any wood fence project, and it is also the one most often made on a single number — the price per board — that turns out to be the least important number in the whole project. The honest summary: cedar costs roughly 50–80% more up front, looks better, needs less chemical protection, and lasts 20–30 years with modest care; pressure-treated pine costs less, is stronger and heavier, resists ground contact and insects through chemistry rather than nature, and lasts 15–20 years — or much longer if you maintain it. Neither wood is the "cheap" option once you count maintenance and replacement, and neither is the "premium" option in every climate. This guide compares them head to head on cost, lifespan, looks, maintenance, strength, climate and environmental impact, with worked budgets for a real 100 ft fence, so you can pick the wood that fits your yard, your climate and your tolerance for upkeep.

What each wood actually is

Cedar fencing is almost always western red cedar, a naturally durable softwood grown in the Pacific Northwest and western Canada. Its durability is not added in a factory: the heartwood contains natural compounds (thujaplicins) that resist decay fungi and repel insects, which is why a cedar fence can stand untreated for years without rotting. Cedar is also light — about two-thirds the weight of pressure-treated pine — straight-grained, and dimensionally stable, meaning it shrinks, swells and warps less with seasonal moisture changes than most woods. Its weakness is softness: cedar dents and scratches easily, splits if fasteners are driven too close to an edge, and its natural durability lives in the heartwood, not the paler sapwood, so boards with a lot of sapwood rot faster than the premium boards they sit next to.

Pressure-treated pine is usually southern yellow pine that has been kiln-dried, placed in a pressure vessel, and forced full of preservative chemicals — today most commonly micronized copper azole (MCA) or alkaline copper quaternary (ACQ) — that protect it from fungi and insects. The treatment, not the species, is what makes it last: untreated pine at ground contact rots in a few years, while treated pine posts rated for ground contact routinely last 20 years or more. Treated pine is dense, heavy, strong and widely available, and it is the cheapest fencing lumber in most of the country. Its weaknesses are cosmetic and practical rather than structural: it arrives wet from treatment, so it shrinks, twists and checks (surface cracks) as it dries; the greenish tint fades unevenly; and the copper in the treatment corrodes ordinary steel fasteners, so you must use hot-dip galvanized or stainless hardware or watch screws rust and stain the boards within a season.

Cost comparison: materials and installed, for a 100 ft fence

Boards are where the price gap shows up first. In 2026 typical US retail, a 6 ft cedar picket runs roughly $4–$7, while a comparable pressure-treated pine picket runs $2–$4. Rails and posts follow the same pattern: cedar posts cost more, and cedar rails are often bought in higher grades to avoid the warping that cheap cedar is prone to. But pickets are only part of a fence — posts, rails, concrete, hardware and labor are largely the same for both woods, which is why the installed gap is narrower than the board gap. On a 6 ft privacy fence, cedar runs roughly $45–$85 per linear foot installed, against $30–$60 for pressure-treated pine. That is a real gap, but it is a 50% premium, not a doubling — and it is the gap you should compare against lifespan and maintenance, not against the price of a single board.

Two details hide inside those ranges. First, grade matters more for cedar than for pine: a cedar fence built from cheap, sapwood-heavy, knotty boards costs nearly as much as a good one once you replace the boards that rot or split early, so the "cheap cedar" quote is often the most expensive fence you can buy. Buy cedar by grade — clear or select-tight-knot heartwood for boards that face the weather — and check boards in the yard for sapwood streaks and large loose knots. Second, treated pine's real price includes stainless or hot-dip galvanized fasteners, which cost roughly twice what bright steel screws cost; skipping them saves perhaps $40 on a 100 ft fence and buys rust streaks on every board. Price the fence with the hardware it actually needs, for either wood.

Lifespan: what 20 years really means for each wood

A well-built cedar privacy fence lasts 20–30 years; pressure-treated pine lasts 15–20 years as a fence, though its ground-contact posts often outlast the boards they hold. Those ranges hide an important asymmetry: cedar ages gracefully and fails slowly — boards weather, silver, and eventually thin and crack, but the fence rarely collapses — while treated pine tends to fail at its weakest link, which is usually the boards' fasteners or a post that was installed with the wrong treatment rating. A cedar fence at year 20 usually looks like an old fence; a treated pine fence at year 20 usually looks like a fence with several replaced boards and a couple of leaning posts. Both can reach 25 years, but cedar gets there with less drama.

Maintenance is what moves a fence from the bottom of its range to the top. Cedar kept clean and sealed every 2–3 years holds its color and resists surface cracking; cedar left completely alone still lasts, but it silvers within a year or two and its surface becomes rougher, which is a look many owners like and some hate. Treated pine needs the same sealing schedule — arguably more, because its surface checks more aggressively as it dries — and it needs its stain or sealer to be applied only after it has dried out from treatment, typically 3–6 months after installation. Staining treated pine too early traps moisture under the finish and causes peeling; this is the single most common treated-pine maintenance mistake, and it is entirely avoidable by waiting for the wood to dry and testing a board with a sprinkle of water (if it beads, wait; if it soaks in, stain).

Looks: the part you live with every day

Cedar wins on looks, and it is not close. Fresh cedar is a warm reddish-brown with fine, even grain; it takes stain beautifully and evenly, and when left unstained it weathers to a soft silver-gray that suits natural and modern landscapes alike. Treated pine starts greenish from the treatment, fades to a blotchy yellow-brown, and takes stain unevenly because the treatment chemicals and the wood's density vary from board to board — two boards from the same bundle can stain two different shades. If you plan to paint your fence, the difference mostly disappears (both woods paint well once primed, though cedar's stability means fewer paint cracks), but if you plan to stain it or leave it natural, cedar is worth real money for appearance alone. This is the reason cedar dominates front-yard and high-visibility fences while treated pine dominates back-lot and budget fences: the wood you see every day from your kitchen window earns its premium faster than the wood along the back property line.

One appearance trap for cedar: sapwood. Cedar sapwood is pale yellow-white and weathers poorly — it is the part of a cedar board that rots first and stains unevenly. A fence built from boards that are half sapwood will look patchy within a year, with pale streaks next to rich brown. When you buy cedar, look at the board ends and faces: heartwood is the reddish-brown part, sapwood the pale part, and the best fence boards are mostly heartwood. Suppliers grade for this, and the price difference between a sapwood-heavy bundle and a heartwood bundle is the difference between a fence that looks expensive and one that looks like a mistake.

Strength, weight and workability

Pressure-treated pine is the stronger, heavier wood: it holds fasteners better, resists impact (a kicked ball, a leaning ladder, a dog jumping against it) better, and its posts are stiffer under wind load. Cedar is lighter and easier to work — a crew can carry and hang cedar boards all day with less fatigue, and DIY builders notice the difference on a 200 ft fence — but it dents under the same impacts that pine shrugs off, and its screws need pre-drilling near board ends to avoid splitting. For posts, the strength argument flips in an interesting way: a treated pine post rated for ground contact is the standard, durable choice, and many builders use treated posts even under a cedar fence, because cedar posts in ground contact rot faster than cedar boards above ground. The hybrid — treated posts, cedar boards and rails — is the most common professional build in the country, and it exists because each wood is used where it is best.

Warping and stability favor cedar overall, but with a caveat. Cedar moves less with moisture, so a cedar fence stays flatter and its gates stay truer. Treated pine, installed wet, moves a lot as it dries: boards can cup, twist and bow in the first year, and a gate built from wet treated pine can sag out of square before its first birthday. The fix for pine is to let it dry before building where possible, to buy kiln-dried-after-treatment (KDAT) lumber where available, and to use screws rather than nails so boards can be re-tightened. The fix for cedar is simpler: buy good grades and keep boards dry on site until installation. Either way, a fence built from boards that were stored flat, dry and stickered (spaced so air circulates) will outperform the same boards stored in a wet pile — storage is a free quality upgrade.

Climate verdict: which wood wins where you live

Climate is the tiebreaker when cost and looks are close. In wet climates — the Pacific Northwest, the Gulf Coast, the humid Southeast — cedar's natural decay resistance and dimensional stability shine, but treated pine's ground-contact rating matters most at the posts, which sit in wet soil year-round; the hybrid build (treated posts, cedar above ground) is the standard answer there. In dry climates — the Southwest, the High Plains — both woods last longer than their national averages, because decay fungi need moisture; cedar's stability advantage shrinks, treated pine's checking (surface cracking from fast drying) grows, and the decision tilts toward whichever wood is cheaper locally. In termite-heavy regions, treated pine's chemical protection is a genuine advantage: cedar resists termites reasonably well, but it is not termite-proof, and a treated post is the safer bet where termites are active.

The regional price twist: cedar is cheapest near its source (the Pacific Northwest) and most expensive in the Southeast, where treated southern pine is a local product and cedar is shipped in. In the Southeast, the installed gap can stretch to nearly double; in the Northwest, it can shrink to 20–30%. Before deciding on principle, price both woods at two local suppliers — a fence quote built on national averages can be wrong by thousands in either direction, and the local price is the only one that matters.

The lifetime cost math: 25 years, one 100 ft fence

Up-front price favors pine by roughly $1,500–$2,500 installed on a 100 ft fence. But a fence is not a one-time purchase. Over 25 years, cedar typically needs sealing every 2–3 years (8–10 applications) and a few board replacements; treated pine needs the same sealing schedule plus more board and fastener replacements, and it is more likely to need a partial rebuild — a section of posts or a run of boards — before year 25. Priced at DIY sealing costs ($150–$300 per application on 100 ft, including stain) and contractor board replacement, the 25-year totals converge: cedar lands around $6,500–$11,500, treated pine around $5,500–$10,500, and in wet climates the order can flip, because pine's earlier partial rebuild erases its up-front savings. The honest conclusion: pine is cheaper over a lifetime, but by hundreds, not thousands — and if you value the way cedar looks, that gap is the price of a feature, not a waste.

The cheapest lifetime fence is usually the hybrid: treated posts and rails where strength and ground contact matter, cedar pickets where looks matter, stainless or hot-dip galvanized fasteners throughout. It costs roughly halfway between all-pine and all-cedar, lasts nearly as long as all-cedar, and looks like cedar from the street — which is the only side most people see. If your budget forces a choice, spend the cedar money on the boards facing the street and the front yard, and use treated pine on the back and side runs nobody photographs. A fence does not have to be one wood all the way around.

Maintenance schedules side by side

Year one differs: cedar can be sealed within weeks of installation, once it is dry to the touch; treated pine must dry 3–6 months first, and staining early is the classic mistake. After that, both woods want cleaning (a gentle wash, never a high-pressure blast that furrows the grain) and resealing every 2–3 years, with the sunny side resealed more often than the shaded side — a fence's south and west faces age roughly twice as fast as its north face. Both woods want vegetation kept off them: vines and shrubs trap moisture against boards and accelerate decay in either species, and soil or mulch piled against the bottom board rots cedar and overwhelms treated pine's protection alike. Keep the bottom board 2 inches above soil and mulch, keep sprinklers from hitting the fence daily, and either wood will reward you with years past its expected life.

The mistakes that decide the outcome

First, buying cedar by price alone and getting sapwood-heavy boards that rot and stain patchily. Second, staining treated pine before it dries, then blaming the stain when it peels. Third, using bright steel or electro-galvanized fasteners with treated pine — the copper treatment eats them, and the rust streaks are permanent. Fourth, putting cedar posts directly in the ground without a treated post or a concrete footing detail that sheds water; cedar posts are the weakest link in an otherwise durable fence. Fifth, burying the bottom board in soil or mulch, which rots either wood from the bottom up. Sixth, skipping the sealer entirely on the theory that "cedar doesn't need it" — it doesn't need it to survive, but it needs it to keep looking like the fence you paid for. Every one of these is a purchasing or first-year decision, not a property of the wood, which is why two fences of the same species can have wildly different lives.

Frequently asked questions

Is cedar or pressure-treated pine better for a fence?

Cedar is better for looks, stability and natural decay resistance, and it lasts longer above ground. Pressure-treated pine is better for strength, ground contact, termite areas and budget. The most common professional build mixes them: treated posts, cedar boards and rails.

How much more does a cedar fence cost than pressure-treated?

About 50–80% more for boards, but only about 50% more installed: roughly $45–$85 per linear foot for cedar versus $30–$60 for pressure-treated pine on a 6 ft privacy fence, because posts, rails, concrete and labor cost the same either way.

How long does a cedar fence last vs pressure-treated pine?

A well-built cedar fence lasts 20–30 years; pressure-treated pine lasts 15–20 years as a fence, though ground-contact treated posts often last longer than the boards. Sealing every 2–3 years moves either wood toward the top of its range.

Can you mix cedar and pressure-treated pine in one fence?

Yes, and it is the standard professional approach: pressure-treated posts for ground contact and strength, cedar pickets and rails for looks and stability, with hot-dip galvanized or stainless fasteners throughout.

Does pressure-treated pine need to dry before staining?

Yes — wait 3–6 months after installation, until a sprinkle of water soaks into the board instead of beading. Staining wet treated pine traps moisture and causes peeling. Cedar can be sealed much sooner, once it is dry to the touch.

Is pressure-treated wood safe for a garden fence?

Modern treatments (MCA and ACQ) replaced the older arsenic-based CCA for residential use in 2004. Current guidance is to avoid using treated lumber where it directly contacts soil growing food crops, and to use hot-dip galvanized or stainless fasteners, since the copper treatment corrodes ordinary steel.
