Roof Pitch Chart: Every Pitch in Degrees and Percent

A roof pitch chart is the translator between the three languages roofers, architects, and building codes use to describe the same slope. Your contractor says "6/12." The shingle manufacturer's spec sheet says "26.6 degrees minimum." The building code says "33 percent slope." All three are the same roof — and if you can't convert between them, you can't check a quote, buy the right material, or size the roof area that drives your price. This guide gives you the complete chart for every pitch from 1/12 to 18/12 — degrees, percent, rise per foot, and the rafter-length factor — plus the worked conversions and cost math that make the numbers useful instead of decorative.

Three numbers, one slope: how to read the chart

Roof pitch is always a ratio of rise over run, written as X/12: the roof rises X inches for every 12 inches of horizontal run. That is the number roofers use, and it is the first column of the chart. The other two columns are the same slope expressed differently, and each one shows up somewhere you will need it.

Degrees is the angle the roof surface makes with horizontal. It is what architects draw, what some manufacturers print on spec sheets, and what a digital level or phone app measures when you lay it on a rafter. The conversion is a tangent: degrees = arctan(rise ÷ run). For a 6/12 pitch, that is arctan(6 ÷ 12) = arctan(0.5) = 26.57 degrees.

Percent slope is rise divided by run, times 100 — the number building codes and drainage specs use, and the same "percent grade" you see on road signs. A 6/12 pitch is 6 ÷ 12 × 100 = 50 percent. This one is the easiest to misread: 100 percent slope is not a vertical wall, it is a 45-degree roof (12/12). A truly vertical wall would be infinite percent.

The fourth column in the chart, the rafter factor, is the one that prices the job. For every foot of horizontal run, the rafter is longer than a foot by this factor — because the rafter runs diagonally. Multiply your horizontal run by the factor and you get rafter length; multiply your roof's footprint area by the same factor and you get the actual roof surface area that shingles, underlayment, and labor are priced on. A 6/12 roof has a factor of 1.118, so a roof covering a 1,000 sq ft footprint is really about 1,118 sq ft of roofing.

Worked example 1: converting between pitch, degrees, and percent

Say your architect's drawing calls for a 30-degree roof, but your roofer quotes in X/12 and the shingle warranty talks about percent slope. One slope, three conversions — all from the tangent.

Degrees to pitch: pitch rise = 12 × tan(degrees). tan(30°) = 0.577, so 12 × 0.577 = 6.93 inches of rise per 12 inches of run. That is a 7/12 pitch for all practical purposes (6.93 rounds to 7, and lumber and framing are not cut to hundredths of an inch).

Degrees to percent: percent = tan(degrees) × 100. tan(30°) × 100 = 57.7 percent. Check it against the chart: 7/12 is 58.3 percent — the half-percent difference is rounding from calling 6.93 a 7.

Pitch to degrees: the other direction is arctan(rise ÷ run). A 5/12 pitch: arctan(5 ÷ 12) = arctan(0.4167) = 22.6 degrees. A 9/12 pitch: arctan(0.75) = 36.9 degrees.

Percent to pitch: divide the percent by 100, multiply by 12. A code that requires a minimum 25 percent slope means 0.25 × 12 = 3/12. A 50 percent requirement is 0.50 × 12 = 6/12. This is the conversion that catches people out most often, because a "25 percent slope" sounds gentle and a "3/12 roof" sounds low — and both are the same roof.

Worked example 2: using the chart to size a real roof

Take a simple gable roof over a 24-foot-wide house at a 6/12 pitch — the most common residential pitch in the chart, and the most common roof in the country. The chart gives you everything in three multiplications.

Ridge height: the horizontal run is half the span, 12 feet. Rise = run × (pitch rise ÷ 12) = 12 ft × 0.5 = 6 feet from the wall plate to the ridge. That is the number that decides whether your attic is a crawl space or a usable room — and whether the ridge clears your local height limit.

Rafter length: the chart's rafter factor for 6/12 is 1.118. Rafter length = 12 ft × 1.118 = 13.42 feet per side, before the eave overhang. Add a 12-inch overhang and the rafter stock is about 14.6 feet — so you order 16-foot lumber, not 14-foot.

Roof surface area: say the house is 40 feet long. Each slope is 13.42 ft of rafter × 40 ft of length = 536.8 sq ft. Two slopes = 1,073.6 sq ft of roofing surface, before overhangs. The footprint is only 24 × 40 = 960 sq ft, so the pitch alone added 113.6 sq ft — about 12 percent — of shingles, underlayment, and labor that a flat-footprint estimate would miss.

Now run the same house at 12/12. Rise becomes 12 feet (a two-story attic), rafter length 12 × 1.414 = 16.97 feet, and each slope 678.8 sq ft — 1,357.6 sq ft total, 41 percent more roofing than the footprint. Same house, same footprint, 284 more square feet of roofing than the 6/12 version. That is the chart's rafter factor doing its quiet work on your budget, and it is why roofers price by roof squares (100 sq ft of surface), never by the house's footprint.

Minimum pitch by roofing material

The chart tells you what a pitch is. The material tells you whether that pitch is allowed. Water runs downhill, and below a certain slope it stops running fast enough to stay out of seams — so every roofing material has a minimum pitch, and going below it voids the warranty even if the roof looks fine on day one.

The practical rule: if your roof is 3/12 or below, you are choosing between metal and membrane, not between shingle colors. If it is 4/12 or above, every common material is on the table and the decision becomes cost, looks, and lifespan. And if your existing roof is 2/12 with shingles on it, the shingles are not the problem — the pitch is, and the next roof needs either a membrane system or a reframed slope.

How pitch changes your roofing cost

Pitch moves a roofing quote three ways, and only the first one is obvious.

1. More surface area. The rafter factor in the chart is a direct price multiplier on materials. At 4/12 the factor is 1.054; at 6/12 it is 1.118; at 9/12 it is 1.250; at 12/12 it is 1.414. On a 1,200 sq ft footprint at $450 per roofing square (100 sq ft) installed, the 4/12 roof costs about $5,690 in surface-area terms, the 6/12 about $6,040, the 9/12 about $6,750, and the 12/12 about $7,640 — the same house, a $1,950 spread driven by nothing but slope.

2. More labor per square. Roofers walk a 4/12 or 5/12 roof comfortably. At 6/12 they slow down; at 7/12 and 8/12 they start using roof jacks and staging; at 9/12 and above, many crews add a steep-slope surcharge of 10–25 percent on labor, plus the time cost of harness work and slower material handling. A 12/12 roof can cost 30–50 percent more in labor per square than a 5/12 roof of the same area.

3. More waste and accessories. Steep roofs need more starter shingles, more ridge cap (the ridge is the same length, but hip and valley cuts multiply on complex steep roofs), and more safety equipment — and every cut shingle on a steep pitch is a shingle that slides, so waste factors creep from the standard 10 percent toward 15 percent. None of this shows up in a per-square material price, which is why two quotes for the same roof can differ by thousands: one priced the footprint, the other priced the slope.

When you compare quotes, ask each roofer for the roof squares they priced, not the house square footage. If one quote's squares match your footprint and the other's match footprint × rafter factor, the cheaper quote is the one that under-measured — and the change order will find you later. How to measure your roof's pitch (so you can use the chart)

The chart is only useful if you know your pitch, and measuring it takes two minutes with a level and a tape measure. From inside the attic, hold a 12-inch level horizontally against the underside of a rafter, mark the 12-inch point, and measure straight down from that mark to the rafter — the vertical distance in inches is your rise, and your pitch is rise/12. A 6-inch drop means 6/12.

From the ground, measure the run from the eave to the ridge line horizontally and the rise from the eave height to the ridge height, then divide: pitch = (rise ÷ run) × 12. Or use a phone level app on a rafter or the roof surface itself — most report degrees directly, and the chart above converts degrees back to pitch. A reading of 26.6 degrees is 6/12; 33.7 degrees is 8/12; 18.4 degrees is 4/12.

One caution: measure on a rafter, not on the roof surface over shingles — shingle thickness and uneven courses can tilt a level by a degree or two, enough to move you a full pitch step. And measure in two places: if the two readings disagree, the roof has settled or the framing varies, and the roofer needs to know before the quote is final.

Choosing a pitch: what the chart says about your climate

Pitch is not just a style choice — it is a climate decision, and the chart's steep end exists because snow and rain demand it.

Heavy snow country (the upper Midwest, New England, the Rockies) favors 7/12 to 10/12. Snow slides off a 30-degree-plus slope instead of sitting, freezing, and forming ice dams at the eaves. A 4/12 roof in snow country holds snow all winter, and every freeze-thaw cycle pushes meltwater under the shingles at the eave line.

Hot, dry climates (the Southwest, southern California) can use almost anything — 3/12 to 5/12 is common, because there is little rain to shed and a low roof keeps the house cooler by reducing the sun-facing surface. Tile, the regional standard, still wants 4/12 minimum.

High-wind and hurricane zones (Florida, the Gulf Coast) favor lower pitches — 4/12 to 6/12 — because a steep roof is a bigger sail. Building codes in these zones often require specific fastening patterns that matter more than pitch, but the wind load on a 12/12 gable end is dramatically higher than on a 5/12.

Heavy rain regions (the Pacific Northwest, the Southeast) want 5/12 or steeper so water clears fast and doesn't linger in valleys and behind chimneys. This is also where the material minimums bite hardest: a 3/12 roof in a rainy climate is a leak waiting for a storm, even with perfect installation.

The bottom line: pick the pitch for your weather first, your style second, and your budget third — because the chart's cost curve punishes the wrong choice twice, once at installation and once at the first bad winter.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common roof pitch?

6/12 — 26.6 degrees, 50 percent slope — is the most common pitch on US homes built in the last 40 years. It sheds rain and snow well, is still walkable for roofers, and keeps the roof area only about 12 percent above the footprint. 4/12 and 5/12 are the next most common, especially on ranch homes.

How do I convert roof pitch to degrees?

Degrees = arctan(rise ÷ run). For a 6/12 pitch: arctan(6 ÷ 12) = arctan(0.5) = 26.6 degrees. For 4/12 it is 18.4 degrees, and for 12/12 it is exactly 45 degrees. The chart above lists degrees for every pitch from 1/12 to 18/12.

What pitch is a 30-degree roof?

A 30-degree roof is about a 7/12 pitch. tan(30°) = 0.577, so 12 × 0.577 = 6.93 inches of rise per foot of run, which rounds to 7/12 (30.3 degrees, 58.3 percent slope).

What is a 4/12 pitch in percent?

A 4/12 pitch is a 33.3 percent slope: 4 ÷ 12 × 100 = 33.3%. Percent slope is the number building codes use most often, and it is easy to misread — 100 percent slope is a 45-degree (12/12) roof, not a vertical wall.

What is the minimum roof pitch for shingles?

Asphalt shingles need a minimum 4/12 pitch for a standard installation. They can go down to 2/12 only with a low-slope installation — double underlayment or full ice-and-water shield over the deck — and below 2/12 you need metal or membrane roofing instead. Going below the minimum voids the shingle warranty.

Does a steeper roof cost more?

Yes, three ways: more surface area (a 12/12 roof has 41 percent more roofing than its footprint, vs 12 percent for a 6/12), more labor per square (steep-slope surcharges of 10–25 percent, plus staging and harness work), and more waste and accessories. On a 1,200 sq ft footprint, the same house costs roughly $1,950 more to roof at 12/12 than at 4/12 on surface area alone.
