Pond Pump Sizing: GPH, Head Height and Turnover Explained

This pond pump size guide answers the question every pond owner asks before buying a pump: how many gallons per hour do I actually need? The short answer is that the pump must move your whole pond through the filter once every one to two hours, and the number on the box is not the number you get — a pump rated at 2,000 GPH can deliver half that once it is pushing water up to a waterfall. The two numbers that decide the purchase are your pond's real volume in gallons and the total head height the pump works against, and both are easy to get wrong in the same direction: too small. This guide works through the turnover rule, the head-height math, how to read a pump curve, a sizing table for ponds from 500 to 10,000 gallons, and the running-cost math that matters more than the price tag over the pump's life.

The short answer: the turnover rule

Pond pump sizing starts with one rule: the pump should circulate the pond's entire volume through the filter once every one to two hours. That is the turnover rate, and it is the standard every pond builder and filter maker works to. A pond that turns over once an hour gets its water filtered 24 times a day. A pond that turns over once every two hours gets 12 passes a day. Both are acceptable; the faster rate is better for ponds with fish, and the slower rate is fine for a lightly stocked water garden.

The formula is simple. Divide the pond's volume in gallons by the turnover time in hours, and the answer is the flow you need in gallons per hour, measured at the head height the pump actually works against — not the number printed on the box. A 1,500-gallon pond turned over once an hour needs 1,500 GPH of real flow. Turned over once every two hours, it needs 750 GPH. A 5,000-gallon pond needs 5,000 GPH for hourly turnover and 2,500 GPH for two-hourly turnover. The arithmetic is trivial. The hard part is that both inputs — the volume and the head height — are usually wrong, and both errors point the same way.

The volume error is the one covered in the pond volume guide: most owners overestimate their pond by 30 to 50% because they use the maximum depth instead of the average depth. That error actually pushes the pump size up, which is the safe direction. The head-height error pushes the other way. A pump's rated GPH is measured at zero head — water flowing out at the same level as the pump, with no lift and no pipe friction. The moment the pump has to lift water to a waterfall or push it through 30 feet of hose, the flow drops, sometimes by half. Buying on the box number without accounting for head is how a pond ends up with a pump that moves 900 GPH when the pond needs 1,500.

Before any of this math means anything, you need an honest gallon number. If you have not measured your pond yet, the volume comes first — use the free Pond Volume Calculator to get the gallons from length, width and average depth, or work through the measuring method in How Many Gallons Is My Pond? Measuring Irregular Ponds. Every number in this guide is built on that gallon figure, and a 30% volume error becomes a 30% pump error.

Head height: the number that halves your pump

Head height is the total resistance the pump works against, expressed in feet of water column. It has two parts: static head and friction head. Static head is the vertical distance from the pond's water surface to the highest point the water is delivered — usually the top of the waterfall or the filter outlet. Friction head is the resistance of the pipe or hose itself: every foot of pipe, every elbow, every valve and every foot of lift through a narrow hose steals a little flow. Add the two together and you have the total head the pump sees.

Static head is easy to measure. Hold a tape from the water surface straight up to the waterfall lip and read the number. If the waterfall is 3 feet above the pond surface, the static head is 3 feet. If the filter sits on a bank 4 feet above the water and the waterfall drops from there, the static head is 4 feet plus whatever the waterfall adds. The common mistake is measuring from the bottom of the pond instead of the surface. The pump sits at the bottom, but the water it is pushing starts at the surface — the lift is surface to outlet, not floor to outlet. Measuring from the floor adds the pond's depth to the head, which overstates it by 2 or 3 feet and pushes you toward a bigger pump than you need. Overstating head is the safe direction, but it costs money.

Friction head is where the guessing starts, because it depends on the pipe. A useful rule of thumb: a straight run of hose costs about 1 foot of head for every 10 feet of length, and each 90-degree elbow costs about 1 foot. So 30 feet of hose with two elbows adds roughly 3 + 2 = 5 feet of friction head on top of the static lift. That rule assumes a hose diameter matched to the pump — a 1.5-inch pump pushing through a 1-inch hose loses far more, because the water has to squeeze through a smaller opening, and the friction climbs steeply as the hose narrows. The single cheapest sizing mistake is buying a big pump and strangling it with a thin hose; the hose, not the pump, becomes the bottleneck.

Work it through for a typical pond. The pond is 1,500 gallons. The waterfall lip is 3 feet above the surface. The hose run is 25 feet with two elbows, and the water passes through a pressurized filter before the waterfall. Static head is 3 feet. Friction is about 2.5 feet for the hose, 2 feet for the elbows, and about 2 feet for the filter — roughly 6.5 feet of friction, for a total head of about 9 to 10 feet. That is the number the pump curve has to be read at, and it is three times the waterfall height alone. Anyone who sized this pond on "3 feet of head" would buy a pump that delivers barely half the flow they need.

There is a shortcut if the arithmetic feels fussy: add 50% to the waterfall height as a rough allowance for friction on a normal setup, and use that as the head for sizing. A 3-foot waterfall becomes 4.5 feet of head; a 5-foot waterfall becomes 7.5 feet. It is not precise, but it is far closer than using the waterfall height alone, and it keeps you on the safe side without oversizing wildly. For a long hose run or a filter and UV in series, do the full addition above instead — the shortcut understates those cases.

Reading a pump curve: rated GPH vs real GPH

Every pond pump has a performance curve, usually printed on the box or the manufacturer's page as a small table: flow at 0 feet of head, at 3 feet, at 5 feet, at 10 feet, and the maximum head where flow stops entirely. The headline number — the one in the product name, like "2,000 GPH pump" — is always the zero-head figure. It is the flow the pump delivers with no lift and no pipe at all, which is a condition that never exists in a real pond. The curve is the truth; the headline is the advertisement.

Here is a realistic curve for a mid-range submersible pump sold as 2,000 GPH. At 0 feet of head it moves 2,000 GPH. At 3 feet it moves about 1,700. At 5 feet, about 1,450. At 10 feet, about 900. At 13 feet — its maximum head — it moves nothing at all. The flow falls by more than half between zero head and 10 feet, and the fall accelerates as the head approaches the maximum. That shape is typical: the first few feet cost a little, the last few feet cost a lot.

The practical rule that follows from the curve: never buy a pump whose maximum head is close to your total head. If your total head is 10 feet, a pump with a 13-foot maximum is running at the edge of its range, where a slightly dirty filter or a kinked hose can push it over the edge and cut the flow to a trickle. Buy a pump whose maximum head is at least 1.5 times your total head, and whose flow at your total head meets the turnover number. In the example above, the 1,500-gallon pond with 10 feet of total head needs 1,500 GPH of real flow for hourly turnover — the "2,000 GPH" pump delivers only 900 at 10 feet, so it fails, while the "3,000 GPH" pump delivers about 1,500 and just makes it. The pond needs a pump sold as 3,000 GPH to actually move 1,500 GPH. That is the single most surprising fact in pond pump sizing, and it is the reason so many ponds are under-pumped.

One more curve habit worth building: check the curve at your head before you buy, not after. Manufacturers publish the curves; retailers often do not show them on the product page, and the headline number is all most buyers ever see. If a listing has no curve, treat the headline number as the zero-head figure and assume you will lose 30 to 50% of it at a normal waterfall height. That assumption is usually close enough to keep you out of trouble, and it is far better than assuming the box number is what the pond gets.

The sizing table: what to buy for your pond

The table below gives the target flow and the pump rating to shop for, for the pond sizes people most often own. The "flow needed" columns are the turnover math: volume divided by 1 hour and by 2 hours. The "pump to buy" column is the box rating to look for, assuming a typical total head of about 5 feet — a 3-foot waterfall plus normal hose and filter friction — where a pump delivers roughly 70% of its rated flow. If your total head is higher, around 10 feet, the pump delivers closer to 50% of its rating, so move one row up: buy the pump listed for the next-larger pond.

Two things about the table are worth spelling out. First, the 2-hour turnover column is the minimum, not the target, for a pond with fish. Koi produce far more waste than a water garden, and the standard advice for a koi pond is hourly turnover at minimum, with many keepers aiming for one and a half times the volume per hour. A 2,500-gallon koi pond sized on the 2-hour column — a pump moving 1,250 GPH — will run green and cloudy in summer, because the filter only sees the water 12 times a day and the fish are adding waste faster than that. Size koi ponds on the 1-hour column, and size lightly stocked water gardens on the 2-hour column.

Second, the "pump to buy" column already includes the head correction. It is the box rating, not the delivered flow. A 1,500-gallon pond with a 3-foot waterfall needs 1,500 GPH of real flow for hourly turnover, and the pump that delivers it at 5 feet of total head is rated around 2,200 to 2,500 GPH. If you buy a pump rated 1,500 GPH for that pond, you get about 1,050 GPH at 5 feet of head — a 2-hour turnover at best, and a green pond in July. The gap between the box number and the delivered number is the whole game, and the table prices it in.

Worked example 1: a 1,200-gallon koi pond

Take a garden pond that holds 1,200 gallons — measured properly, with the average-depth method, so the number is trusted. It has four small koi, a 2-foot waterfall, a 20-foot hose run with one elbow, and a pressurized filter. The owner wants hourly turnover because of the fish, so the target flow is 1,200 GPH of real flow.

Head first. Static head is 2 feet, the waterfall height. Friction is about 2 feet for the hose, 1 foot for the elbow, and about 2 feet for the filter — roughly 5 feet of friction, for a total head of about 7 feet. Now the curve: a pump rated 2,000 GPH delivers roughly 1,200 to 1,300 GPH at 7 feet of head on a typical curve, which just meets the target. A pump rated 1,500 GPH delivers about 950 at 7 feet and misses by 20%. So the buy is a pump rated around 2,000 GPH, and the hose should be at least 1.5 inches — the diameter the pump's outlet expects — because dropping to a 1-inch hose on a 20-foot run would add several feet of friction and undo the sizing.

Check the running cost while you are at it. A 2,000 GPH submersible typically draws 120 to 150 watts. At 135 watts, running 24 hours a day, it uses 0.135 × 24 × 365 = 1,183 kWh a year. At the US average residential rate of about $0.17 per kWh, that is about $201 a year in electricity — more than the pump itself cost. The running cost is the real price of the pump, and it is why the watts-per-GPH figure in the next section matters more than the purchase price.

Worked example 2: a 5,000-gallon pond with a waterfall

Now a bigger pond: 5,000 gallons, lightly stocked with goldfish, with a 4-foot waterfall that the owner wants to look full and lively. Goldfish and a 2-hour turnover would need 2,500 GPH of real flow, but the waterfall changes the requirement. A waterfall needs its own flow to look right — roughly 100 GPH per inch of waterfall width is the rule of thumb for a decent sheet of water. A 12-inch-wide waterfall needs about 1,200 GPH just for the display, on top of the filtration flow. If the same pump drives both, the target is the larger of the two numbers, not the sum: the 2,500 GPH filtration flow already exceeds the 1,200 GPH the waterfall wants, so 2,500 GPH covers both. But if the waterfall were 24 inches wide, it would want 2,400 GPH — nearly the whole filtration flow — and the waterfall would look thin at the pond's normal turnover rate.

Head for this pond: 4 feet static, plus a 40-foot hose run at about 4 feet of friction, two elbows at 2 feet, and a filter and UV clarifier at about 3 feet — roughly 13 feet of total head. That is a lot of head, and it pushes the pump choice firmly into external-pump territory, because a submersible rated high enough to deliver 2,500 GPH at 13 feet would be large, hot and expensive to run. An external pump rated 4,000 GPH with a maximum head of 25 feet delivers about 2,600 GPH at 13 feet, which meets the target with a little margin. The same pond bought as a "2,500 GPH" submersible would deliver barely 1,200 GPH at 13 feet of head — less than half the target — and the pond would be under-filtered from day one.

The waterfall-width rule deserves a number of its own, because it catches people who size only for filtration. Measure the waterfall lip in inches and multiply by 100 GPH for a full sheet, or by 50 GPH for a thin trickle. A 6-inch lip wants 600 GPH for a full look; an 18-inch lip wants 1,800. If the filtration flow is smaller than the waterfall flow, the waterfall sets the pump size, and the filtration gets the extra flow for free. If the filtration flow is larger, the waterfall is already covered. Either way, the pump must satisfy whichever demand is bigger.

Submersible vs external pumps: the cost table

Small ponds almost always use submersible pumps: they sit in the pond, they are cheap, they are quiet, and they install in minutes. Large ponds usually use external pumps: they sit in a dry pit or housing beside the pond, they handle high head far better, and they cost less to run per gallon moved. The crossover is around 3,000 to 4,000 GPH of real flow. Below it, a submersible is simpler and cheaper. Above it, an external pump pays for itself in electricity within a year or two.

The watts row is the one that decides the long-term cost. A submersible moving 2,000 GPH at 150 watts and an external moving the same 2,000 GPH at 110 watts differ by 40 watts, running 24 hours a day. That is 40 × 24 × 365 ÷ 1,000 = 350 kWh a year, or about $60 a year at $0.17 per kWh. Over a 5-year pump life, the external pump saves about $300 in electricity — more than the price difference between the two pumps. On a small pond the saving is smaller and the simplicity of a submersible wins. On a big pond, the external pump is the cheaper pump within two years, even though it costs twice as much on the day you buy it.

There is also a middle option worth knowing about: variable-speed and asynchronous submersible pumps. A standard magnetic-drive submersible runs at one speed and one power draw, all the time. An asynchronous or variable-speed pump lets you turn the flow down — in winter, when the fish are dormant and the filter needs less, or at night — and the watts fall with the flow. Turning a pump down to half flow can cut its power draw by more than half, because the power a pump uses rises faster than the flow it moves. If your pond runs year-round, a variable-speed pump can save $50 to $100 a year over a fixed-speed one, and it pays for its premium in about two seasons.

Running costs: the price tag is the small number

A pond pump runs 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, which makes its electricity cost the dominant number in its life. The math is watts times hours divided by 1,000 to get kilowatt-hours, times your electricity rate. At the US average of about $0.17 per kWh, the table below shows what common pump sizes cost per year. Your rate may be higher — California and the Northeast run $0.25 to $0.35 per kWh, which nearly doubles these numbers — so check your own bill and multiply accordingly.

Two lessons follow. First, an oversized pump is not a harmless mistake — it is a bill. Buying a 250-watt pump when a 100-watt pump would have done the job costs an extra $223 a year at average rates, every year, for as long as it runs. The "buy bigger just in case" instinct that feels safe at the store costs more in electricity in a single year than the pump itself. Size the pump to the turnover math, add a modest margin of 10 to 20% for filter clogging, and stop there.

Second, the watts-per-gallon figure is the right way to compare two pumps that claim the same flow. Divide the watts by the GPH the pump delivers at your head — not the box rating. A pump that delivers 1,500 GPH at your head on 100 watts costs 0.067 watts per gallon per hour; one that delivers the same 1,500 GPH on 150 watts costs 0.10 watts per gallon per hour. The second pump costs 50% more to run for the same water moved. Manufacturers rarely publish this figure, but it is one division from the numbers they do publish, and it is the number that decides your electricity bill.

There is one more running-cost habit worth the money: a timer or a variable-speed controller for winter. In cold climates, fish go dormant below about 50°F, the filter bacteria slow down, and the pond needs far less circulation. Cutting the flow in half for the three coldest months saves roughly a quarter of the year's electricity on a fixed-speed pump with a timer, and more on a variable-speed pump. Do not turn the pump off entirely in winter — still water freezes from the surface down and can trap gases under the ice — but a reduced flow keeps the water moving and the bill down.

Notice the pattern: the first four mistakes all leave the pond under-pumped, and the fifth over-pumps it at a price. The common thread is that none of them is a pump problem — every one is a measurement problem. The volume was not measured, the head was not measured, the hose was not matched, the waterfall width was not counted, or the watts were not checked. The pump itself is the easy part. The measuring is the work, and it takes twenty minutes with a tape and a stick.

One more mistake belongs on the list even though it is not a sizing error: putting the pump on the pond floor in the deepest spot. A pump on the floor sucks up settled debris and fish waste and clogs its intake cage constantly. Raising the pump 6 to 12 inches off the bottom on a brick or a stand keeps it out of the sludge layer, and the intake stays clear far longer. It also protects the fish: if the hose splits or a fitting fails, a pump sitting on the floor can drain the pond down to the pump's level, while a raised pump leaves 6 to 12 inches of water in the pond when it runs dry. That margin can be the difference between stressed fish and dead fish.

Checking the pump you already have

If the pond is already running and you suspect the pump is wrong, you do not need the box or the curve — you can measure the flow directly with a bucket and a stopwatch. Hold the hose outlet over a 5-gallon bucket and time how long it takes to fill. If it fills in 15 seconds, the flow is 5 gallons per 15 seconds, or 20 gallons per minute, or 1,200 GPH. If it fills in 30 seconds, the flow is 600 GPH. Compare that number with the turnover target — volume divided by 1 or 2 hours — and you have your answer in a minute.

Do the bucket test at the waterfall outlet, not at the pump, because the outlet is where the head has already done its work. A pump that measures 1,800 GPH straight out of the pond and 1,100 GPH at the waterfall lip has lost 700 GPH to head — and the waterfall number is the one the pond actually gets. If the bucket test comes in well below the target, check the cheap fixes first: a clogged intake cage, a kinked hose, a filter that needs backwashing, or a hose diameter smaller than the pump outlet. Each of those can steal hundreds of GPH, and each costs nothing to fix.

If the flow is right but the water is still green, the pump is probably not the problem — the filter is. A pump can only move water through the filter; it cannot make a filter that is too small work harder. The filter's own rating, in gallons per hour and in pond size, has to match the pump's flow, and a filter rated for 1,000 gallons on a 2,500-gallon pond will stay green no matter how big the pump is. The pump and the filter are a pair, and sizing one without the other is the most common reason a correctly sized pump still leaves a cloudy pond.

Frequently asked questions

What size pond pump do I need for a 1,000 gallon pond?

For hourly turnover you need 1,000 GPH of real flow at your head height, which means buying a pump rated around 1,500 GPH. For two-hour turnover you need 500 GPH of real flow, so a pump rated around 750 to 1,000 GPH. Koi ponds should use the hourly number; lightly stocked water gardens can use the two-hour number.

How do you calculate head height for a pond pump?

Add the vertical lift from the water surface to the highest outlet, usually the waterfall lip, to the friction loss of the pipe run. Roughly 1 foot of head per 10 feet of hose, 1 foot per 90-degree elbow, and 1 to 2 feet each for a filter or UV clarifier. A 3-foot waterfall with 25 feet of hose, two elbows and a filter is about 9 to 10 feet of total head, not 3 feet.

What does GPH mean on a pond pump?

GPH is gallons per hour, the flow the pump moves. The headline GPH on the box is measured at zero head, with no lift and no pipe. At a normal waterfall height the pump delivers 30 to 50 percent less than the box number, so always check the pump curve at your total head height before buying.

How many GPH per gallon of pond water?

For hourly turnover, 1 GPH of real flow per gallon of pond water. For two-hour turnover, 0.5 GPH per gallon. Those are delivered-flow numbers at your head height, not box ratings - a 1,500 gallon pond needs a pump rated around 2,200 to 2,500 GPH to actually deliver 1,500 GPH at 5 feet of head.

Can a pond pump be too big?

Yes, and the cost is electricity. Each extra 100 watts costs about 149 dollars a year at average US rates when the pump runs 24/7. A pump far bigger than the turnover math needs also pushes water through the filter too fast for the media to work properly. Size to the turnover number plus 10 to 20 percent margin for filter clogging, and stop there.

How much does it cost to run a pond pump per year?

A 100-watt pump running 24/7 uses 876 kWh a year, about 149 dollars at 0.17 dollars per kWh. A 60-watt pump costs about 89 dollars a year, a 150-watt pump about 223 dollars, and a 250-watt pump about 372 dollars. Rates in California and the Northeast run 0.25 to 0.35 dollars per kWh, which nearly doubles those figures.
