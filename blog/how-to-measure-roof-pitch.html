Roof Pitch

How to Measure Roof Pitch from the Attic, Ground or a Photo

Knowing how to measure roof pitch is the difference between a roofing quote you can check and one you have to take on faith. The pitch decides which materials are allowed on your roof, how many squares of shingles the job needs, and whether the crew charges a steep-slope surcharge — and roofers measure it before they price anything. The good news is that you can measure it yourself in about two minutes, without climbing on the roof, using one of four methods: a level and tape measure in the attic, a tape measure from the ground, a phone level app, or even a straight-on photo of the gable end. This guide walks through each one with worked numbers, tells you how accurate each method is, and flags the five mistakes that give people the wrong pitch.

First, what you are actually measuring

Roof pitch is a ratio: rise over run, written as X/12. It is the number of inches the roof rises for every 12 inches of horizontal run. A roof that rises 6 inches over a 12-inch horizontal distance is a 6/12 pitch. The same slope is 26.6 degrees, or a 50 percent slope — three ways of saying the same thing, and our roof pitch chart converts between all of them. Every method in this guide ends at the same place: a rise number and a 12-inch run, which gives you the pitch directly. If you only need the answer and not the method, run your span and rise through the free Roof Pitch Calculator and it returns pitch, degrees, and percent together.

One thing to settle before you measure: pitch is measured on the rafter, not on the shingles and not on the fascia board. Shingles add thickness and sit in uneven courses, so a level laid on the roof surface can read a degree or two off — enough to move you a full pitch step. The fascia board is vertical, so it tells you nothing about slope at all. Every method below measures the rafter line, directly or by geometry, which is why the attic method is the most accurate of the four.

Method 1: the attic level-and-tape method (most accurate)

This is the method roofers use, and it needs nothing more than a 12-inch level (or any level with a 12-inch mark) and a tape measure. You stay inside the attic, on the floor, measuring the underside of a rafter.

Step 1. Pick a rafter you can reach comfortably, away from the ridge and away from any sagging or sistered section. A straight, typical rafter in the middle of the roof is what you want — not the one next to a skylight or a chimney, where framing often changes.

Step 2. Hold the level horizontally against the underside of the rafter, with one end touching the rafter and the bubble centered. The level is now your 12-inch run.

Step 3. Mark the 12-inch point on the level, then measure straight down — vertically, with the tape hanging plumb — from that mark to the underside of the rafter. That vertical distance, in inches, is your rise.

Step 4. Your pitch is rise/12. A 6-inch drop means 6/12. A 4.5-inch drop means 4.5/12, which rounds to a 4/12 or 5/12 depending on the roof — most residential roofs are built to whole-number pitches, so a reading between two whole numbers usually means your level was slightly off and the true pitch is the nearer whole number.

Worked example. You hold the level against a rafter and measure 7 inches straight down at the 12-inch mark. Pitch = 7/12. From the chart, that is 30.3 degrees and a 58.3 percent slope, with a rafter factor of 1.158 — every foot of horizontal run is 1.158 feet of rafter. If instead you measure 5 inches, the pitch is 5/12 (22.6 degrees, 41.7 percent, rafter factor 1.083). The difference between 5/12 and 7/12 on a 1,000 sq ft footprint is about 75 sq ft of roofing — real money on a quote, which is why measuring beats guessing.

Tips that matter. Measure in two places on two different rafters. If both readings agree, you have your pitch. If they disagree by more than half an inch, the roof has settled, the framing varies, or one rafter is bowed — measure a third rafter and use the reading that repeats, and tell your roofer about the variation before the quote is final. And keep the level truly level: a bubble even slightly off center at the 12-inch mark moves the vertical measurement by a noticeable amount on a steep roof.

Method 2: measuring from the ground (no attic access needed)

If you cannot get into the attic — no access hatch, a finished attic, a vaulted ceiling — measure from the ground using the eave and the ridge. You need a tape measure and a helper, or a laser measure if you are working alone.

Step 1. Measure the horizontal run: the distance from the outside of the eave wall to the ridge line, measured horizontally. For a simple gable roof on a 24-foot-wide house, the run is half the span — 12 feet — but measure it rather than assuming it, because eave overhangs and wall thickness change the number.

Step 2. Measure the rise: the vertical distance from the eave height to the ridge height. The practical way is to measure the height of the eave (ground to the underside of the eave) and the height of the ridge (ground to the peak), then subtract. A laser measure aimed at the ridge from the ground works well here; a tape measure needs a helper holding the far end at the ridge, which usually means a ladder.

Step 3. Pitch = (rise ÷ run) × 12.

Worked example. Your house is 28 feet wide, so the run is 14 feet. The eave is 9 feet above the ground and the ridge is 16 feet above the ground, so the rise is 16 − 9 = 7 feet. Pitch = (7 ÷ 14) × 12 = 0.5 × 12 = 6/12. Check: 6/12 is 26.6 degrees, the most common residential pitch in the country.

Worked example 2. A 30-foot-wide house, run 15 feet. Eave at 10 feet, ridge at 20 feet, rise 10 feet. Pitch = (10 ÷ 15) × 12 = 0.667 × 12 = 8/12 (33.7 degrees, rafter factor 1.202). That is a steep roof — roofers will need jacks or staging, and quotes should reflect it.

Where this method goes wrong. The two height measurements are the weak point: a one-foot error in the ridge height on a 14-foot run moves the pitch by almost a full step (12 × 1 ÷ 14 = 0.86). Measure both heights from the same ground point, on level ground, and double-check the ridge height — it is the number people most often estimate instead of measuring. Sloped ground is the other trap: if the ground falls away from the house, both heights are inflated by the same amount only if you measure from the same spot, so pick one spot and do not move between the two readings.

Method 3: a phone level app (fastest, good enough for a quote check)

Almost every phone has a level or angle app, and most report degrees directly when you lay the phone on a slope. This is the fastest method, and it is accurate enough to check a roofer's pitch claim — but only if you use it correctly.

Step 1. Calibrate first. Lay the phone on a surface you know is level — a kitchen counter checked with a real level, or a flat floor — and zero the app there. Phone sensors drift, and an uncalibrated app can read 1–2 degrees off, which is a full pitch step.

Step 2. Place the phone flat against the underside of a rafter in the attic, or against the roof surface itself if you are already up there safely. Read the angle in degrees.

Step 3. Convert degrees to pitch: pitch rise = 12 × tan(degrees). Or look it up in the chart: 18.4° is 4/12, 22.6° is 5/12, 26.6° is 6/12, 30.3° is 7/12, 33.7° is 8/12, 36.9° is 9/12, 45° is 12/12.

Worked example. Your phone reads 27 degrees on the rafter. tan(27°) = 0.5095, so 12 × 0.5095 = 6.11 inches of rise — a 6/12 pitch (26.6 degrees is the exact value; 27 is within normal phone error). If it reads 34 degrees: tan(34°) = 0.6745, 12 × 0.6745 = 8.09 — an 8/12 pitch.

Accuracy. A calibrated phone on a rafter is usually within half a degree, which is close enough to name the pitch. An uncalibrated phone, or one laid on shingles instead of a rafter, can be off by 1–2 degrees — enough to call a 6/12 roof a 7/12. Use the app to confirm a measurement you made another way, not as your only measurement when the pitch decides a material choice or a warranty.

Method 4: measuring from a photo (no ladder, no attic)

You can measure roof pitch from a photograph of the gable end — the triangular wall under the roof peak — as long as the photo is taken straight on, square to the gable, with no tilt. This is the method to use when you are buying a house from out of state, checking a listing, or pricing a roof you cannot visit.

Step 1. Take or find a photo of the gable end shot from directly in front of it, with the camera level. A photo taken from an angle, or from below looking up, distorts the triangle and the measurement with it. Phone photos are fine; the gable must fill a good part of the frame.

Step 2. On the photo, measure the gable triangle: the vertical height from the eave line (where the roof meets the wall) to the peak, and the horizontal half-width from the center of the peak to one eave corner. Measure in any unit — pixels on screen, millimetres on a printout — because only the ratio matters.

Step 3. Pitch = (gable height ÷ gable half-width) × 12.

Worked example. On screen, the gable height measures 240 pixels and the half-width measures 480 pixels. Ratio = 240 ÷ 480 = 0.5. Pitch = 0.5 × 12 = 6/12. Another photo: height 300 pixels, half-width 400 pixels. Ratio = 0.75, pitch = 0.75 × 12 = 9/12 (36.9 degrees — steep).

Step 4 (sanity check with a known reference). If the photo is not perfectly straight-on, cross-check with something of known size in the frame: a standard entry door is 80 inches tall, a brick course with mortar is about 2.67 inches, and vinyl siding courses are usually 4 inches of exposure. If the gable height equals three door heights, the rise is about 240 inches; divide by the half-width in the same units and the ratio still holds. The reference does not fix a tilted photo, but it catches a bad measurement — if the door-based rise and the pixel-ratio rise disagree wildly, retake the photo.

Where this method goes wrong. Perspective is everything. A photo taken from 20 degrees off-center compresses the half-width and inflates the pitch; a photo taken looking upward stretches the height. Satellite and street-view imagery can work the same way if the gable faces the camera squarely, but roof-overhang shadows and tree cover make those images unreliable — use them for a rough check, then confirm with method 1 or 2 before ordering materials.

The pattern is simple: the closer you measure to the rafter, the better the answer. The attic method and the speed square both touch the rafter, so they agree with each other. The ground and photo methods measure the roof's geometry from a distance, so small errors in height or angle get multiplied. When two methods disagree by more than one pitch step, trust the attic reading and re-measure the other one — something in the distant measurement is off, and it is usually a height that was estimated instead of measured.

The speed-square shortcut (bonus method)

If you already own a speed square, it measures pitch directly. Hold the square's pivot point against the underside of the rafter with the square's fence flat on the rafter, then rotate the square until its edge is level — a small torpedo level on the square helps, or just read where the rafter edge crosses the square's common-rafter scale. The number at the crossing is the pitch: if the rafter edge crosses at the 6 mark, the pitch is 6/12. Framers use this because it is one motion and needs no arithmetic. The catch is the same as every rafter method: the square must sit on the rafter itself, not on shingles or decking, and the rafter must be straight where you measure.

Five mistakes that give you the wrong pitch

1. Measuring on the shingles. Shingle thickness, uneven courses, and curled edges tilt a level by 1–2 degrees. Always measure the rafter — from inside the attic, or the roof deck if the shingles are off.

2. Measuring the fascia instead of the slope. The fascia board runs vertically down from the roof edge. It has no slope to measure, and a level on it reads nothing useful. The slope lives on the rafter and the roof surface above it.

3. Using the overhang as the run. The eave overhang sticks out past the wall, so the horizontal distance from the fascia to the ridge is longer than the true run. The run is wall to ridge, not fascia to ridge. Including a 12-inch overhang in a 12-foot run inflates the denominator and understates the pitch by nearly a full step.

4. Measuring a warped or sistered rafter. Old rafters bow, and sistered rafters (a second board nailed alongside a damaged one) often sit at a slightly different angle. Measure a typical rafter mid-roof, and confirm on a second one.

5. Measuring a hip roof like a gable roof. On a hip roof the end slopes are not the same pitch as the main slopes in the way a photo suggests — the hip rafter runs diagonally, so a measurement along the hip reads shallower than the true pitch. Measure the common rafters on the main slope, not the hip, and do not use the gable-photo method on a house that has no gable end.

What your pitch number is actually for

Once you have the pitch, three decisions follow from it. First, material minimums: standard asphalt shingles need 4/12, with a special low-slope install allowed down to 2/12; below that you need metal or membrane. Measure a 3/12 roof and the shingle color choice is already made for you. Second, roof area and cost: multiply your footprint by the rafter factor — 1.054 at 4/12, 1.118 at 6/12, 1.202 at 8/12, 1.414 at 12/12 — and you have the surface area the quote should be based on. A 1,200 sq ft footprint is 1,265 sq ft of roofing at 4/12 and 1,697 sq ft at 12/12, a 432 sq ft spread on the same house. Third, labor pricing: crews walk a 4/12 or 5/12 comfortably, slow down at 6/12, and add steep-slope surcharges of 10–25 percent from 7/12 or 8/12 upward. If a quote has no steep-slope line on a 9/12 roof, either the pitch was measured wrong or the surcharge is coming later as a change order.

Planning the project

Measure twice, with two different methods, before you order materials or sign a roofing quote — the attic level-and-tape method plus a phone-app confirmation takes five minutes total and catches the one-in-four measurement that is a full pitch step off. Write the pitch on the quote next to the roofer's number: if they match, you can compare quotes on price alone; if they differ, ask which rafter they measured, because that is usually where the disagreement lives.

Frequently asked questions

How do I measure roof pitch without going on the roof?

Measure from inside the attic: hold a 12-inch level horizontally against the underside of a rafter, then measure straight down from the 12-inch mark to the rafter. The vertical distance in inches is your rise, and your pitch is rise/12. A 6-inch drop is a 6/12 pitch. No ladder and no roof walking needed.

What is the easiest way to measure roof pitch?

A phone level app on a rafter in the attic is the easiest: lay the phone flat against the rafter, read the angle in degrees, and convert — 26.6 degrees is 6/12, 30.3 degrees is 7/12, 18.4 degrees is 4/12. Calibrate the app on a known level surface first, and confirm with the level-and-tape method before ordering materials.

Can I measure roof pitch from a photo?

Yes, from a straight-on photo of the gable end: measure the gable height and the half-width on the photo, then pitch = (height ÷ half-width) × 12. A 240-pixel height over a 480-pixel half-width is 6/12. The photo must be taken square to the gable — an angled photo compresses the width and inflates the pitch, so use it as a remote check and confirm with an attic measurement before ordering.

How accurate is a phone app for measuring roof pitch?

Within about half a degree when calibrated on a known level surface and laid on a rafter — close enough to name the pitch. Uncalibrated, or laid on shingles instead of a rafter, it can be off by 1–2 degrees, which is a full pitch step. Use it to confirm another measurement, not as your only measurement when the pitch decides a material choice.

What pitch is a 6-inch rise over 12 inches?

A 6-inch rise over a 12-inch run is a 6/12 pitch — 26.6 degrees, a 50 percent slope, and a rafter factor of 1.118. It is the most common pitch on US homes built in the last 40 years.

How do I measure pitch on a hip roof?

Measure the common rafters on the main slope, not the hip rafter — the hip runs diagonally, so a measurement along it reads shallower than the true pitch. The attic level-and-tape method on a main-slope rafter works exactly as it does on a gable roof, and the gable-photo method does not apply because a hip roof has no gable end.
