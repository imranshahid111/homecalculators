Drywall Cost Per Square Foot in 2026 (DIY vs Pro)

Drywall cost per square foot in 2026 runs roughly $0.75–$1.25 per square foot if you do it yourself (materials only), and roughly $3–$5 per square foot professionally installed at a standard paint-grade finish — but those two numbers are only comparable if they measure the same square foot, and most of the time they do not. The single most expensive misunderstanding in drywall pricing is the difference between a square foot of drywall and a square foot of floor. A 10×10 bedroom has 100 square feet of floor but about 420 square feet of drywall once you count the walls and the ceiling. A quote of "$2 per square foot" means a very different total depending on which square foot the contractor is counting. This guide prices drywall the honest way — per square foot of drywall surface — with a full DIY breakdown, a full professional breakdown, worked budgets for three standard rooms, and the factors that push a real quote to either end of the range.

First, which square foot? The trap behind most quotes

There are two square feet in every drywall conversation, and quotes rarely say which one they mean. A square foot of drywall surface is the actual area being covered: wall area (perimeter times ceiling height) plus ceiling area (length times width). A square foot of floor is just the room's footprint. Because walls and ceilings add up to several times the floor area, a price per floor square foot looks much bigger than the same job priced per drywall square foot — and a contractor quoting per floor foot can sound dramatically cheaper or more expensive than one quoting per drywall foot, for the identical job.

The conversion depends on room size, because small rooms have more wall relative to their floor. A 10×10 room with 8 ft ceilings has 420 square feet of drywall over 100 square feet of floor — a ratio of 4.2 to 1. A 12×12 room has 528 square feet of drywall over 144 square feet of floor, a ratio of 3.7 to 1. A 20×20 room has 1,040 square feet of drywall over 400 square feet of floor, a ratio of 2.6 to 1. So a $3-per-drywall-square-foot installed price on a 10×10 room equals about $12.60 per floor square foot, while the same $3 rate on a 20×20 room equals about $7.80 per floor square foot. Same rate, wildly different "per square foot" headline. Whenever you compare quotes, convert them all to the same basis — total price divided by drywall surface area is the only comparison that means anything — and ask any contractor who will not state their basis to put it in writing.

One more basis warning: some quotes price only the hanging (screws and sheets on the wall) and leave taping, mudding and sanding — the finishing — as a separate line, or leave it out entirely. A "hang-only" price of $1–$1.50 per drywall square foot sounds like a bargain until the finishing crew bills another $1.50–$2.50. Always ask whether a quote covers hanging plus finishing to a stated finish level, and whether primer and paint are included (usually they are not).

DIY cost, line by line

DIY materials for a standard room break down into five buckets. Sheet prices in 2026 run roughly $13–$18 per 4×8 sheet of 1/2 inch board, $18–$25 for 4×12 sheets, and $20–$30 for 5/8 inch Type X fire-rated board. Screws run about $8–$12 per 5-pound box, a 5-gallon bucket of all-purpose joint compound about $18–$25, a 250 ft roll of paper tape about $5–$8, and corner bead roughly $3–$5 per 8 ft stick. None of these items is expensive alone; the total is just their sum over a lot of square feet.

Those totals land slightly below the $0.75–$1.25 headline range because the headline range adds the consumables people forget: sanding screens and sponges ($10–$20), a mud pan and taping knives if you do not own them ($20–$40), plastic sheeting and painter's tape for dust control ($15–$30), and delivery if your order falls below the free-delivery minimum (commonly 20–30 sheets; a paid delivery runs $50–$100). A first-timer who needs tools pays more again: a drywall lift rental ($40–$60 per day) is the single best purchase for ceiling work, a screw gun or a drill with a dimpler bit ($30–$60 if you do not own a drill), a T-square ($15–$25), and a utility knife with spare blades ($10). Budget $100–$300 in tool costs for a first room, most of it reusable on the next one — which is why the second DIY room is always cheaper per square foot than the first.

The cost DIYers most often undercount is not money at all: it is time. A competent DIYer can hang a 10×10 room in a long day, but finishing takes three coats with a full day of drying between each, plus sanding and touch-ups — realistically two weekends for a first room, and a ceiling doubles the awkwardness. If your time has a price, or if the room needs to be usable by a deadline, that time belongs in the comparison even though it never appears on a receipt.

Professional cost, line by line

A professional quote has three labor layers: hanging, finishing, and (optionally) texture and paint. Hanging alone typically runs $1–$1.75 per drywall square foot including materials, because a two-person crew can hang a room in hours. Finishing — taping, three coats of mud, sanding — adds $1.50–$2.50 per square foot and takes several days of elapsed time even though the crew is only on site for parts of them. Together, hang plus finish to level 4 (the standard paint-grade finish: taped seams, three coats, sanded smooth) lands at $3–$5 per square foot including materials, which is the headline number in this guide.

Finish level is the biggest lever inside a professional quote, and it is worth understanding before you sign anything. Level 0–2 covers are for areas that will never be seen (behind tile, inside service chases). Level 3 is a single-coat finish for areas getting heavy texture. Level 4 is the standard for painted walls in living spaces. Level 5 adds a thin skim coat over the entire surface and is specified for smooth walls under glossy paint or harsh side-lighting, where even a perfect level 4 can show joints. Level 5 adds roughly $1.50–$2.50 per square foot over level 4, and it is worth it only where the lighting or the paint sheen will actually reveal the difference — a hallway lit by a single ceiling fixture rarely needs it, a wall facing floor-to-ceiling windows often does. Texture, meanwhile, is usually quoted separately at $0.50–$0.75 per square foot: orange peel and knockdown are the common choices, and texture is forgiving — it hides minor finishing imperfections, which is one reason level 3 plus texture costs less than level 4 plus smooth paint.

Paint is almost always separate: primer plus two coats of paint adds $0.75–$1.50 per drywall square foot, or $1.50–$3.50 per floor square foot if the painter quotes that way. Confirm whether your drywall quote includes primer — a surprising number of "finished" quotes stop at sanded mud, and bare mud drinks paint unevenly if it is not primed first.

Read the 10×10 row as the decision in miniature: DIY saves roughly $950–$1,575 on a small room, and roughly $2,340–$3,900 on a 20×20 room. The savings scale with size, but so does the finishing workload — a 20×20 room has 1,040 square feet of seams to tape and mud, and a DIY finish at that scale is a multi-weekend project. That is the real trade: DIY wins on money at every size, and professionals win on time and finish quality, with the balance tipping toward professionals as the room gets bigger, the ceilings get higher, or the finish needs to be flawless.

For a whole-house sense of scale: a 2,000-square-foot house typically has 6,000–8,000 square feet of drywall surface (the multiplier falls as houses get bigger, because interior walls are shared between rooms). At professional rates that is roughly $18,000–$40,000 for hang and finish, before paint; DIY materials for the same house run roughly $4,500–$10,000. New-construction drywall is usually quoted per sheet or per board foot by the crew rather than per square foot, and volume brings the per-foot rate toward the bottom of the range — but the comparison still holds, and it is why owner-builders who DIY their drywall save five figures and spend a month doing it.

What moves your quote up or down

Seven factors move a drywall quote more than haggling does. Ceiling height is first: 9 ft and 10 ft walls need more material per room, more ladder or scaffold work, and often 4×12 sheets or a lift, adding roughly 10–20% over an 8 ft room of the same footprint. Ceilings themselves cost more per square foot than walls because the work is overhead, slower, and harder on the crew — vaulted or cathedral ceilings add more again, since the surface area exceeds the floor footprint and every sheet is cut at an angle.

Second, thickness and type: 5/8 inch Type X board costs more per sheet and weighs more to hang, and moisture-resistant board for bathrooms adds a premium over standard board. Third, demolition and disposal: removing old drywall, plaster or paneling before hanging new board adds $1–$2 per square foot, plus dump fees — and plaster removal can reveal framing repairs that nobody quoted. Fourth, repairs and patches: small patch jobs are priced per patch ($150–$400 each is common) rather than per square foot, because the fixed cost of a visit dominates; per-square-foot rates only make sense above roughly 100 square feet of work.

Fifth, access and conditions: tight stairwells that force 4×8 sheets instead of 4×12, occupied homes that need daily dust protection and cleanup, and jobs that require working around existing trim or cabinets all slow a crew down. Sixth, region and season: labor rates vary by 30–50% between markets, and good crews book out weeks ahead in remodeling season. Seventh, finish and paint choices, covered above — level 5 and glossy paint are the two upgrades that cost the most for the least visible return in most rooms, so spend them where light and sheen will actually show the difference.

The decision rule that fits most homeowners: DIY the rooms where a good-enough finish is genuinely good enough — garages, basements, closets, and rooms getting texture or flat paint in forgiving light. Hire out the rooms where the finish will be judged every day under hard light — the main living wall, a smooth-finish feature wall, anything with gloss paint or floor-to-ceiling windows. And consider the hybrid that experienced renovators use: hang the sheets yourself (the fast, forgiving half) and hire a finisher for taping and mudding (the slow, skill-heavy half). A finish-only quote often lands at $1.50–$2.50 per square foot, splitting the savings and the quality roughly down the middle.

The money traps

First, the floor-area trap from the opening section: never compare quotes until every quote is converted to the same basis, ideally total price for the same scope. Second, the "materials included" trap: a quote that includes materials but uses thinner board, fewer screws or a single coat of mud is not the same job at a lower price — ask for board thickness and finish level in writing. Third, the small-job trap: minimum charges mean a 50-square-foot patch costs far more per square foot than a whole room, so batch small patches into one visit. Fourth, the second-delivery trap from our sheet-count guide: ordering exactly the computed sheet count with no waste saves about $30 in sheets and risks a $50–$100 second delivery plus a lost weekend. Fifth, the paint trap: a finished wall that was never primed will drink the first coat of paint unevenly and show flashing — if primer is not in the quote, budget for it separately. Finally, the permit trap: some jurisdictions require permits and inspections for drywall in garages (fire separation) and basements (egress and moisture rules); the permit fee is small, the cost of redoing unpermitted work is not.

Frequently asked questions

How much does drywall cost per square foot installed in 2026?

About $3–$5 per square foot of drywall surface for professional hanging plus finishing to a level 4 paint-grade finish, materials included. Adding texture brings it to about $3.50–$5.75, and a level 5 skim-coat finish runs about $4.50–$7. Always confirm whether the quote is per square foot of drywall or per square foot of floor — the totals differ by a factor of roughly 3 to 4.

How much does DIY drywall cost per square foot?

About $0.75–$1.25 per square foot of drywall for materials alone (sheets, screws, mud, tape, corner bead, plus consumables and delivery). A first-timer buying tools should budget $1.10–$1.90 per square foot for the first room; later rooms drop back toward the materials-only range because the tools are already owned.

What does it cost to drywall a 10×10 room?

DIY materials run about $315–$525 for a 10×10 room with 8 ft ceilings (420 sq ft of drywall). Professionally installed, hang plus level 4 finish runs about $1,260–$2,100, or about $1,785–$2,730 with primer and paint included. Texture adds roughly $210–$315.

Is drywall priced per square foot of floor or wall?

It should be priced per square foot of drywall surface (walls plus ceiling), but some quotes use floor area instead. A 10×10 room has 100 sq ft of floor but 420 sq ft of drywall, so a $3-per-drywall-foot rate equals about $12.60 per floor foot. Convert every quote to a total price for the same scope before comparing.

How much extra is a level 5 drywall finish?

Level 5 adds roughly $1.50–$2.50 per square foot over a level 4 finish, bringing an installed job to about $4.50–$7 per square foot. It is worth it for smooth walls under glossy paint or harsh side-lighting, and usually unnecessary for rooms getting texture or flat paint in forgiving light.

Does drywall cost include paint?

Usually not. Most drywall quotes stop at sanded mud, and primer plus two coats of paint adds about $0.75–$1.50 per square foot of drywall. Confirm in writing whether primer is included — unprimed mud absorbs paint unevenly and shows flashing.

Is it cheaper to hang drywall yourself and hire a finisher?

Yes, often by a useful margin. Hanging is the fast, forgiving half of the job; finishing is the slow, skill-heavy half. A finish-only quote typically runs $1.50–$2.50 per square foot, so hanging yourself and hiring out the finishing splits the savings and the quality roughly down the middle.
