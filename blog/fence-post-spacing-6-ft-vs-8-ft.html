6 ft vs 8 ft Post Spacing: Which Fence Lasts Longer?

Fence post spacing is the distance between the centers of adjacent posts, and the two standard choices — 6 ft and 8 ft — decide more about your fence's life than most builders realize. The short answer: 8 ft spacing is fine for short, open fences in calm areas, but a 6 ft privacy fence in a windy spot, a tall fence, or any fence with a gate should be built on 6 ft spacing, because it cuts the wind load on each post by roughly a quarter and stops the rails from sagging between posts. The extra posts cost real money — about 25% more posts and concrete — but a fence that stays straight for 25 years is cheaper than a fence that leans after five. This guide works through the post counts, the wind physics, the rail question, and the cost math for real fence lengths, so you can pick the spacing your fence actually needs rather than the one your contractor defaults to.

What post spacing means, and how it is measured

Post spacing is always measured center to center: from the middle of one post to the middle of the next. That detail matters, because a fence built with 8 ft between post faces is actually spaced about 8 ft 4 inches center to center once you account for the 4-inch post itself — and that extra span is exactly where rails start to sag. When a contractor says "8-foot spacing," confirm they mean center to center, and check it with a tape on the first few posts before the holes are dug. A spacing error discovered after concrete is poured is a spacing error you live with.

The two standards exist for a reason. Lumber comes in 8 ft and 16 ft lengths, so 8 ft spacing lets a single 8 ft rail span exactly one bay with no cutting — that is why 8 ft became the default for budget fences. Six-foot spacing exists because builders learned, over decades of leaning fences, that 8 ft is too far apart for a tall, solid fence catching wind. Neither number is a rule of nature; both are trade-offs between cost and strength, and the right one depends on fence height, fence style, wind exposure, and soil. The rest of this guide weighs those four factors one by one.

Post counts: what 6 ft vs 8 ft spacing means for real fence lengths

The post count for a straight run is simple: divide the fence length by the spacing, then add one for the final end post. A 100 ft fence at 8 ft spacing needs 100 ÷ 8 = 12.5 bays, which rounds up to 13 bays and 14 posts; at 6 ft spacing it needs 100 ÷ 6 = 16.7 bays, or 17 bays and 18 posts. That is four extra posts, four extra holes, and four extra bags of concrete on every 100 ft — the core cost of the upgrade. Gates, corners and ends add posts on top of either number, and they are the same in both layouts, so the comparison below counts line posts only; your total will be a few posts higher in both cases.

Why spacing decides lifespan: wind load and rail sag

A fence post is a lever. Wind pushes on the fence boards above ground, and that force is transferred to the posts, which resist it through their buried section and footing. The wind load each post carries is proportional to the area of fence it supports — which is the fence height multiplied by the spacing. A 6 ft tall fence at 8 ft spacing loads each post with 48 square feet of wind area; at 6 ft spacing, the same fence loads each post with 36 square feet. That is a 25% reduction in load per post, from spacing alone, with no change to post size, depth or concrete. For a privacy fence — a solid wall of boards with no gaps for wind to pass through — that 25% is the difference between posts that stay plumb and posts that slowly lean in the direction of the prevailing wind.

The second failure mode is rail sag. Rails span between posts like little bridges, and a rail's sag grows roughly with the cube of its span — so a rail spanning 8 ft sags far more than twice as much as a rail spanning 6 ft; in practice, an 8 ft span of standard 2x4 rail visibly sags within a few years under the weight of fence boards, while a 6 ft span of the same rail stays straight. Sagging rails pull boards out of alignment, open gaps at the top of the fence, and put a twisting load on the posts at each end of the span. The usual fix for 8 ft spans is a third rail (more on that below), which adds back part of the cost the wider spacing saved. On a tall fence, 6 ft spacing with two rails often outperforms 8 ft spacing with three — and looks cleaner, because the middle rail on a privacy fence interrupts the board pattern.

Height multiplies both effects. Wind force on a fence grows with the square of wind speed and linearly with height, so a 6 ft privacy fence catches roughly 50% more wind load per post than a 4 ft fence of the same style at the same spacing. That is why the spacing question barely matters for a 4 ft picket fence — there is little load to worry about — and matters enormously for a 6 ft or 8 ft privacy fence. If your fence is tall and solid, spacing is the single biggest structural decision you make, bigger than post size and nearly as important as post depth.

Cost comparison: a 100 ft privacy fence at each spacing

The cost of tighter spacing is posts, concrete, and digging — not boards or rails, which stay the same. Using 2026 typical DIY material prices (4x4 pressure-treated post $12–$18, one 50 lb bag of concrete mix $6–$8 per post, and roughly $30–$50 per hole in contractor labor where a crew digs), the upgrade from 8 ft to 6 ft spacing on a 100 ft fence costs about $100–$150 in DIY materials and roughly $250–$400 installed. Against a 100 ft privacy fence that costs $3,000–$5,000 installed, that is a 5–10% premium — and it buys a 25% load reduction on every post and rails that stay straight. One cost that often surprises people: the third rail. An 8 ft privacy fence span usually needs three rails to control sag, while a 6 ft span is fine with two. A third rail on a 100 ft fence adds 13 rails at roughly $8–$12 each — about $100–$155 — which wipes out most of the material savings of wider spacing. Once you price the fence honestly, with the rails it actually needs, the 6 ft layout is often within $100 of the 8 ft layout on materials, and it is the stronger fence. Contractors who quote 8 ft spacing with two rails are quoting a fence that will sag; ask whether the quote includes a third rail before comparing prices.

When 6 ft spacing wins

Choose 6 ft spacing when any of these apply. First, a privacy fence 6 ft or taller — the wind load is simply too high for 8 ft spans to hold straight over decades. Second, a windy or exposed site: hilltops, open plains, coastal areas, and any yard where the fence faces the prevailing wind without a windbreak. Third, heavy fence styles: board-on-board (where boards overlap and the fence is effectively thicker and heavier), horizontal slat fences (which put more load on rails), and any fence with a top cap or lattice topper that adds wind area. Fourth, gates and gate-adjacent bays: the bays on either side of a gate take extra stress from the gate's weight and swing, and 6 ft spacing there is cheap insurance even if the rest of the fence is at 8 ft. Fifth, soft or sandy soil, where each post grips less — tighter spacing spreads the load across more posts and more footings. If two or more of these apply, 6 ft spacing is not an upgrade, it is the baseline.

A useful rule of thumb from fence builders: if the fence is solid and 6 ft tall, space at 6 ft; if the fence is open (picket, spaced board, or ranch rail) and 4 ft or shorter, 8 ft is fine. Everything in between is a judgment call, and the tiebreakers are wind exposure and soil. A 5 ft spaced-board fence on flat ground in a sheltered suburb will last decades at 8 ft spacing. The same fence on a windy hilltop in sandy soil will not, and the builder who spaced it at 6 ft will be the one whose fence still looks straight in year fifteen.

When 8 ft spacing is fine

Eight-foot spacing is not a mistake — it is the right call for the right fence. Open fences let wind through, so the load per post is a fraction of a privacy fence's load: a picket fence with half-inch gaps between boards catches perhaps 40–60% of the wind a solid fence catches, and a ranch rail fence catches far less. Short fences have less leverage, so a 3 ft or 4 ft fence at 8 ft spacing has loads a 6 ft privacy fence would never see. Sheltered yards — fences backed by a house, a hedge, or a neighboring fence on the windward side — see much less wind than the weather report suggests. And on flat ground in firm soil, 8 ft posts hold well, because the soil itself is doing part of the work the extra posts would do.

Budget is a legitimate reason too. On a 200 ft run, the 6 ft upgrade costs eight extra posts and holes, and if the fence is a 4 ft picket fence in a sheltered yard, that money buys almost nothing in lifespan — the fence would outlast its boards either way. The mistake is not choosing 8 ft spacing; the mistake is choosing it by default, without checking height, style, wind and soil first. A fence quote that does not mention spacing at all is a quote worth questioning, because the builder has picked a default for you, and defaults favor the builder's digging time, not your fence's twentieth year.

Worked example: 150 ft privacy fence, both spacings

Take a 150 ft backyard run: 6 ft tall, solid cedar privacy boards, flat ground, moderate wind, firm loam soil. At 8 ft spacing you need 20 posts (19 bays) and, to control sag, three rails per bay — 57 rails. At 6 ft spacing you need 26 posts (25 bays) and two rails per bay — 50 rails. The 6 ft layout costs six more posts, six more bags of concrete, and six more holes, but seven fewer rails, and every post carries 25% less wind load. Priced at DIY material rates, the 6 ft layout runs roughly $100–$180 more in total — about $0.70–$1.20 per linear foot — on a fence whose boards alone cost $15–$25 per foot. For that premium, the fence's posts are loaded like a fence with a third less wind, and its rails will not sag between posts.

Now change one variable: make the site a windy hilltop. The wind load per post at 8 ft spacing now exceeds what a standard 4x4 post in a 12-inch footing comfortably resists over decades, and the fix at 8 ft spacing is bigger posts (6x6), deeper holes, or more concrete per post — each of which costs more than the six extra posts the 6 ft layout needed. This is the pattern to remember: when conditions are hard, the cost of fixing wide spacing exceeds the cost of tighter spacing. Builders who work windy sites know this, which is why coastal and plains fence companies often default to 6 ft spacing while suburban companies default to 8 ft. Ask a local builder what spacing they use on privacy fences, and why — their answer tells you what your local wind and soil demand.

Laying out the spacing: equal bays on an odd length

Few real fence runs divide evenly by 6 or 8. The fix is to divide the run into equal bays that are close to your target spacing, never exceeding it. For a 100 ft run at a 6 ft target: 100 ÷ 6 = 16.7, so use 17 bays of 100 ÷ 17 = 5 ft 10.5 inches — slightly under target, which is exactly right, because a bay shorter than target is stronger than target. For a 100 ft run at an 8 ft target: 100 ÷ 8 = 12.5, so use 13 bays of 7 ft 8 inches. Never round down to fewer bays — 12 bays of 8 ft 4 inches on a 100 ft run exceeds the 8 ft target and creates the longest, weakest span on the fence. And never let one bay absorb the remainder: a run of eleven 8 ft bays plus one 12 ft bay is a fence with one span guaranteed to sag.

Three layout details finish the job. First, stake the whole run and measure twice before digging — moving a stake is free, moving a post is not. Second, keep gate bays and corner bays at or under your target spacing, even if that means a slightly shorter bay there; gates stress their neighbors. Third, on a slope, measure spacing horizontally (along the ground's run), not along the slope's surface, or the bays will be longer than planned on steep ground. A stepped fence on a slope — where each bay drops to follow the grade — keeps spacing honest and looks intentional; a racked fence that follows the slope needs the same horizontal spacing, with rails angled to match.

The spacing mistakes that show up in year five

First, spacing face to face instead of center to center, which silently adds 4 inches to every span. Second, trusting a string line over a long run without checking — string sags and stretches, and a run that drifts a few inches per bay ends up with one bay far over target. Third, letting the last bay absorb the leftover length, which creates the sagging span described above. Fourth, spacing gate posts at the standard spacing instead of tightening the bays around a gate, which is why gates are the first thing to lean. Fifth, pairing wide spacing with two rails on a tall privacy fence, which guarantees sag no matter how good the posts are. Every one of these is free to avoid on layout day and expensive to fix later, because the fix is digging out posts or rebuilding rails after the fence is up.

Frequently asked questions

What is standard fence post spacing?

8 ft center to center is the most common standard, because 8 ft lumber spans one bay exactly. 6 ft center to center is the standard upgrade for tall privacy fences, windy sites and heavy styles, and it is the default many fence companies use on 6 ft privacy fences.

Is 6 ft or 8 ft post spacing better?

For a 6 ft privacy fence, 6 ft spacing is better: each post carries about 25% less wind load and the rails span a shorter distance, so they do not sag. For a short, open fence in a sheltered yard, 8 ft spacing is fine and saves posts. Height, style, wind and soil decide — not habit.

How many posts do I need for a 100 ft fence?

At 8 ft spacing, 14 posts (13 bays); at 6 ft spacing, 18 posts (17 bays). Add posts for corners, gates and ends. Use our free calculator to get the exact count for your fence length and spacing.

Do I need 3 rails with 8 ft post spacing?

For a 6 ft privacy fence, yes — a 2x4 rail spanning 8 ft sags under the weight of boards within a few years. Three rails control the sag. At 6 ft spacing, two rails are usually enough for a 6 ft fence, which offsets much of the cost of the extra posts.

Can I mix 6 ft and 8 ft spacing on one fence?

Yes, and it is often smart: use 6 ft spacing around gates, on the windward side, and on the tallest or most exposed sections, and 8 ft spacing on short, sheltered, open sections. Just keep each bay at or under its target — never let one bay run long to absorb leftover length.

How do I space posts on an uneven fence length?

Divide the run into equal bays at or under your target spacing: for 100 ft at a 6 ft target, use 17 bays of about 5 ft 10.5 inches; at an 8 ft target, use 13 bays of about 7 ft 8 inches. Equal bays share wind load evenly and look right from the street.
