How to Calculate Board Feet: The Simple Formula Lumber Yards Use

If you want to know how to calculate board feet, the good news is the math is simple: multiply thickness by width by length, then divide by 12. This guide walks through that formula exactly the way lumber yards and hardwood dealers use it — with worked examples on real board sizes, the nominal-versus-actual trap that ruins most first-timers' math, 2026 price ranges, and the waste allowance you should always add before you buy.

What a board foot actually is

A board foot is a unit of volume, not length or area. One board foot equals 144 cubic inches of wood — a block that is 1 foot long, 1 foot wide, and 1 inch thick. Because it measures volume, a board foot stays fair no matter what size of lumber you are comparing: a short, wide, thick plank and a long, narrow, thin board can contain exactly the same number of board feet.

This is why lumber — especially hardwood at specialty dealers — is priced by the board foot rather than by the piece or the linear foot. A 2-inch-thick oak plank contains twice the wood of a 1-inch plank of the same width and length, so charging by the board foot automatically prices the thicker board at roughly double. Linear-foot pricing would be wrong here: two boards that are both 8 feet long would cost the same even though one holds twice as much lumber.

Big-box stores selling construction-grade 2×4s and 2×6s usually price per piece instead, because every piece in a stack is the same size and the math is already done. But the moment you shop hardwood for furniture, cabinetry, or any custom milling, you will hear prices quoted per board foot, and the yard will expect you to compute your own lumber bill. That is exactly what this guide teaches.

The board foot formula

Here is the complete formula for how to calculate board feet:

Board Feet = Thickness (inches) x Width (inches) x Length (feet) / 12

That is all there is. Measure the board's thickness in inches, its width in inches, its length in feet, multiply the three numbers, and divide by 12. If you prefer to measure length in inches instead of feet, use the equivalent form:

Board Feet = Thickness (in) x Width (in) x Length (in) / 144

Both formulas are identical — 12 x 12 = 144 — so use whichever matches your tape measure. Professional estimators memorize the first form because lumber is sold in foot lengths: "T times W times L, over 12."

Worked example: one board, step by step

Let us calculate the board feet in a single common board: a 1×6, 8 feet long.

  1. Thickness: Use the actual thickness, not the nominal. A 1×6 board is really 0.75 inches thick.
  2. Width: A 1×6 is really 5.5 inches wide.
  3. Length: 8 feet.
  4. Multiply: 0.75 x 5.5 x 8 = 33.
  5. Divide by 12: 33 / 12 = 2.75 board feet.

So one 1×6×8 board contains 2.75 board feet. If you need 12 of them, that is 12 x 2.75 = 33 board feet total. Notice how the actual (dressed) dimensions went into the formula, not the printed 1×6 name. Lumber yards measure what is in the rack — more on that below.

One more quick example with a chunkier board: a 2×8, 10 feet long. Actual dimensions are 1.5 inches thick and 7.25 inches wide. Math: 1.5 x 7.25 x 10 = 108.75, divided by 12 = 9.06 board feet. Roughly nine board feet in a single stick of 2×8×10.

Full project example: a simple bookshelf

Let us put it all together with a realistic project — a small freestanding bookshelf built from 1×10 poplar:

  • Sides: 2 pieces, 1×10, 6 ft long. One board-foot per foot of 1×10 is 0.58, so 2 x 6 x 0.58 = 6.94 BF.
  • Shelves: 5 pieces, 1×10, 3 ft long. 5 x 3 x 0.58 = 8.67 BF.
  • Top: 1 piece, 1×10, 3.5 ft long. 1 x 3.5 x 0.58 = 2.02 BF.

Project total: 6.94 + 8.67 + 2.02 = 17.63 board feet. Now add waste. This is furniture with cutouts around a few knots, so allow 15%: 17.63 x 1.15 = 20.27 BF, round up to 21. At roughly $4 per board foot for poplar, the lumber budget is 21 x $4 = about $84. Doing this arithmetic before the store trip means no surprises at the checkout — and no second trip when you come up two boards short.

Nominal vs actual: the trap that breaks your math

The single most common mistake when calculating board feet is plugging the nominal size into the formula. A "1×6" is not 1 inch by 6 inches. Dimensional lumber is cut rough, then dried and planed smooth, and the finished board is smaller than its name:

NominalActual thicknessActual width

2 x 81-1/2 in7-1/4 in

4 x 43-1/2 in3-1/2 in

For construction lumber from a big-box store, use the actual dressed sizes in the table above — that is what the yard's per-piece price is based on. For rough-sawn hardwood straight from a mill, measure the actual board with a tape before you pay, because rough boards can vary a sixteenth either way and hardwood dealers charge on the real dimensions of each board in the rack, including any bark edge or taper you are buying.

A quick sanity check: compute both ways once. A 2×4×8 using nominal dimensions (2 x 4 x 8 / 12) gives 5.33 BF; using actual dimensions (1.5 x 3.5 x 8 / 12) gives 3.5 BF. That is a 52% overcount if you use the nominal numbers — enough to blow a whole project budget.

Mistakes that cost people money

Forgetting the waste allowance. Ten percent is the minimum for straight, clean cuts. Knotty lumber, grain matching, angled cuts and furniture joinery need 15–20%. Buying the exact calculated amount guarantees a second trip — or a visibly different board that does not match the first batch.

Confusing board feet with linear feet. Linear feet measure length only: 10 feet of board is 10 linear feet whether it is a 1×2 or a 2×12. Board feet measure volume, so a 2×12×10 (20 BF) costs roughly four times what a 1×6×10 (5 BF) costs at the same price per board foot.

Ignoring the board's real width at the narrow end. Rough boards taper. Hardwood dealers measure and average; if you are estimating from a stack, measure the narrower end, not the fat one.

Buying in separate trips. Wood color and grain vary between kiln runs and even between boards in one rack. Buying the whole project's lumber in one trip — using your board-foot total — keeps the pieces consistent.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate board feet?

Multiply the board's thickness in inches by its width in inches by its length in feet, then divide by 12. For a 2x6x10 board with actual dimensions 1.5 x 5.5 in: 1.5 x 5.5 x 10 / 12 = 6.88 board feet.

What is the board foot formula when length is in inches?

Use (thickness x width x length) / 144, with all three dimensions in inches. It is the same formula — 144 is just 12 x 12, converting the cubic-inch volume into board feet.

How many board feet are in a 2x4x8?

A 2x4x8 contains about 3.5 board feet when you use its actual dimensions (1.5 x 3.5 in): 1.5 x 3.5 x 8 / 12 = 3.5 BF. Using the nominal 2 x 4 numbers gives 5.33 BF, which is why yards measure the real board.

Is a board foot the same as a square foot?

No. A square foot measures area; a board foot measures volume (144 cubic inches). Two boards can cover the same square footage but contain very different board feet if one is thicker.

Why is lumber priced by the board foot?

Because board-foot pricing scales with how much wood you actually get. A 2-inch-thick board holds twice the lumber of a 1-inch board of the same width and length, so per-board-foot pricing keeps the cost fair across sizes and species.

How much extra lumber should I buy for waste?

Add 10% for simple straight cuts and 15-20% for furniture, knotty stock, grain matching or angled cuts. Hardwood bought in separate batches can also differ in color, so buying it all at once pays off.
