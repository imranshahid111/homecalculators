How Many Yards for a Quilt? Every Size, Charted

If you are asking how much fabric for a queen quilt, the short answer is about 10 yards for the quilt top, 3.5 yards of 108-inch backing fabric (or 10 yards of 44-inch backing), and 1 yard for the binding — roughly 14.5 yards in total when you buy wide backing, or 21 yards when every piece comes off a 44-inch bolt. The number surprises most first-time quilters, because a quilt is not one piece of fabric: it is three layers — top, batting, backing — plus a binding strip that runs around all four sides, and the yardage is decided by the width of the bolt before the length is ever measured. This guide charts every standard size from crib to king with the top, backing and binding yardage for each, the width-first formula behind the numbers, and the worked math for a queen, a twin and a king, so you can buy the right amount the first time and stop guessing at the cutting table.

Three patterns jump out of the table. First, the top is the big number: a queen top is 10 yards, two-thirds of the whole quilt's yardage, because the top is 90 inches wide and 44-inch fabric needs three widths to cover it. Second, the backing is where the width of the bolt matters most — a queen back is 3.5 yards from 108-inch fabric but 10 yards from 44-inch fabric, a saving of 6.5 yards, which is why wide backing fabric exists and why it is worth finding even when the print range is small. Third, the binding is always the small number, under a yard for every size, which is why it is the one piece quilters most often buy as a single yard and forget about.

One warning about the table: the sizes are the standard finished sizes, but real quilts vary. A queen top can be 90 by 90 inches for a mattress-only quilt with no drop, or 96 by 108 inches for a quilt with a generous drop over the sides of a thick mattress. If your quilt is bigger than the standard size, the yardage grows in whole-width jumps — an extra 6 inches of width can add a whole extra width of fabric, which is an extra 3 yards on a queen. Measure your finished size first, then read the chart; the chart is a starting point, not a substitute for your measurements. The formula: width first, then length

Every yardage number in the chart comes from one formula, and the order matters: fit the width first, then multiply the length. The formula is widths needed equals the project width divided by the usable fabric width, rounded up to a whole number; yardage equals widths needed times the cut length in inches, divided by 36, plus a 10 percent allowance. The usable width is about an inch less than the printed width, because the selvages — the tightly woven edges, roughly half an inch on each side — are trimmed off or kept out of the visible piece. A 44-inch bolt gives about 43 usable inches, a 54-inch bolt gives about 53, and a 108-inch bolt gives about 107.

Take the queen top as the worked example. The finished top is 90 inches wide and 108 inches long. Widths needed are 90 divided by 43, which is 2.09, rounded up to 3 widths — because two widths give only 86 inches, which is 4 inches short of a 90-inch top, and there is no layout that saves it. Each width is cut to the quilt length, 108 inches, so three widths at 108 inches is 324 inches, which is 9 yards. Add the 10 percent allowance and the figure is 9.9 yards, so buy 10 yards. The same top from 54-inch decorator fabric would need only 2 widths, because two widths give 108 inches, and the yardage would be 2 times 108 inches, or 216 inches, which is 6 yards plus allowance, so buy 7 yards — but quilting prints are rarely sold on 54-inch bolts, so the 44-inch figure is the one that counts in practice.

The allowance is not optional padding. It covers the seam allowances that disappear into every seam — a quarter inch on each side of every piece, which adds up across a pieced top — plus the fabric lost to squaring up the bolt, the pieces cut slightly wrong, and the one strip that gets cut with the print running the wrong way. Ten percent is the standard allowance for a pieced top; for a top with a large pattern repeat, use 15 to 20 percent, because each cut length is rounded up to a whole number of repeats. A 24-inch repeat on a 108-inch quilt length adds up to 24 inches per width, and on a three-width queen top that can add 2 yards on its own. The repeat math is in the fabric width guide, and the yardage guide by project works through the same formula for quilts, curtains and dresses.

Quilt top yardage: size by size

The top is where the yardage lives, and it is also where the width rule bites hardest, because the top is the widest piece of the quilt. The table below shows the top yardage for each size from 44-inch quilting cotton, with the width count that produces it, so you can see exactly where each number comes from.

The jump that matters is between the full and the queen. A full top is 81 inches wide, which fits in two widths of 44-inch fabric with 5 inches to spare, and needs 6 yards. A queen top is 90 inches wide, which is 4 inches too wide for two widths, and needs a third width — so the yardage jumps from 6 yards to 10 yards, a 4-yard jump for 9 extra inches of width. That is the width rule in its purest form: yardage does not grow smoothly with size, it grows in whole-width steps, and the step from two widths to three is the most expensive one in the whole chart.

The king shows the other side of the same rule. A king top is 108 inches wide, which needs the same three widths as the queen, because three widths give 129 inches and two give only 86. So a king top and a queen top need the same 10 yards of 44-inch fabric for the top, even though the king is 18 inches wider — the extra width is absorbed inside the third width, and the yardage does not change. The king costs more than the queen only in the backing and the batting, not in the top. If you are choosing between a queen and a king, the top yardage is not the deciding factor; the backing is.

Two more top-yardage facts belong here. First, the numbers above assume a plain or simply pieced top cut from one fabric. A patchwork top made from many fabrics needs the same total yardage, but split across the fabrics in the pattern — a queen top that is half one print and half another needs 5 yards of each, not 10 yards of one. Fat quarters and charm packs do not change the total either: a fat quarter is 18 by 22 inches, or a quarter of a yard, so a queen top needs the equivalent of 40 fat quarters, and buying them one at a time costs far more per yard than buying yardage off the bolt. Second, a top with borders needs extra fabric beyond the chart: a 6-inch border around a queen top adds about 1.5 yards, because the border strips run the full length of each side. Add borders after the main top yardage, not inside it.

Backing yardage: the 108-inch advantage

The backing is the underside of the quilt, and it is cut 4 inches larger than the finished top on every side — an 8-inch overhang in total — so the quilter has room to clamp and baste it during quilting. A queen top of 90 by 108 inches needs a backing of 98 by 116 inches. From 44-inch fabric that is three widths, because 98 inches divided by 43 is 2.28, rounded up to 3, and each width is cut 116 inches long: 3 times 116 is 348 inches, which is 9.67 yards, plus allowance, so buy 10 yards. From 108-inch backing fabric it is one width, because 98 inches fits inside the 107 usable inches, and one width cut 116 inches long is 116 inches, which is 3.22 yards, plus allowance, so buy 3.5 yards. The saving is 6.5 yards, or about $97 at $15 a yard — the single biggest saving in the whole quilt.

The full size shows why wide backing is almost mandatory past the twin. A full backing is 89 inches wide, which is 3 inches too wide for two widths of 44-inch fabric, so it needs a third width and 9 yards — more than the top itself needs. From 108-inch fabric the same backing is one width and 3 yards. The king is the only size where even 108-inch fabric needs two widths, because the king backing is 116 inches wide and the usable width of a 108-inch bolt is about 107 inches: two widths at 116 inches is 232 inches, or 6.44 yards, so buy 7 yards. Even so, 7 yards beats 10 yards from 44-inch fabric, and it beats it with one seam instead of two.

The seam question matters as much as the yardage. A backing pieced from three widths of 44-inch fabric has two long seams running down the back of the quilt, and those seams are visible and can pucker under quilting. A backing from 108-inch fabric has no seam at all up to the queen size, and one seam on a king. The trade-off is the print range: wide backing fabric comes in a much smaller range of prints than quilting cotton, mostly solids, small prints and a few wide-scale designs. If the exact print matters more than the seam, buy the 44-inch fabric and accept the yardage; if the seam matters more, buy the wide backing and pick from what is available. Either way, buy the backing from the same bolt as the top if the colours need to match, because two dye lots of the same print can differ enough to show in daylight.

Binding yardage: small, but easy to get wrong

The binding is the strip that wraps around the raw edge of the quilt and finishes it, and it is always the smallest yardage number — but it is the one quilters run out of most often, because the calculation has three steps and people skip one. The binding is cut in 2.5-inch strips across the width of the fabric, joined end to end into one long strip, folded in half, and sewn around the perimeter of the quilt. The formula is: total strip length needed equals the quilt perimeter plus 10 inches for joins and corners; strips needed equals that total divided by 40 inches of usable strip length per width of fabric; yardage equals strips times 2.5 inches, divided by 36.

Worked for the queen: the perimeter is twice the width plus twice the length, or 2 times 90 plus 2 times 108, which is 396 inches. Add 10 inches for the joins and the corners, and the strip length needed is 406 inches. Each strip cut across a 44-inch bolt gives about 40 usable inches, so 406 divided by 40 is 10.15, rounded up to 11 strips. Eleven strips at 2.5 inches wide is 27.5 inches of fabric, which is 0.76 yards, so buy 1 yard — and buying the full yard is the right call, because the binding is the last piece cut and there is no fabric left to cut it from if the estimate is short. A yard of binding fabric costs $12, and a quilt that stalls at the binding stage costs the whole project.

Two binding variations change the number. A wider binding, 2.75 inches instead of 2.5, adds about 10 percent to the yardage — a queen goes from 1 yard to about 1.1 yards, so still buy 1.25 yards to be safe. A bias binding, cut on the diagonal for curved edges or scallops, needs about 40 percent more fabric than a straight-grain binding, because the strips are cut across the diagonal and much of the fabric is wasted between strips — a queen bias binding needs about 1.5 yards. Scalloped and curved quilts are the only ones that need bias binding; a straight-edged quilt is always bound on the straight grain, and the chart above assumes that.

Batting, borders and sashing: the extras

The batting is the middle layer, and it is sold by the size, not by the yard — a crib batting is about $10, a twin about $20, a queen about $30 and a king about $40 for standard cotton or an 80/20 cotton-polyester blend, and the premium wool and bamboo battings run roughly double those prices. Batting is cut to the same size as the backing, 4 inches larger than the top on every side, and it is bought as a pre-cut package or off a wide roll, so there is no yardage calculation: buy the package labelled for your quilt size, and check that it is at least 8 inches larger than your finished top in both directions. A queen batting is typically 90 by 108 inches or larger; if your top is 96 by 108 inches, buy the king batting and trim it.

Borders and sashing are the two extras that add yardage to the top. A 6-inch border around a queen top needs about 1.5 yards of border fabric: the two side borders are each 108 inches long, and the top and bottom borders are each 102 inches long once the side borders are on, so the total is 420 inches of 6.5-inch strips, which is 6 strips of 44-inch fabric, or 39 inches of fabric, which is 1.08 yards plus allowance, so buy 1.5 yards. A 3-inch sashing between the blocks of a queen top needs about 1 yard, depending on how many sashing strips the pattern calls for. Both are calculated the same way as the binding — total strip length divided by 40, times the strip width, divided by 36 — and both are bought as separate yardage, not taken from the top allowance. The free Fabric Yardage Calculator handles borders and sashing as separate line items for exactly this reason.

Pattern repeat deserves its own paragraph, because it is the one factor that can double the top yardage without changing the quilt size. A large-scale print with a 24-inch repeat means each cut length is rounded up to a whole number of repeats: a 108-inch queen length becomes 120 inches, which is 5 repeats, and on a three-width top that adds 36 inches, or 1 yard, before the allowance. A directional print — one with an obvious up and down, like a landscape or a row of houses — cannot be rotated to save space, so the pieces must all run the same way and the waste is higher still. For a bold print with a large repeat, budget 15 to 20 percent extra on the top instead of 10 percent, and buy it all from the same bolt. The width-first formula is exact; the repeat is the variable that breaks it if you forget it.

The same queen built entirely from 44-inch fabric — top, backing and binding all off the narrow bolt — needs 21 yards and costs about $282: 10 yards of top at $120, 10 yards of backing at $120, 1 yard of binding at $12, and the same $30 batting. The wide backing saves 6.5 yards and $67, and it removes two long seams from the back of the quilt. That is the single most valuable decision in the whole project, and it is made at the fabric store, before a single piece is cut.

For comparison, a twin quilt built the same way needs 5.5 yards of top at $66, 3 yards of 108-inch backing at $45, 0.75 yards of binding at $9, and a $20 batting, for a total of $140 — and a king needs 10 yards of top at $120, 7 yards of 108-inch backing at $105, 1 yard of binding at $12, and a $40 batting, for a total of $277. The king costs only $62 more than the queen in materials, because the top yardage is identical and the extra cost is all in the backing and the batting. The crib is the cheapest quilt in the chart at about $52 in materials: 1.5 yards of top at $18, 1.75 yards of backing at $21, half a yard of binding at $6, and a $10 batting — which is why crib quilts are the standard first quilt, and why they make such good gifts.

Frequently asked questions

How many yards of fabric do I need for a queen size quilt?

A queen quilt, 90 by 108 inches, needs about 10 yards for the top from 44-inch quilting cotton, 3.5 yards of 108-inch backing fabric, and 1 yard for the binding — 14.5 yards in total plus a packaged queen batting. If the backing also comes off a 44-inch bolt, the total rises to 21 yards, because the backing alone needs 10 yards. The free Fabric Yardage Calculator runs the same width-first formula on your exact measurements.

How much fabric do I need for a twin quilt?

A twin quilt, 72 by 90 inches, needs 5.5 yards for the top, 3 yards of 108-inch backing (or 6 yards of 44-inch backing), and 0.75 yards for the binding — 9.25 yards in total with wide backing, plus a twin batting. The top is 2 widths of 44-inch fabric, because 72 inches fits inside two usable widths of 43 inches each.

How many yards for a king size quilt?

A king quilt, 108 by 108 inches, needs 10 yards for the top — the same as a queen, because both need three widths of 44-inch fabric — plus 7 yards of 108-inch backing, or 10 yards of 44-inch backing, and 1 yard for the binding. The total is 18 yards with wide backing, plus a king batting. The king costs more than the queen only in the backing and the batting, not in the top.

How much backing fabric do I need for a quilt?

Backing is cut 4 inches larger than the finished top on every side, so a queen needs a 98 by 116 inch backing. From 108-inch wide backing fabric that is 3.5 yards; from 44-inch fabric it is 10 yards, because the backing needs three widths. Buy 108-inch backing for anything bigger than a twin — it saves 6.5 yards on a queen and removes two long seams from the back.

How much fabric do I need for quilt binding?

Binding yardage comes from the perimeter: total strip length equals the perimeter plus 10 inches, divided by 40 inches per strip, times 2.5 inches wide. A queen has a 396-inch perimeter and needs 11 strips, or 1 yard; a twin needs 0.75 yards and a crib needs 0.5 yards. Buy the binding with the top fabric, not after it, so the dye lots match.

How do I calculate quilt yardage myself?

Fit the width first: widths needed equals the project width divided by the usable fabric width (about 43 inches for a 44-inch bolt), rounded up. Then yardage equals widths times the cut length in inches, divided by 36, plus a 10 percent allowance. Work each piece separately — top, backing with its 8-inch overhang, and binding from the perimeter — and add them up.
