Pattern Repeat: Why Patterned Fabric Needs More Yardage

If you are wondering why pattern repeat yardage always comes out higher than the plain-fabric estimate, the answer is simple: every cut length has to start at the same point in the pattern, so each piece is rounded up to a whole number of repeats. A curtain panel that needs 92 inches of fabric with a 27-inch repeat is cut at 108 inches — four full repeats — and the extra 16 inches per panel is fabric you buy but never see in the finished curtain. On a pair of panels that is 32 inches, or almost a full yard, and on a queen quilt top with a 24-inch repeat it is a whole extra yard. This guide explains what a pattern repeat is, the round-up formula that prices it, worked numbers for curtains, quilts, dresses and cushions, and how to buy so the pattern lines up at every seam.

What is a pattern repeat?

A pattern repeat is the distance before a printed design starts over. On a floral fabric with a 24-inch vertical repeat, the same flower appears every 24 inches down the length of the bolt, and on a striped fabric with a 13.5-inch horizontal repeat, the same stripe sequence repeats every 13.5 inches across the width. The vertical repeat is the number that costs you yardage, because it runs along the length you are cutting; the horizontal repeat matters when two widths of fabric are joined side by side and the pattern has to line up across the seam. Both numbers are printed on the end of the bolt and usually on the selvage, written as something like “V 24 in. / H 13.5 in.”, and online listings show them as “Repeat: 24 in. vertical, 13.5 in. horizontal”. If a listing shows no repeat at all, the fabric is usually a solid, a texture or a random-match print, and the repeat math in this guide does not apply to it.

Three kinds of repeat behave differently at the cutting table. A straight match means the motifs line up in straight rows and columns, so the same flower sits at the same height on every width — the easiest kind to match, and the most common on florals and geometrics. A half-drop match means every second column is shifted down by half a repeat, so the flower on the next width sits 12 inches lower on a 24-inch repeat — harder to match, and the one that catches beginners out, because the pattern looks straight until the widths are joined. A random match means the print has no obvious repeat to line up — small dots, abstract textures, tossed leaves — and pieces can be cut anywhere, so it needs no extra yardage at all. A fourth case is not a repeat type but a direction: a one-way or directional print, like a landscape, a row of houses or a line of animals, has an obvious up and down, and the pieces cannot be rotated to save space, which adds waste on top of the repeat itself. The formula: round every cut up to a whole repeat

The whole of pattern repeat yardage is one rule: each cut length is rounded up to a whole number of repeats. The formula is repeats per cut equals the cut length divided by the repeat, rounded up; fabric per cut equals repeats times the repeat; and the extra fabric is the fabric per cut minus the cut length. The cut length is the finished length plus hems and seam allowances, exactly as in the plain-fabric calculation — the repeat rounding is applied after the hems are added, not before. A 24-inch repeat never adds a fixed number of inches; it adds whatever is needed to reach the next whole multiple of 24, which can be anything from nothing to just under 24 inches.

Two things jump out of the table. First, a small repeat can cost nothing: a 6-inch or 12-inch repeat divides evenly into a 60-inch cut, so the fabric is cut exactly at the length needed and there is no waste at all. Second, the waste is not proportional to the repeat size — it depends on the remainder. An 18-inch repeat and a 24-inch repeat both waste exactly 12 inches on a 60-inch cut, because both round up to 72 inches, while a 27-inch repeat wastes 21 inches on the same cut, because it rounds up to 81. The rule to carry to the shop is that the extra per cut is always less than one full repeat, and the worst case is a cut that is one inch longer than a whole number of repeats — a 61-inch cut on a 24-inch repeat rounds up to 72 inches and wastes 11, while a 73-inch cut rounds up to 96 and wastes 23. That is why the same fabric can be cheap on one project and expensive on another: the waste is decided by how the cut length lands against the repeat, not by the repeat alone.

The other half of the formula is that the rounding happens on every cut length, not once per project. A quilt top cut in three widths pays the rounding three times, once per width; a pair of curtains pays it twice, once per panel; a dress with a front, a back and two sleeves pays it four times. That is why the repeat hurts wide projects most: the more separate lengths a project is cut into, the more times the rounding is charged. The worked examples below show the charge on four real projects.

Worked example 1: a curtain pair with a 27-inch repeat

Take a window 60 inches wide with an 84-inch finished drop, covered by two panels of 54-inch decorator fabric at $15 a yard. The width math comes first: at 1.5 times fullness the panels need 90 inches of fabric width in total, or 45 inches per panel, and one width of 54-inch fabric gives about 53 usable inches, so each panel is a single width. The length math is next: the finished drop is 84 inches, plus 4 inches for the top hem and 4 inches for the bottom hem, which is a 92-inch cut length before the repeat. With a 27-inch repeat, 92 divided by 27 is 3.41, rounded up to 4 repeats, and 4 times 27 is 108 inches per panel. Two panels at 108 inches is 216 inches, which is exactly 6 yards — buy 6 yards.

Without the repeat, the same pair would need 2 times 92 inches, or 184 inches, which is 5.11 yards, so buy 5.25 yards. The repeat adds 32 inches, or 0.89 yards, and at $15 a yard that is about $13 extra on the pair. The same pair with a 24-inch repeat would be cheaper: 92 divided by 24 is 3.83, rounded up to 4 repeats, which is 96 inches per panel, or 192 inches total, which is 5.33 yards — buy 5.5 yards, an extra of only 8 inches and about $3. The difference between the two repeats is $10 on one pair of curtains, and it comes entirely from where 92 inches lands against 24 versus 27. If the drop had been 96 inches instead of 84, the 24-inch repeat would divide evenly — 4 repeats exactly — and cost nothing extra at all, while the 27-inch repeat would still round up to 108. Check the repeat against your drop before you buy, not after: a drop that lands on a whole number of repeats is the cheapest curtain you will ever make. Worked example 2: a queen quilt top with a 24-inch repeat

A queen quilt top is 90 inches wide and 108 inches long, made from 44-inch quilting cotton, and the repeat math is the same as the curtains, charged three times instead of twice. The width comes first: 90 inches divided by 43 usable inches is 2.09, rounded up to 3 widths, because two widths give only 86 inches. The length is next: each width is cut to the 108-inch quilt length, and with a 24-inch repeat, 108 divided by 24 is 4.5, rounded up to 5 repeats, which is 120 inches per width. Three widths at 120 inches is 360 inches, which is 10 yards. Without the repeat the top would need 3 times 108 inches, or 324 inches, which is 9 yards — so the repeat adds 36 inches, or exactly 1 yard, and at $12-a-yard quilting cotton that is $12 extra on the top alone.

The backing and the binding do not pay the repeat charge if they are cut from plain fabric, which is the usual choice: backing is normally a solid or a small random print, and binding is a solid that matches the top. But if the backing is cut from the same large-scale print as the top, it pays the same rounding on its own cut lengths — a queen backing cut 116 inches long from 44-inch fabric would round up to 120 inches per width, the same 4 inches per width, and add another yard. The lesson is to keep the patterned fabric where the pattern shows — the top — and cut the hidden layers from plain fabric. The full queen yardage, top, backing and binding together, is charted in the quilt yardage guide, and the fabric width guide explains the usable-width rule that decides the three-width count in the first place.

Worked example 3: a dress and a set of cushions

A simple shift dress shows the repeat on a small project. The front and back are each 45 inches long, cut from 44-inch fabric with an 18-inch repeat. Each piece rounds up separately: 45 divided by 18 is 2.5, rounded up to 3 repeats, which is 54 inches per piece. Two pieces at 54 inches is 108 inches, or 3 yards, against 90 inches, or 2.5 yards, without the repeat — an extra 18 inches, or half a yard, which is about $8 at $15 a yard. The sleeves and facings are small pieces cut from the same fabric, and they are usually cut from the offcuts between the big pieces, so they rarely add yardage of their own — but only if the print is not directional. On a one-way print the sleeves must run the same way up as the body, so they cannot be rotated into a spare corner, and the dress needs an extra quarter yard or so for them.

A set of four 20-inch square cushions shows the repeat at its smallest. Only the fronts are patterned; the backs are plain. Each front is cut 22 inches square, allowing a seam allowance, and with a 12-inch repeat, 22 divided by 12 is 1.83, rounded up to 2 repeats, which is 24 inches per front. Four fronts at 24 inches is 96 inches, or 2.67 yards, against 88 inches, or 2.44 yards, without the repeat — an extra 8 inches, or 0.22 yards, which is about $3. The cushion is the project where the repeat barely matters, because the pieces are small and the repeat is small relative to them. The rule of thumb holds across all four examples: the repeat costs the most when the pieces are long and the repeat is large, and the least when the pieces are short and the repeat is small.

That is small money next to the fabric itself, but it is exactly the amount that leaves a project one piece short when it is forgotten — and a project that runs out of a patterned fabric is worse than a project that runs out of a solid, because the replacement has to come from the same dye lot to match. Match types compared: straight, half-drop, random and one-way

Not every patterned fabric charges the same repeat price, because the match type decides how much freedom the cutter has. The table below ranks the four match types by the extra yardage they typically need, as a percentage of the plain-fabric estimate.

Match typeHow it lines upTypical extraWatch out for
Random matchNo alignment needed; cut anywhere0–5%None — the cheapest patterned fabric to cut
Straight matchMotifs align in straight rows and columns10–15%Seams must match across widths
Half-drop matchAlternate columns shifted by half a repeat10–20%Easy to match wrong; check the offset before cutting
One-way / directionalPrint has an up; pieces cannot be rotated15–25%Nap and direction waste on top of the repeat

The half-drop deserves a closer look, because it is the match type that produces the most expensive mistakes. On a half-drop fabric with a 24-inch repeat, the motifs on the second width sit 12 inches lower than on the first width, so a seam that looks matched at the top of the curtain is 12 inches out at the bottom unless the second width is slid down by half a repeat before cutting. The slide costs up to half a repeat of extra fabric on that width, and it is easy to miss, because the fabric looks like a straight match until the widths are laid side by side. Always lay the widths out on the floor and check the match at the seam before cutting anything — the check takes two minutes and it is the difference between a curtain that looks made and a curtain that looks wrong from across the room.

The one-way print is the most expensive match type because it removes the cutter's best trick: rotation. On a non-directional print, a piece that does not fit one way can be turned 90 degrees or upside down to fit the spare space; on a landscape or animal print, every piece must run the same way up, so the offcuts are unusable and the waste is higher. Directional prints also have a nap effect — the print looks darker from one end than the other — so two pieces cut in opposite directions will look like two different fabrics even when they came off the same bolt. If the project has many small pieces, like a patchwork quilt or a set of cushion covers, a directional print can add a quarter more fabric than the plain estimate, and a random-match or straight-match print is the cheaper choice.

Matching across the seam: the horizontal repeat

The vertical repeat decides how long each cut is; the horizontal repeat decides how the widths join. When a project is wider than one width of fabric — a wide curtain, a quilt top, a tablecloth — two or more widths are sewn together, and the pattern has to continue across the seam as if it were one piece. The second width is slid up or down until its motifs line up with the first width's motifs, and the slide can cost up to one full vertical repeat on the second width. On our curtain example with a 24-inch repeat, joining two widths can add up to 24 inches to the second width alone, which is two-thirds of a yard on a wide window with three or four widths.

Three practical rules keep the seam match cheap. First, buy the widths from the same bolt and in order — the shop cuts them consecutively, so the pattern continues from one width to the next and the slide is small. Second, match at eye level, not at the top: the seam is judged where people look, at the middle of the curtain or the middle of the quilt, so align the motifs there and let the top and bottom take the difference, where the hem hides it. Third, on a quilt top, piece the widths so the seam falls where the pattern is busiest — a seam through a dense floral reads as part of the print, while a seam through a plain patch of background reads as a mistake. The seam match is the one part of pattern repeat yardage that cannot be calculated exactly in advance, because it depends on where the shop cut the bolt; budget half a repeat per joined width as an allowance, and treat anything less as a saving.

Buying rules for patterned fabric

The cutting math is only half the job; the buying rules are the other half, and they are what keep a patterned project from stalling. Buy all of the fabric at once, from the same bolt, because two dye lots of the same print can differ enough to show in daylight — a curtain panel from one lot next to a panel from another lot will look like two different curtains. Buy the repeat allowance with the main fabric, not as a separate trip: the 15 to 20 percent extra is part of the order, and a shop that has sold the rest of the bolt by the time you come back cannot help you. Ask the shop to cut on the repeat — a cut that starts at the same motif on every length makes matching easier and can save a repeat per length, because the pieces all start from the same point. Keep a swatch: cut a single repeat, about 24 inches, from the start of the bolt and keep it with the pattern, so you can check the dye lot and the repeat if you ever need more. And use plain fabric for the parts nobody sees — cushion backs, curtain linings, quilt backings — because every yard of patterned fabric hidden inside a project is a yard that paid the repeat charge for nothing.

One more buying rule, and it is the one that saves the most money: check the repeat before you fall in love with the print. A 27-inch repeat on an 84-inch drop wastes 16 inches per panel, while a 12-inch repeat on the same drop wastes 4 inches — and both prints can look identical from across the shop. The repeat is printed on the bolt end and in the online listing; read it the way you would read the price, because on a wide project it is part of the price. A small repeat is not a compromise: small-scale prints are easier to match, waste less fabric, and hide seams better than large-scale prints, which is why experienced sewers reach for them on anything wider than a single width.

Frequently asked questions

How much extra fabric do I need for pattern repeat?

Round each cut length up to a whole number of repeats, then add the pieces up. As a rule of thumb, add 15 to 20 percent to the plain-fabric estimate for a repeat over 12 inches, and 10 percent for a smaller repeat. The worst case is just under one full repeat per cut length — a 24-inch repeat can add up to 24 inches per piece — and the charge is paid once per cut, so wide projects with many separate lengths pay it several times. The free Fabric Yardage Calculator gives the plain-fabric starting figure to add the repeat allowance to.

What is a pattern repeat in fabric?

A pattern repeat is the distance before a printed design starts over: the vertical repeat runs down the length of the bolt and the horizontal repeat runs across its width. A 24-inch vertical repeat means the same motif appears every 24 inches down the fabric. Both numbers are printed on the end of the bolt and in online listings, written as something like “V 24 in. / H 13.5 in.”. Solids and random-match prints have no repeat to match.

How do I calculate fabric with a 24-inch repeat?

Take the cut length — finished length plus hems and seam allowances — divide it by 24 and round up to a whole number, then multiply back by 24. A 92-inch curtain cut length divided by 24 is 3.83, rounded up to 4 repeats, which is 96 inches per panel. A 108-inch quilt length divided by 24 is 4.5, rounded up to 5 repeats, which is 120 inches per width. Do the rounding separately for every cut length in the project.

Does pattern repeat matter for solid fabric?

No. Solid fabric, textured fabric and random-match prints have no motif to line up, so pieces can be cut anywhere on the bolt and no extra yardage is needed beyond the usual 10 percent cutting allowance. The repeat charge applies only to prints with a straight, half-drop or directional match — which is why solids are the cheapest choice for linings, cushion backs and quilt backings.

What is a half-drop pattern repeat?

A half-drop repeat is a print where every second column of motifs is shifted down by half a repeat — 12 inches on a 24-inch repeat. It is harder to match than a straight repeat, because the second width has to be slid by half a repeat before the motifs line up, and it typically needs 10 to 20 percent extra fabric. Check the bolt end for “half drop” before cutting, and lay the widths side by side to confirm the offset.

Can I avoid the extra yardage from pattern repeat?

Partly. Choose a small repeat or a random-match print, which can cost nothing extra; pick a cut length that lands on a whole number of repeats; use plain fabric for backs and linings; and ask the shop to cut on the repeat so every length starts at the same motif. What you cannot avoid is the rounding itself — once a patterned fabric is chosen, every cut length has to be a whole number of repeats, and the extra is the price of the pattern lining up.
