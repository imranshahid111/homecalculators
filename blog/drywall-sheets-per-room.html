How Many Sheets of Drywall for a 10×10, 12×12 and 20×20 Room?

Drywall sheets per room is the first number on any drywall order, and it is also the number most often guessed wrong — usually by exactly one or two sheets, which is exactly enough to cost you a second delivery trip or a weekend lost waiting for the lumberyard to open. The short answers, for a standard room with 8 ft ceilings and 4×8 sheets: a 10×10 room needs about 16 sheets, a 12×12 room needs about 19 sheets, and a 20×20 room needs about 37 sheets, waste included. Those numbers count walls plus ceiling, and they include a 10% waste allowance for cuts, breakage and offcuts you cannot reuse. This guide shows the full math behind each number, the counts for 9 ft ceilings and 4×12 sheets, what to order alongside the sheets (screws, mud, tape), and the measuring mistakes that leave even careful buyers short.

The formula: wall area + ceiling area, divided by sheet area

Drywall estimating is area math with one rounding rule: always round up, per room, never per wall. A standard 4×8 sheet covers 32 square feet; a 4×12 sheet covers 48 square feet. Wall area is the room's perimeter times its ceiling height. Ceiling area is length times width. Add them, divide by the sheet area, round up to the next whole sheet, then add waste — 10% for a simple rectangular room, 15% if the room has lots of corners, angles, closets or soffits.

Take the 10×10 room at 8 ft ceilings as the worked example. Its perimeter is 10 + 10 + 10 + 10 = 40 linear feet. Wall area is 40 × 8 = 320 square feet. Ceiling area is 10 × 10 = 100 square feet. Total drywall area is 420 square feet. In 4×8 sheets that is 420 ÷ 32 = 13.1, which rounds up to 14 sheets before waste. Add 10% waste (1.4 sheets, rounded up to 2) and the order is 16 sheets. Every count in this guide follows that same path, so you can re-run it for any room size of your own: perimeter × height, plus length × width, divided by 32 or 48, rounded up, plus waste.

One deliberate choice in that math: do not subtract doors and windows. A standard interior door opening is about 21 square feet and a typical window about 15 square feet, and it is tempting to deduct them. Resist it. Drywall is hung in full sheets and cut around openings on the wall, so the offcut from a door opening is rarely a usable full sheet elsewhere — and the offcut pieces around windows are exactly the fiddly pieces most likely to break. Treat openings as your waste buffer instead: the sheets you "save" by deducting openings are the sheets that cover your miscuts. Estimators who deduct every opening are the ones making a second trip.

Read the 20×20 row carefully, because it surprises people: the ceiling alone is 400 square feet, more than the entire wall-and-ceiling area of the 10×10 room. Large rooms are ceiling-heavy, which is why ceiling sheet choice matters more there — 4×12 sheets on a 20 ft ceiling span mean fewer butt joints across the middle of the room, and fewer joints means less taping and a flatter-looking ceiling. On a 10×10 ceiling, 4×8 sheets are fine; the ceiling is small enough that a couple of extra joints disappear under texture or paint.

What about a room that is not square? The formula does not care about shape, only perimeter and area. A 10×12 room has a 44 ft perimeter, so at 8 ft ceilings its walls are 352 square feet and its ceiling is 120 square feet: 472 square feet total, 15 sheets before waste, 17 with waste. A 12×16 room has a 56 ft perimeter: 448 + 192 = 640 square feet, 20 sheets before waste, 22 with waste. An L-shaped room works the same way — measure each wall run, add them for the perimeter, and measure the ceiling in rectangles and add them — but bump waste to 15%, because every inside and outside corner generates offcuts.

9 ft ceilings: the extra foot costs more than you think

Nine-foot ceilings add one foot of wall height around the whole perimeter, and on a big room that is a lot of drywall. The same three rooms at 9 ft need noticeably more sheets, and 4×8 sheets stop fitting neatly: a 9 ft wall needs a full 8 ft sheet plus a 1 ft strip, which doubles the horizontal joints on every wall. That is the main reason 4×12 sheets, hung horizontally, are popular in 9 ft rooms — hung sideways, a 12 ft sheet covers more wall per joint, and a 9 ft wall takes two horizontal courses with far fewer seams.

The 4×12 column deserves a practical note: 12 ft sheets are heavy (about 75–80 pounds each in 1/2 inch, over 100 pounds in 5/8 inch), awkward in stairwells, and may not fit around a tight hallway corner or into a small elevator. Before ordering 12-footers, walk the delivery path with a tape measure: doorways, stair turns and hallway widths decide the sheet length, not the price list. Many DIY jobs settle on a split — 4×12 sheets for long straight walls and the ceiling, 4×8 sheets for short walls and tight rooms — and that split is perfectly fine as long as the total area still adds up.

4×8 vs 4×12 sheets: which to buy

Sheet length is a joints-versus-handling trade-off, not a quality choice. Longer sheets mean fewer butt joints (the end-to-end joints that are hardest to finish flat), which means less taping, less mud and a smoother wall. Shorter sheets mean easier carrying, easier cutting, easier solo handling, and less waste on short walls where a 12 ft sheet would leave a long offcut. The rule of thumb: use the longest sheet you can physically get into the room and maneuver onto the wall. For 8 ft ceilings hung horizontally, 4×8 sheets give two courses per wall with a joint at 4 ft — fine, and the joint lands at a comfortable working height. For 9 ft ceilings, 4×12 sheets hung horizontally reduce a wall to fewer, longer courses. For ceilings, longer sheets shine on rooms wider than 12 ft, where a 4×8 ceiling forces a butt joint mid-room.

Thickness is the other sheet choice, and it is mostly decided for you. Standard interior walls take 1/2 inch drywall. Ceilings take 1/2 inch on joists 16 inches on center, but 5/8 inch if joists are 24 inches on center (to resist sagging between supports) or where fire separation is required — garage ceilings and walls adjoining a garage typically require 5/8 inch Type X fire-rated board by code. Bathrooms take moisture-resistant board (often green or purple) in wet zones, though standard board is fine for the rest of a bathroom. Thickness changes weight and price but not the sheet count: the area math is identical, so order the same number of sheets in the thickness each surface needs.

Closets, soffits, niches and other sheet-eaters

The table counts assume four plain walls and a flat ceiling. Real rooms add surfaces, and each one eats sheets. A standard reach-in closet (about 6 ft wide, 2 ft deep, 8 ft tall) adds roughly 2 sheets: its side walls and back wall plus a small ceiling. A walk-in closet (say 5×7 ft) adds roughly 5–7 sheets, because it is effectively a small room of its own. A soffit or bulkhead — the boxed-in run above kitchen cabinets, for example — adds about 1 sheet per 8 linear feet of soffit, counting its face and underside. A tray ceiling or coffered detail adds sheets in proportion to its extra surface area, and angled or vaulted ceilings should be measured as their actual sloped surface, not their floor footprint: a vaulted ceiling over a 12×12 room can easily be 200+ square feet instead of 144.

The safe way to handle these is to measure them as separate mini-rooms and add their sheet counts to the main room's count, then apply waste once to the total. Do not fold them into the room's perimeter — a closet's walls are not part of the room's perimeter, and folding them in understates the area. If you are drywalling a whole house or a basement with many rooms, estimate room by room and add a single 5–10% project-level waste allowance on top, rather than padding every room: padding every room compounds and can leave you with a stack of spare sheets you paid to deliver and then paid to return.

What to order with the sheets: screws, mud, tape and corner bead

Sheets are only half the order. A 4×8 sheet takes roughly 32 screws when hung on studs 16 inches on center (about 6 screws per stud across 5 studs, plus ceiling screws on joists), so a 10×10 room at 16 sheets needs roughly 500 screws — buy a 1-pound box per 8–10 sheets as a rough guide, or a 5-pound box for a 20×20 room. Screws should be 1-1/4 inch coarse-thread for 1/2 inch board on wood studs, 1-5/8 inch for 5/8 inch board; on metal studs use fine-thread screws. Nails are still used by some crews, but screws hold better and are far easier for a DIYer to correct — a proud nail pops, a proud screw just gets driven a touch deeper.

Joint compound estimates assume two to three coats over all seams plus screw heads, which is the standard finish for paint-grade walls. A level-5 skim coat over the whole surface — sometimes specified for glossy paint or critical lighting — can double mud usage, so do not order for a skim coat on a standard estimate. Corner bead goes on every outside corner: count outside corners (a plain rectangular room has none on the inside, but door and window returns, soffits and closet openings create them) and buy one 8 ft stick per corner, plus a few spares — corner bead is cheap and a bent stick is useless. Inside corners need no bead, just tape and mud.

2026 cost snapshot: sheets, materials and hanging

Sheet prices in 2026 run roughly $13–$18 per 4×8 sheet of 1/2 inch board at big-box retail, $18–$25 for 4×12 sheets, and $20–$30 for 5/8 inch Type X. Screws run about $8–$12 per 5-pound box, a 5-gallon bucket of all-purpose joint compound about $18–$25, and a 250 ft roll of paper tape about $5–$8. That puts the materials for a 10×10 room at roughly $300–$450, a 12×12 room at $360–$540, and a 20×20 room at $700–$1,050, before corner bead, primer and paint. Professional hanging and finishing typically adds $2–$4 per square foot of drywall area in 2026 — so roughly $850–$1,700 of labor on a 10×10 room, $1,050–$2,100 on a 12×12, and $2,100–$4,200 on a 20×20 — with the usual regional spread. The labor range is wide because finishing level drives it: a garage-grade finish costs far less than a level-4 paint-grade finish, and texture hides imperfections that a smooth finish charges extra to eliminate.

The money-saving insight in all of this is delivery. Drywall delivery is often free above a minimum order (commonly 20–30 sheets) and charged below it, and a second delivery for the two sheets you forgot can cost $50–$100. That is why the waste allowance exists and why rounding up matters: two spare sheets sitting in the garage cost about $30; a second delivery costs more than that and a lost day on top. If you finish with spare sheets, keep one or two for future repairs — a matching offcut is exactly what you want when a doorknob punches through a wall in two years.

The mistakes that leave you short

First, measuring the room's floor dimensions but forgetting the ceiling — the single most common error, and it understates a 20×20 room by nearly 40%. Second, deducting doors and windows, which spends your waste buffer before you cut a single sheet. Third, measuring wall-to-wall instead of stud-to-stud or forgetting that a room's drywall wraps inside corners: inside measurements are what you want, since drywall covers the framing, not the finished room. Fourth, ordering exactly the computed count with no waste — the math gives you a minimum, not an order quantity. Fifth, forgetting the closet, which is really a small room hiding inside the big one. Sixth, assuming every wall is 8 ft: a room with a dropped section, a beam soffit or a sloped ceiling has more area than its floor plan suggests. Seventh, mixing up sheet sizes mid-order — a 4×12 sheet covers 50% more area than a 4×8, so a count computed for one size is wrong for the other. Run the formula once for the sheet size you are actually buying.

One more practical trap: stud spacing. Drywall edges must land on framing, and standard sheets are sized for studs 16 inches on center — a 4 ft sheet spans exactly three stud bays. If your framing is 24 inches on center, sheets still work (two bays), but 1/2 inch board on 24-inch ceiling joists sags over time, which is why 5/8 inch is specified there. If your framing is irregular — an old house with studs at odd spacing — expect more cuts, more waste, and more offcuts that do not land on a stud. Check a few stud bays with a tape before ordering, especially in renovations, and bump waste to 15% if the framing is not a clean 16-inch grid.

Estimate disclaimer: Counts are planning estimates for standard rectangular rooms; irregular rooms, vaulted ceilings, closets and soffits add area. Confirm sheet thickness and fire-rating requirements with your local building code before ordering, and confirm quantities with your supplier.

Frequently asked questions

How many drywall sheets do I need for a 10×10 room?

About 16 sheets of 4×8 drywall for a 10×10 room with 8 ft ceilings, walls plus ceiling, including 10% waste. Without waste the math is 14 sheets (420 sq ft ÷ 32 sq ft per sheet). At 9 ft ceilings, order about 17 sheets of 4×8, or 11 sheets of 4×12.

How many drywall sheets for a 12×12 room?

About 19 sheets of 4×8 drywall for a 12×12 room with 8 ft ceilings, including waste (528 sq ft total, 17 sheets before waste). At 9 ft ceilings, order about 20 sheets of 4×8, or 14 sheets of 4×12.

How many drywall sheets for a 20×20 room?

About 37 sheets of 4×8 drywall for a 20×20 room with 8 ft ceilings, including waste (1,040 sq ft total, 33 sheets before waste). At 9 ft ceilings, order about 39 sheets of 4×8, or 27 sheets of 4×12. The ceiling alone is 400 sq ft, so ceiling sheet choice matters most in large rooms.

Should I use 4×8 or 4×12 drywall sheets?

Use the longest sheet you can physically get into the room and onto the wall. 4×12 sheets mean fewer butt joints and less taping, which matters most on 9 ft walls and ceilings wider than 12 ft. 4×8 sheets are lighter, easier to handle solo, and better for short walls and tight delivery paths.

Do I subtract doors and windows from the drywall count?

No — do not deduct openings. Drywall is hung in full sheets and cut around openings, so the offcuts are rarely reusable, and the pieces around windows are the most likely to break. Treat openings as part of your waste allowance instead.

What thickness drywall should I use?

Use 1/2 inch for standard interior walls, 1/2 inch for ceilings on 16-inch joist spacing, and 5/8 inch Type X where code requires fire separation (garage ceilings and adjoining walls) or on ceilings with 24-inch joist spacing to prevent sagging. Use moisture-resistant board in wet bathroom zones.

How many screws per sheet of drywall?

About 32 screws per 4×8 sheet on studs 16 inches on center. A 10×10 room at 16 sheets needs roughly 500 screws; a 20×20 room at 37 sheets needs roughly 1,200. Use 1-1/4 inch coarse-thread screws for 1/2 inch board on wood studs.
