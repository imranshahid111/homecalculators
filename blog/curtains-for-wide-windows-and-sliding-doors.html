Curtains for Wide Windows and Sliding Doors: Panel Math

Curtains for wide windows follow different math from curtains for ordinary windows, and the difference is not a small rounding error — it is the difference between a window that looks dressed and a window that looks under-dressed, and between a rod that holds and a rod that sags. On a 36-inch bedroom window, two panels and a basic rod are enough, and every sizing mistake costs at most one extra panel. On a 96-inch sliding door or a 120-inch wall of glass, the same mistakes cost three extra panels, a rod that bows in the middle, and a stack of gathered fabric that covers a third of the glass when the curtains are open. This guide works out the panel math for wide windows step by step — rod length, panel count, stack-back, bracket spacing and cost — for a 72-inch picture window, a 96-inch sliding door and a 120-inch wall of glass, so you can buy the right panels and the right rod the first time.

The short answer: three numbers change when the window gets wide

Everything about sizing curtains for a wide window comes down to three numbers, and all three grow with the window. The first is the rod length. The rod is not the window width: it is the window width plus about 5 inches of trim allowance plus an extension of 10 to 12 inches on each side, and on a wide window you want the wide end of that extension, because the stack of gathered panels is wider and needs more rod to sit on. The second is the panel count, which is the rod length times the fullness divided by the panel width, rounded up to whole panels. The third is the stack-back: the width the panels take up on the rod when they are open, which is the panel count times the stack per panel, split across the two sides. On a standard window the stack is a small share of the window; on a wide window the stack is the number that decides how much glass stays visible, and it is the number most people forget to check.

Those three numbers are connected, and the connection is what makes wide windows tricky. More panels mean more fabric and more cost, but they also mean a wider stack, which means less visible glass, which means you want a longer rod, which means more panels. The way out of the loop is to pick the style first, because the style sets the fullness, and the fullness sets the panel count: grommet and tab top need 1.5 to 2 times fullness, rod pocket and pinch pleat need 2 to 2.5 times, and back tab needs about 2 times. The worked examples below all use 2 times fullness, the middle of the range, so the panel counts are comparable; if you are buying grommet panels at 1.75 times, subtract roughly one panel from each example. The style comparison itself — fullness, stack per panel and cost for each header — is in Grommet vs Rod Pocket vs Pleated: How Style Changes Size, and the placement rules for how high the rod goes and how far it extends are in How High to Hang Curtains (Decorator Rules).

The three formulas, in order

First, the rod length. Rod length equals window width plus 5 inches of trim allowance plus 2 times the extension, where the extension is 10 inches on each side for a standard window and 12 inches on each side for a wide window or a sliding door. A 72-inch window therefore needs a rod of 72 plus 5 plus 24, which is 101 inches — call it 100 inches, since rods are sold in 8-inch steps and the nearest sizes are 96 and 104. A 96-inch sliding door needs 96 plus 5 plus 24, which is 125 inches, and a 120-inch wall of glass needs 120 plus 5 plus 24, which is 149 inches, or about 150 inches. The extension is not decoration: it is where the open panels sit, and on a wide window the stack is wide enough that a 10-inch extension leaves a foot of panel hanging on the glass on each side.

Second, the panel count. Total fabric needed equals rod length times fullness, and the panel count is the total fabric divided by the panel width, rounded up to whole panels. Standard ready-made panels are 54 inches wide. At 2 times fullness, the 100-inch rod for the 72-inch window needs 200 inches of fabric, and 4 panels give 216 inches, which is 2.16 times — right. The 125-inch rod for the sliding door needs 250 inches of fabric, and 5 panels give 270 inches, which is 2.16 times — also right. The 150-inch rod for the wall of glass needs 300 inches of fabric, and 6 panels give 324 inches, which is 2.16 times — the same ratio, because the math scales. The rounding matters: 4 panels on the 100-inch rod is 2.16 times, but 3 panels would be 1.62 times, which is too flat for 2-times fabric. Never round down on panel count — a flat curtain on a wide window looks worse than a flat curtain on a narrow one, because there is more of it to see.

Third, the stack-back. Stack per side equals the panels per side times the stack per panel. A 54-inch grommet panel stacks to about 12 to 14 inches of rod when it is open, a rod pocket panel to 9 to 11 inches, and a pinch pleat panel to 8 to 10 inches. With 4 panels on the 72-inch window, 2 per side at 13 inches each, the stack is 26 inches per side. The rod extension is 12 inches, so about 14 inches of panel sits on the glass on each side, and the visible glass is about 44 inches of the 72-inch window. That is the honest trade of a wide window: even with the rod extended as far as it should be, a fifth of the glass is covered when the curtains are open. The fix is not a longer rod — the extension cannot grow past about 12 inches without the rod looking wrong — it is a narrower-stacking style, or accepting the coverage.

Worked example 1: a 72-inch picture window

Take the common living-room picture window: 72 inches wide, 60 inches tall, 8-foot ceiling. The rod is 101 inches — the 72-inch window plus 5 inches of trim plus 12 inches of extension on each side — so buy a 96 to 120-inch adjustable rod and set it at 100 inches. At 2 times fullness the fabric needed is 200 inches, and 4 standard 54-inch panels give 216 inches, which is 2.16 times. Split 2 panels per side, which is the even split every wide window wants: an odd panel count on a two-sided window means one side has an extra panel and the two sides hang unevenly, so if the math gives an odd number, round up to the next even number. Here the math already gives 4, so the split is clean.

The stack is 2 panels per side at 13 inches each, or 26 inches per side. The extension is 12 inches, so 14 inches of panel sits on the glass on each side, and the visible glass is 72 minus 28, or 44 inches. That is the number to check before you buy: if 44 inches of visible glass out of 72 feels too little, switch to pinch pleat panels, which stack to 9 inches each, giving a stack of 18 inches per side, 6 inches on the glass, and 60 inches of visible glass — at custom prices of $80 to $150 per panel, or $320 to $600 for the set, against $140 to $220 for the ready-made grommet set. The rod is a 1 to 1.25-inch diameter rod at $40 to $70, and it needs 3 brackets: one at each end and one in the centre, because a rod over about 72 inches long sags in the middle under the weight of the panels without a centre support. The brackets add $15 to $25. All in, the 72-inch window is $200 to $330 in grommet, or $380 to $700 in pinch pleat.

The length for this window is the standard floor-length drop: the rod sits 4 to 6 inches above the frame, the panel length is the rod-to-floor distance, and the grommet header adds about 2 inches above the rod, so buy the rod-to-floor length plus 2 inches. The length rules for sill, apron, floor and puddle drops are in Curtain Length Guide: Sill, Apron, Floor or Puddle?, and the panel-count math for the same window is worked out in What Size Curtains Do I Need?.

Worked example 2: a 96-inch sliding door

Now the case that causes the most returns: a 96-inch sliding glass door, 80 inches tall, 9-foot ceiling. The rod is 125 inches — the 96-inch door plus trim plus 12 inches of extension on each side, the top of the range, because the stack is large and the door handle needs clearance. At 2 times fullness the fabric needed is 250 inches, and 5 standard 54-inch panels give 270 inches, which is 2.16 times. But 5 is an odd number, and a two-sided window with an odd panel count hangs unevenly, so round up to 6 panels, 3 per side, giving 324 inches of fabric, which is 2.59 times fullness — fuller than the minimum, and right for a door, where the curtain is closed at night for privacy and the fullness matters. The set is 6 panels at $35 to $55 each, $210 to $330.

The stack is 3 panels per side at 13 inches each, or 39 inches per side. The extension is 12 inches, so 27 inches of panel sits on the glass on each side when the curtains are open, and the visible glass is 96 minus 54, or 42 inches — less than half the door. That is the worst stack ratio of any common window, and it is why sliding doors are the one place where the style choice matters more than the panel count: pinch pleat panels stack to 9 inches each, giving a stack of 27 inches per side, 15 inches on the glass, and 66 inches of visible glass. The other fix is a traverse rod, which pulls the panels to the sides on a cord and holds them flat against the wall, so the stack sits off the glass entirely. A traverse rod for a 125-inch span is $60 to $120, and it is the right hardware for any door that is opened and closed all day.

The sliding door has two rules the picture window does not. The first is the style: grommet, back tab, or pinch pleat on a traverse rod all slide freely, and rod pocket and tab top grip the rod and have to be walked along by hand — on a door that opens twenty times a day, that means the curtain is either left closed, left open, or pulled off the rod within a month. The second is the projection: the rod brackets need a 3 to 4-inch projection so the curtain clears the door handle and the sliding panel, and the rod must be 1.25 inches in diameter with 3 brackets, one at each end and one in the centre, because the 125-inch span and the weight of 6 panels will bow a 1-inch rod. All in, the sliding door is $290 to $470 in grommet on a standard rod, or $360 to $570 with a traverse rod.

Worked example 3: a 120-inch wall of glass

The widest common case is a 120-inch wall of glass — a three-panel slider or a bank of windows with no break between them — 120 inches wide, 80 inches tall, 9-foot ceiling. The rod is 149 inches, call it 150 inches, which is past the longest single adjustable rod most stores sell: standard rods top out at about 144 inches, so a 150-inch span needs either a rod spliced from two sections with a centre connector, or two separate rods meeting in the middle, or a ceiling-mounted track. A track is the best answer at this width: it is rated for the weight, it spans the full width without a centre bracket in the middle of the glass, and the carriers glide instead of sliding on a rod. A 150-inch ceiling track is $90 to $180, and the panels hang from carriers that clip into the header.

At 2 times fullness the fabric needed is 300 inches, and 6 standard 54-inch panels give 324 inches, which is 2.16 times — an even split, 3 panels per side. The stack is 3 panels per side at 13 inches each, or 39 inches per side, and the extension is 12 inches, so 27 inches of panel sits on the glass on each side, and the visible glass is 120 minus 54, or 66 inches. On a wall this wide, the honest answer is that the stack is a feature, not a flaw: 39 inches of gathered fabric on each side frames the glass the way a valance frames a window, and trying to eliminate it means a narrower-stacking style and a much higher price. Pinch pleat at 9 inches per panel gives a stack of 27 inches per side and 84 inches of visible glass, at $480 to $900 for 6 custom panels. The ready-made grommet set is 6 panels at $35 to $55 each, $210 to $330, plus the track at $90 to $180, for $300 to $510 all in.

At this width, the weight is the number to check: 6 panels of mid-weight fabric weigh about 12 to 18 pounds, and the rod or track has to carry that weight plus the pull of opening and closing. A spliced rod with a centre connector sags at the joint unless it has a centre bracket, and a centre bracket in the middle of the glass is a bracket the panels have to slide past, which means the panels cannot cross it. That is why the track wins at 120 inches and above: no centre bracket, no joint, and the carriers pass each other freely. If you do use a rod, put the centre bracket at the point where the panels meet in the middle and accept that the panels stop there — or use two rods, one per side, each with its own pair of brackets.

Bay windows and corner windows

A bay window is three windows at angles — usually a wide centre window and two narrower side windows at 30 or 45 degrees — and it is sized as three separate windows, not one wide one. Measure each section separately, work out the rod length and panel count for each section, and hang each section on its own rod or on a bay window rod kit, which is a set of rods joined by corner connectors that bend at the bay angle. A typical bay is a 48-inch centre window and two 24-inch side windows: the centre needs a 77-inch rod and 3 panels, each side needs a 53-inch rod and 2 panels, for 7 panels total. The corner connectors hold the rods at the bay angle, and the panels on the side sections stack into the corners, which is where the fabric goes when the curtains are open — the bay's own geometry hides the stack.

A corner window — two windows meeting at a 90-degree corner with no wall between them — is sized the same way: two separate rods meeting at the corner with a corner connector, or two rods that overlap at the corner by 4 to 6 inches so the panels can cross the corner. The overlap is the cheaper fix and the one that works best, because a corner connector at 90 degrees leaves a gap the light comes through, and the overlap closes it. In both cases, the mistake is to treat the bay or the corner as one window and buy one long rod: a straight rod cannot bend, and the panels cannot turn the corner.

Rod hardware: diameter, brackets and projection

The rod is a structural choice on a wide window, not a decorative one. The diameter sets how much weight the rod carries without bowing: a 1-inch rod is fine up to about 60 inches and 3 panels, a 1.25-inch rod carries up to about 120 inches and 6 panels, and anything wider than that wants a 1.5-inch rod or a track. The bracket spacing sets where the rod is supported: brackets at the ends and then every 48 to 60 inches along the span, so a 100-inch rod needs 3 brackets, a 125-inch rod needs 3, and a 150-inch rod needs 4 — or a track, which needs none in the glass. The bracket count in the table above follows that rule, and the cost of the extra brackets, $5 to $10 each, is the cheapest insurance on the whole job.

The projection — how far the rod sits from the wall — matters on a sliding door and on any window with a handle, a crank or a deep sill. A standard bracket projects 2 to 3 inches, which clears a flat window frame but not a sliding door handle. A sliding door needs a 3 to 4-inch projection, which comes from an extended bracket or a bracket with an adjustable arm, at $10 to $20 per bracket instead of $5 to $10. The projection also matters for layered curtains: a sheer layer and a blackout layer on the same window need a double rod, with the sheer on the back rod and the blackout on the front rod, and the two rods need a combined projection of 4 to 6 inches so the layers do not catch on each other. The layered look is the right answer for a wide window that needs both light and privacy, and it is cheaper than one heavy blackout set: sheers at 2.5 to 3 times fullness on the back rod, blackout at 2 times on the front.

Panel layout: two panels, four panels, or one-way draw

On a wide window, the layout is a choice with real consequences. Two panels, one per side, is the cheapest layout and the one most people buy first, and it is wrong for any window over about 72 inches: one panel per side at 2 times fullness on a 100-inch rod is 2 panels of 54 inches, 108 inches of fabric, which is 1.08 times — flat, skimpy, and visibly under-dressed. Four panels, two per side, is the right layout from 72 inches up: the panels overlap in the middle when closed, the stack is even on both sides, and each side can be opened independently. Six panels, three per side, is the right layout from 96 inches up, for the same reasons, and it is the layout that lets you open just the middle panels for a partial view.

The one-way draw is the alternative layout for a sliding door: all the panels stack on one side, and the curtain opens in one direction, like the door itself. It needs the same panel count, but the stack is all on one side — 6 panels at 13 inches is 78 inches of stack on one side, which needs a rod extension of 12 inches plus a wall of at least 66 inches beside the door to hold it. The one-way draw is right when the door has a wide wall on one side and a narrow wall on the other, and it is wrong when both sides are narrow, because the stack has nowhere to go. A centre split — panels opening to both sides — is the default, and it is right for every window with a wall on both sides.

The first two mistakes are the ones that send people back to the store, and they are the same mistake in opposite directions: the rod is too short, or the panels are too few. The third mistake is the one that shows up a month later, when the rod has bowed and the panels no longer slide. Measure the window, work out the rod, the panels and the stack in that order, and check the stack against the glass before you buy — the three formulas at the top of this guide are the whole job.

Frequently asked questions

How many curtain panels do I need for a 96-inch window?

Five panels at 2 times fullness with standard 54-inch panels, rounded up to 6 panels so the split is even — 3 per side. The rod is 125 inches, the fabric needed is 250 inches, and 5 panels give 270 inches, but 5 is an odd number, so 6 panels at 324 inches is the even split. On a sliding door, 6 panels is also the right count because the door is closed at night and the extra fullness matters.

How wide should a curtain rod be for a sliding door?

The rod should be the door width plus 5 inches of trim plus 12 inches of extension on each side. For a 96-inch sliding door that is 125 inches. The rod should be 1.25 inches in diameter with 3 brackets, and the brackets need a 3 to 4-inch projection so the curtain clears the door handle.

Do wide windows need a centre bracket?

Yes, from about 72 inches up. A rod over 72 inches long sags in the middle under the weight of the panels without a centre support. Put brackets at the ends and then every 48 to 60 inches along the span: 3 brackets for a 72 to 125-inch rod, 4 brackets for a 150-inch rod. Over 144 inches, a ceiling track is better than a rod, because it needs no centre bracket in the glass.

Can one curtain rod span 12 feet?

Not as a single rod — standard rods top out at about 144 inches, and a 150-inch span needs a spliced rod with a centre connector, two rods meeting in the middle, or a ceiling track. The track is the best answer at 120 inches and above: it carries the weight, it spans the full width without a centre bracket, and the carriers glide instead of sliding on a rod.

How much glass will the curtains cover when open?

It depends on the stack: the panels per side times the stack per panel, minus the rod extension. On a 96-inch sliding door with 6 grommet panels, the stack is 39 inches per side and the extension is 12 inches, so about 27 inches of panel sits on the glass on each side and the visible glass is about 42 inches. Pinch pleat panels stack narrower, at about 9 inches each, and give about 66 inches of visible glass on the same door.

Should wide windows use one panel per side or two?

Two panels per side from 72 inches up, three per side from 96 inches up. One panel per side on a 100-inch rod is 108 inches of fabric, which is 1.08 times fullness — flat and skimpy. Two panels per side gives 216 inches, which is 2.16 times, the right fullness, and the stack is even on both sides.
