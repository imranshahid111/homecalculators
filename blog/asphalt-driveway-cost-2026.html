Asphalt Driveway Cost in 2026: New vs Resurface

The asphalt driveway cost question has two answers, and mixing them up is how people pay for a full replacement when a resurface would do. A brand-new asphalt driveway in 2026 runs about $3–$7 per square foot installed; resurfacing (an overlay on sound existing pavement) runs about $2–$4 per square foot. The material itself is the cheap part — one 20×40 driveway needs roughly $1,100–$1,800 of mix — so the spread between a $2,400 quote and a $5,600 quote is almost never the asphalt. This guide works through real budgets for three driveway sizes, what actually moves a quote, and the decision rule that picks new vs resurface.

The short answer: the 2026 numbers

For a straight, rectangular residential driveway with reasonable access, these are the 2026 US planning ranges contractors are actually quoting:

The headline takeaway: on a standard 20×40 two-car driveway (800 sq ft), new construction lands roughly $2,400–$5,600 and an overlay lands roughly $1,600–$3,200. A $2,400 quote for new construction is not a steal unless you know which end of the specification it sits on — the base work, thickness and prep are the quote, the asphalt is the ingredient.

Two things jump out. First, the overlay discount is real but not magical — you save roughly 30–45% by reusing the existing pavement as your base, because the base work is the expensive half of a new job. Second, long driveways amplify every per-square-foot difference: the $3–$7 range spans $9,600 on a 12×200 drive. On a job that size, three comparable quotes matter more than any guide ever will.

Now the material cross-check, because it is worth feeling the gap between mix and invoice. A 20×40 driveway at 3 inches needs about 16 tons of asphalt (length × width × depth ÷ 166 — see the worked math in our asphalt tonnage guide). At $70–$110 per delivered ton, the mix is roughly $1,120–$1,760. The remaining $1,300–$3,800 of a new-construction quote is base aggregate, excavation, equipment, and crew labor. That is why "the asphalt price went up" can never justify a $2,000 quote jump — the mix is at most a third of the invoice.

New vs resurface: the decision rule

An overlay only works when the existing driveway is a sound base wearing a tired surface. Asphalt fails in two completely different ways, and only one of them is overlayable:

Sign of failureWhat it meansNew or resurface?
Surface cracking, fading, minor ravelingOxidation and age; structure underneath is fineResurface — ideal candidate
Isolated potholes, sunken patchesLocal base problems in a few spotsResurface with patching of the bad spots first
Alligator (spiderweb) cracking across large areasThe pavement is flexing — the base or subgrade has failedFull replacement — an overlay cracks right through within a year or two
Drainage problems: ponding, edge crumblingWater is destroying the baseFull replacement with drainage fixed, or the new driveway dies the same death
Heaving, frost damage, soft spots you can feel underfootSubgrade instabilityFull replacement — excavation and re-compacted base required

The honest rule contractors use: resurface only if the existing pavement is at least 75% sound and the failures are surface-level. If you can trace the crack pattern into a map of the base underneath — widespread alligatoring, settlement, water channels — the existing driveway is not a base, it is a problem wearing a costume, and paving over it just schedules the same job again in two years. One more hard constraint: a driveway that already sits high relative to the garage apron or street may not take another 2 inches without creating a lip or drainage reversal — another vote for replacement.

What actually moves a quote

Five line items decide whether your 20×40 lands at $2,400 or $5,600:

1. Base work. Excavating old pavement, hauling it off, and placing 4–6 inches of compacted crushed aggregate is the single biggest cost on new construction — often half the invoice. Quotes that skip base prep are cheaper for exactly the reason they fail early. Always ask every bidder to state base depth and compaction in writing.

2. Thickness of the asphalt lift. Three inches costs roughly 30–50% more mix and labor than two inches on the same driveway (about 5 extra tons on a 20×40 — $350–$550 in material, more in labor for the extra lift). The cheapest quote sometimes achieves cheap by laying 2 inches where the job needed 3.

3. Prep and repairs before the overlay. On a resurface, crack filling, patching the bad spots, and edge trimming are the invisible labor. A quote that paves over unpatched alligatoring is not $800 cheaper because the contractor is efficient — it is $800 cheaper because the repair step was deleted, and the overlay will reflect-crack through the old failures within one to two seasons.

4. Access and site conditions. Tight turns, steep grades, long hauls from the street, overhead wires, landscaping to protect — every obstacle burns crew time. Remote or difficult sites can add 10–20% to labor.

5. Drainage fixes. Regrading, new culverts, or a drain along the garage apron are separate work, and they are the work that decides whether the driveway lasts 15 years or 6. A quote that includes proper drainage is not the expensive quote — it is the only complete one.

Regional labor markets swing the whole range: dense metro areas with high crew costs sit near the top of the $3–$7 band; areas with many competing pavers sit near the bottom. Material prices matter less than people assume — but distance from the plant matters, because asphalt is hauled hot and plants charge by the load.

When is the cheapest time to pave?

Paving season runs roughly April through November in most of the US, and timing shifts price for a simple reason: plants batch hot mix in warm weather, and pavers' crews are booked months out. Early spring and late fall are typically the softest pricing — crews want work as the season ramps up or winds down. Mid-summer is peak demand, peak price, and peak waiting list. There is a floor to this: asphalt needs roughly 50°F and rising ground temperatures to compact properly, so the cheapest week of December is not a bargain, it is a failed driveway. The sweet spot is booking 3–4 weeks before the season's rush, not in the middle of it.

Comparing quotes: the five-line test

Three quotes for the same driveway will never use the same format, so normalize them yourself. Every comparable quote should state these five lines explicitly; if one does not, ask until it does:

1. Total square footage measured. Verify it yourself — a generous "about 900 sq ft" vs a measured 800 sq ft is a 12% phantom surcharge.
2. Base specification. New work: how many inches of compacted aggregate, and whether existing pavement is removed and hauled. Overlay: what patching and crack repair is included before paving.
3. Asphalt thickness, compacted. Confirm inches AFTER rolling, and whether 3-inch jobs are placed in one lift or two (two lifts compact better in cold weather).
4. Edge and drainage work. How edges are finished, and whether the quote fixes the grade that was pooling water.
5. Warranty in writing. What is covered, for how long, and what voids it — a two-year workmanship warranty is the honest minimum on new construction.

The red flags that should end the conversation: a quote with no stated thickness, a "we'll see the base when we get there" attitude, pressure to sign before the plant ticket is checked, or a price more than 30% below the other two bids. That discount is coming from somewhere, and the somewhere is usually the base course or the asphalt thickness.

DIY math: is paving it yourself worth it?

The material numbers seduce everyone at least once. For the 20×40 driveway: ~16 tons at $70–$110 delivered = $1,120–$1,760 in mix, plus base aggregate and rental of a roller, spreader box and a compacted base. Under about 5 tons, plants add short-load fees ($50–$150), and minimums of 1–2 tons make small jobs oddly expensive per ton. The honest DIY case is the patch or the apron extension — a 10×10 patch at 2 inches is ~1.5 tons and a weekend. A full driveway needs a crew spreading hot mix before it cools, a roller on site the same hour, and timing you cannot fake. Most homeowners find the DIY full-driveway math saves the labor line but trades it for a learning-curve driveway — the crew's speed is genuinely part of the product.

Frequently asked questions

How much does an asphalt driveway cost in 2026?

A new asphalt driveway costs roughly $3–$7 per square foot installed in 2026 — $2,400–$5,600 for a standard 20×40 two-car driveway. The spread comes from base work, asphalt thickness, site access and region, not the price of the mix itself.

How much does it cost to resurface an asphalt driveway?

Resurfacing (a 1.5–2 inch overlay on sound existing pavement) costs roughly $2–$4 per square foot in 2026 — $1,600–$3,200 for a 20×40 driveway. It only works if the existing pavement is structurally sound; alligator cracking and base failures need full replacement.

Is it cheaper to resurface or replace an asphalt driveway?

Resurfacing costs about 30–45% less than new construction because the old pavement serves as the base. But paving over failed pavement is the most expensive "saving" in the book — an overlay over alligator cracking reflects through within one to two years, and then you pay for the replacement anyway.

Can you pave over an existing asphalt driveway?

Yes, if at least 75% of the existing pavement is sound and the failures are surface-level (fading, minor cracking). Cracks get filled and bad spots get patched first. Do not overlay alligator cracking, settlement, or drainage-caused failures — and check that another 2 inches of height will not create a lip at the garage or street.

How long does an asphalt driveway last?

About 15–20 years with a proper base, correct thickness and sealcoating every 2–3 years. A driveway that fails in 6–8 years almost always has a base problem, a thickness problem, or a drainage problem — rarely an asphalt problem.

Why do asphalt driveway quotes vary so much?

Three quotes differ because they are selling different jobs: different base depths, different compacted thicknesses (2 in vs 3 in), different prep work on overlays, and different site-access assumptions. Ask every bidder to state square footage, base spec, compacted thickness, edge/drainage work and warranty — then the prices compare fairly.

How many tons of asphalt does my driveway need?

Tons = length (ft) × width (ft) × depth in inches ÷ 166. A 20×40 driveway at 3 inches compacted is 14.5 tons exactly — order 16 with the 10% allowance. Our asphalt tonnage guide works through five driveway sizes.
