Nominal vs Actual Lumber Sizes: Why a 2×4 Isn't 2×4

The 2x4 actual size is 1.5 inches × 3.5 inches — a full half inch smaller on each face than the name promises. This is not a scam and not a mistake: every piece of dimension lumber in the store is sold by its nominal size (what it was when rough-sawn) and delivered at its actual size (what survives drying and planing). This guide explains where the missing inches go, gives you the complete nominal-vs-actual chart for every common size, and works the math showing how those shrunken dimensions change your board feet, your material bill, and real framing decisions like insulation depth.

Where the missing half inch goes

A 2×4 starts life as a rough-cut board that really is close to 2 × 4 inches — a little oversize, in fact, because the saw leaves a ragged surface. Then it goes through the kiln. Green lumber leaves the kiln drier and measurably smaller as the wood fibers lose moisture. Then the board passes through the planer, which shaves the rough faces down to a smooth, uniform finish. Each face loses roughly a quarter inch to the planer: that is where the 2-inch thickness becomes 1.5 inches, and where the 4-inch width becomes 3.5 inches.

The sizes you see are not arbitrary leftovers of this process. They are standards, set and enforced through the American Lumber Standard Committee's product standard (PS 20), which defines the actual minimum dimensions for every nominal size of softwood lumber. A 2×4 from one mill and a 2×4 from another are both 1.5 × 3.5 inches because the standard says so — which is why the chart below is safe to trust for any store-bought framing lumber in the US.

One wrinkle to know: rough-sawn lumber, the kind you buy straight from a small local mill, has not been planed, so its actual dimensions sit much closer to the nominal ones — a rough 2×4 really is about 2 × 4 inches. If your project mixes rough stock and surfaced stock, measure every piece rather than trusting the name on either one.

The one place the "nominal equals actual" habit comes closest to being true is 5/4 decking stock, which surfaces to a true 1-inch thickness.

Worked math: how the shrink changes your board feet

Board feet are computed from actual dimensions — Thickness × Width × Length / 12 — as our board-foot guide shows. Computing with the nominal size inflates the number by a wide margin. Run the same 8-foot 2×4 both ways:

  • Nominal math: 2 × 4 × 8 / 12 = 5.33 board feet.
  • Actual math: 1.5 × 3.5 × 8 / 12 = 3.50 board feet.

The nominal calculation overstates the real wood content by more than 50%. If your yard quotes $1.80 per board foot, the nominal math prices that one stud at $9.60 while the actual math prices it at $6.30 — a $3.30 phantom surcharge per board that never existed in the wood you took home.

Scale that to a project. Say you need two hundred 2×6×10s for a deck frame. Nominal math says 200 × (2 × 6 × 10 / 12) = 2,000 board feet. Actual math (1.5 × 5.5 × 10 / 12 per board) says 1,375 board feet. At $1.80 per board foot the nominal total is $3,600 and the real total is $2,475 — a $1,125 error built entirely from measuring wood you never had. Nobody at the yard is cheating you; the trap is purely in the arithmetic, and the fix is purely to use the chart above.

Where it bites in real framing: the insulation example

Dimensions matter beyond pricing. Consider wall insulation. A 2×4 exterior wall has a cavity depth of 3.5 inches, not 4 — and that half inch decides which insulation product fits. Standard R-13 fiberglass batts are manufactured 3.5 inches thick, exactly for the 2×4 cavity. If you planned the wall around a 4-inch cavity and ordered R-15 batts (3.5 inches thick but compressed differently) or assumed you could fit deeper high-density batts, the actual 3.5-inch space makes the decision for you.

The same half inch governs plumbing, electrical, and hardware. Recessed medicine cabinets, electrical boxes sized for "2×4 walls," and door jamb extensions are all manufactured around the 3.5-inch reality. Door jambs for a 2×4 wall come 4-9/16 inches wide — that is the 3.5-inch stud plus two half-inch drywall layers. Every one of those products would be wrong if the wall were truly 4 inches thick. When in doubt, measure one stud with a tape rather than trusting the nominal name.

Switching stud size makes it concrete: a 2×6 wall gives a 5.5-inch cavity that takes R-19 or R-21 batts, and an R-value gain like that is part of the real cost argument for 2×6 framing — you cannot make that tradeoff without the actual numbers.

The plywood surprise: sheet goods do it too

Nominal-vs-actual is not just a dimension-lumber habit. A "1/2-inch" sheet of plywood is really 15/32 inch thick (about 0.47 in); "3/4-inch" is really 23/32 inch. The undersize comes from the sanding of the faces after the veneers are laid up, and it is baked into the standard.

Where it bites: stacking two layers for a subfloor (the stack-up lands thinner than two nominal sheets), cabinet joinery cut to exact dado depths, and tile-underlayment math where a 1/8-inch-per-layer shortfall compounds across rooms. Plywood is sold by the sheet and measured per sheet, so the cost trap is smaller than with board feet — the trap is fit, not price. Measure the stack with calipers before you rout the dados.

Hardwoods and the quarters system

Hardwood lumber from a hardwood dealer uses a different naming system — quarters of an inch — and it has its own nominal-vs-actual gap. A board sold as 4/4 ("four-quarter") is 1 inch thick rough, and after surfacing it typically measures 13/16 inch. Common mappings:

Hardwood nameRough thicknessSurfaced thickness
4/41 in13/16 in
5/41-1/4 in1-1/16 in
6/41-1/2 in1-5/16 in

Two warnings here. First, hardwood is often sold rough or "skip-planed," meaning the boards you bring home can vary from the surfaced numbers above — always measure hardwood yourself. Second, hardwood widths are random — there is no "6-inch wide" oak board at the hardwood dealer, so the width column of the framing chart has no hardwood equivalent. This is exactly why hardwoods are priced by the board foot rather than by length: the unit handles random widths fairly.

Mistakes people make with nominal sizes

Using nominal sizes in the board-foot formula. The most expensive version of this habit — the 2×6×10 project above shows a $1,125 swing. Always use the chart's actual column.

Assuming engineered lumber matches. LVL, glulam and I-joists are manufactured products, not sawn lumber, and their dimensions follow their own tables — a 1-3/4 × 11-7/8 LVL is what the label says it is, but you cannot read it as "a 2×12." Never swap an engineered product into framing math using the nominal chart.

Designing to the nominal depth. Cabinets, countertops, and recessed fixtures sized to a "4-inch" wall land wrong against a 3.5-inch cavity. Measure the framing you actually have, especially in older houses where pre-1960s lumber was often true-dimensioned and newer work is not.

Mixing rough and surfaced stock. Rough-sawn 2×4s really are ~2 × 4; surfaced 2×4s are 1.5 × 3.5. A fence or outbuilding framed with a mix of both ends up with uneven faces unless you plane the rough stock down to match.

Assuming the chart covers every mill's 4×4. Standard dressed 4×4s are 3.5 × 3.5, but some big-box stores sell "4×4" landscape timbers and treated posts at slightly different actual sizes. For fence posts, read the tag on the specific product — our Fence Material Calculator works from the standard 3.5-inch post, but the store tag has the final word.

Frequently asked questions

What is the actual size of a 2x4?

A standard surfaced 2x4 measures 1.5 inches thick by 3.5 inches wide. The nominal 2x4 name refers to the rough-sawn size before kiln drying and planing; the finished board loses about half an inch on each face to those two steps.

Why is lumber smaller than its nominal size?

Two reasons: kiln drying shrinks the wood as moisture leaves, and the planer shaves each rough face smooth, removing roughly a quarter inch per face. The remaining dimensions are standardized by the American Lumber Standard Committee's PS 20 standard, so a 2x4 is 1.5 x 3.5 inches at any store.

Is a 2x6 actually 2x6?

No. A surfaced 2x6 measures 1.5 inches thick by 5.5 inches wide. Like all dimension lumber, it is named for its rough-sawn size and sold at its smaller finished size.

Should I use nominal or actual sizes in the board foot formula?

Always use actual dimensions. Using nominal sizes inflates the board-foot total by roughly 50% — for example, a 2x4x8 works out to 5.33 board feet nominally but only 3.50 board feet actually. That difference can change a material bill by hundreds of dollars.

Is plywood also smaller than its stated thickness?

Yes. A "1/2-inch" plywood sheet is actually 15/32 inch thick, and "3/4-inch" is really 23/32 inch, because the faces are sanded after the veneers are laid up. The difference matters for stacked subfloors and dado joinery.

Are hardwood boards the same as nominal sizes?

Hardwoods use a separate system: 4/4 ("four-quarter") is 1 inch thick rough and about 13/16 inch surfaced. Widths are random, so there is no nominal-width equivalent — another reason hardwoods are priced by the board foot.
