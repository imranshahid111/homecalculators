Wood Fence Cost Per Foot in 2026: Materials vs Installed

Wood fence cost per foot in 2026 runs about $15–$45 per linear foot for materials alone and $30–$85 per linear foot installed, for a standard 6 ft privacy fence. The spread is wide because three choices dominate the price: the wood species (pressure-treated pine at the low end, cedar in the middle, redwood at the top), the fence height, and whether a crew or you do the work. A 100 ft pine privacy fence costs roughly $1,500–$2,500 in materials or $3,000–$5,500 installed; the same fence in cedar costs roughly $2,500–$4,500 in materials or $5,000–$8,500 installed. This guide breaks down where every dollar goes, works through real 100 ft and 200 ft budgets, and shows what moves a contractor quote up or down — so you can tell a fair price from an inflated one before you sign.

The short answer: cost per foot at a glance

All figures below are per linear foot for a 6 ft privacy fence with standard 4x4 posts, two or three horizontal rails, and concrete footings, at 2026 retail prices. Gates, removal of an old fence, permits and staining are excluded — they are priced separately in the sections below, and they are the items most often missing from a "per foot" quote.

Two things jump out of that table. First, labor roughly doubles the material cost — installed prices are not materials plus a small fee, they are materials plus a crew's day or two, and labor is the single biggest line on any fence job. Second, species matters more than style: the jump from pine to cedar adds about $20 per foot installed, while the jump from a spaced picket to a privacy fence in the same wood adds only about $5–$10 per foot, because the extra boards are cheap compared with the posts, rails and labor that both styles share.

Where the materials dollar goes

A fence is not one product — it is a kit of parts, and the per-foot material cost is the sum of those parts. For a 6 ft cedar privacy fence, the breakdown per linear foot looks roughly like this: pickets $7–$11 (about 2.2 boards per foot at $3.50–$5.50 each, including waste), rails $2–$3 (three 2x4 rails shared across 8 ft post spacing), posts $3–$5 (one 4x4 post every 8 ft, amortized, plus concrete), concrete and gravel $1–$2, and fasteners, caps and hardware $1–$2. That totals $14–$23 per foot in the most stripped-down accounting — and the reason published material ranges run higher, to $25–$45, is that retail buyers pay bundle prices, buy longer posts for gate and corner positions, add post caps, and lose 10% to waste, all of which the bare parts list understates.

Pine changes the picture mainly through the pickets. Pressure-treated pine pickets run $2.00–$3.50 each against cedar's $3.50–$5.50, so the same fence in pine sheds roughly $4–$5 per foot on pickets alone. Rails and posts are usually pressure-treated pine even on a cedar fence — cedar posts rot faster in ground contact than treated pine, so most builders use treated posts under cedar pickets — which means the post and rail cost barely changes between species. That is why the pine-to-cedar gap is $10–$20 per foot rather than double: only the pickets, and sometimes the cap board, actually change wood.

Worked budgets: 100 ft and 200 ft fences

Take a straight 100 ft backyard fence, 6 ft privacy style, no gates, flat ground, no old fence to remove. In pressure-treated pine, materials run about $1,500–$2,500: roughly 220 pickets plus waste (about 240 boards) at $2.00–$3.50, 13 posts with concrete, 38 rails, and fasteners. Installed, the same fence runs $3,000–$5,500 — the crew charges roughly $15–$30 per foot for labor, which covers a day to a day and a half of work for two people, including setting posts and waiting on concrete. In cedar, the same 100 ft fence costs $2,500–$4,500 in materials and $5,000–$8,500 installed. The cedar picket bill alone is $845–$1,325 for the run before waste, and cedar crews tend to price at the higher end of the labor range because the boards demand more careful handling and sorting.

Double the length to 200 ft and the per-foot price falls slightly, because setup, delivery and the crew's fixed time spread over more feet. A 200 ft pine privacy fence runs about $2,800–$4,800 in materials and $5,500–$10,000 installed; in cedar, about $4,800–$8,500 in materials and $9,500–$16,000 installed. The per-foot saving is real but modest — roughly 5–10% — because the parts scale linearly with length; only the fixed costs dilute. Watch out for quotes that scale perfectly linearly from a 100 ft price: a contractor who quotes 200 ft at exactly twice the 100 ft price is either padding the longer job or underpricing the shorter one.

Height: 4 ft, 6 ft and 8 ft

Height moves the price in steps, not smoothly. A 4 ft fence uses shorter pickets and shorter posts, and it often uses two rails instead of three, so it runs about 25–35% cheaper per foot than a 6 ft fence in the same wood — roughly $20–$40 installed per foot in pine, $35–$60 in cedar. A 6 ft fence is the standard backyard height and the price anchor for every table in this guide. An 8 ft fence is a different job: 8 ft pickets cost disproportionately more than 6 ft ones, posts must be 10 ft or longer to keep enough length in the ground, wind load rises sharply, and many jurisdictions require a permit or an engineering check above 6 ft. Expect an 8 ft fence to cost 40–60% more per foot than a 6 ft fence — roughly $45–$80 installed per foot in pine, $70–$120 in cedar — and check the local height limit before budgeting for one, because an 8 ft fence that violates the code is a removal bill, not a fence.

What moves a contractor quote

Five factors move a quote more than any other, and they explain most of the gap between two bids for the same fence. First, terrain: a sloped lot forces stepped or racked sections, which slow the crew and add cutting waste — add 10–25% on a steep lot. Second, soil and access: rocky ground, clay that holds water, or a backyard a truck cannot reach all add labor, and hand-digging post holes in rock can double the post-setting time. Third, gates: a walk gate adds $150–$400 installed, a double drive gate $400–$1,200, including hardware — and gates are where cheap quotes hide their profit, so always get gates itemized. Fourth, old fence removal and disposal: tearing out and hauling away an old fence runs $3–$8 per foot, and some quotes silently exclude it. Fifth, region: labor rates in high-cost metro areas run 20–40% above the national range, while rural quotes can sit below it. A quote that ignores all five of these — flat, easy, no gates, no removal — is a quote for a fence that does not exist on your lot.

DIY vs pro: the honest comparison

DIY saves the labor line, which is roughly half the installed price — but only if you count your time at zero and own the tools. The real DIY bill for a 100 ft pine fence is the $1,500–$2,500 in materials plus tool costs: a post-hole auger rental ($75–$150 a day), a level, a string line, a circular saw and a nail gun or driver if you do not own them, plus delivery fees if the lumber yard charges for drop-off. A first-timer should also budget a full weekend for a 100 ft fence, not a Saturday, because post setting alone eats a day once concrete curing is counted — posts set in the morning should not carry rails until the concrete firms up. The classic DIY mistakes each cost money: posts set too shallow or out of plumb (a leaning fence within two years), pickets ordered by nominal instead of actual width (18 boards short on a 100 ft run, as our picket guide shows), and concrete mixed too wet (weak footings that crack). A pro crew's price buys speed, a warranty on workmanship, and the crew's practiced eye for grade and drainage — for most homeowners, the DIY saving is real but smaller than the sticker gap suggests, roughly $1,500–$3,000 on a 100 ft fence once tools and time are counted honestly.

The costs people forget

Four costs sit outside the per-foot price and surprise buyers after the fence is up. Staining or sealing: a cedar fence needs its first coat within a year and recoating every 2–3 years, at $1–$3 per foot per coat if hired, or $200–$400 in materials for a 100 ft fence DIY. Pine needs sealing sooner and more often, because untreated cut ends and ground contact invite rot. Permits: many cities require a fence permit at $50–$300, and some homeowner associations require approval before work starts — a stop-work order costs more than the permit. Survey and property lines: if the boundary is uncertain, a survey costs $300–$800 and is cheaper than rebuilding a fence on a neighbor's land. And maintenance: budget roughly $100–$300 a year for a 100 ft wood fence in stain, replacement boards and hardware, or the fence that cost $5,000 installed will look like a $2,000 fence within five years.

How to cut cost without cutting life

The cheapest durable fence is usually a 6 ft pine privacy fence with treated posts, built at standard height on a straight run, with gates kept to one walk gate. Beyond that, four savings hold up. Buy pickets by the bundle rather than by the board — bundle pricing typically saves 10–15%. Keep the fence at 6 ft rather than 8 ft unless privacy genuinely demands the extra height; the 8 ft premium buys little extra screening for a lot of money. Use treated pine posts even under cedar pickets — the posts are underground, nobody sees them, and treated posts outlast cedar posts in soil. And schedule the build in the contractor's slow season: late fall and winter quotes in many regions run 10–15% below spring prices, because crews are hungry and lumber yards discount. What not to cut: post depth, concrete, and rail count — a fence with shallow posts or two rails where three belong is a fence you will pay to rebuild, and the rebuild costs more than the saving ever did.

Frequently asked questions

What is the average wood fence cost per foot in 2026?

About $15–$45 per linear foot for materials alone and $30–$85 per linear foot installed, for a standard 6 ft privacy fence. Pine sits at the low end ($30–$55 installed), cedar in the middle ($50–$85), and redwood at the top ($65–$100).

How much does a 100 ft wood fence cost?

About $3,000–$5,500 installed in pressure-treated pine or $5,000–$8,500 in cedar, for a 6 ft privacy fence with no gates on flat ground. Materials alone run $1,500–$2,500 in pine or $2,500–$4,500 in cedar.

Is cedar worth the extra cost over pine?

For most homeowners, yes: cedar costs about $20 more per foot installed but resists rot and insects far better, holds stain more evenly, and typically lasts 20–25 years against pine's 12–18. Pine wins when the budget is tight and you commit to regular sealing.

How much does a fence gate add to the cost?

A walk gate adds roughly $150–$400 installed and a double drive gate $400–$1,200, including posts, hardware and latching. Always get gates itemized in a quote — they are the most common hidden extra.

Does fence height change the cost per foot a lot?

Yes. A 4 ft fence runs about 25–35% cheaper per foot than a 6 ft fence, while an 8 ft fence costs 40–60% more per foot — and may need a permit. Six feet is the standard backyard height and the best value in most cases.

How much does it cost to remove an old fence?

Removal and disposal typically adds $3–$8 per linear foot to a job, or $300–$800 for a 100 ft fence. Check whether a quote includes it — many per-foot prices do not.
