Why New Sod Turns Yellow (and How to Save It)

New sod turning yellow is the single most common panic of first-time sod buyers — and the good news is that it is almost always fixable. In the first two weeks after installation, a sod roll has no roots reaching into your soil, so the entire lawn lives or dies on what is happening at the surface: water, heat, and contact with the ground. Yellow is the grass telling you one of those three is wrong. This guide teaches you to read the pattern of the yellowing to name the cause, gives you the exact watering math to fix it, and ends with the pull test that settles the scary question: is my sod dead?

The 30-second diagnosis: read the pattern

Before you do anything, look at where the yellow shows up. Different causes paint different pictures, and the pattern narrows the suspect list immediately:

What you seeMost likely causeConfidence
Rolls yellowing in streaks or patches along the edgesUnderwatering — edges dry firstHigh
Whole rolls going yellow evenly, soil squishy underfootOverwatering — roots drowningHigh
Yellow with crispy, scorched tips a few days after fertilizingFertilizer burnHigh
Yellow in random patches that lift up like carpet with no rootsGrubs or poor soil contactMedium
Circular yellow spots that grow wider, worse in humid weatherFungal disease (brown patch)Medium
Uniform pale yellow-green across the whole lawnNitrogen hunger (rare before week 3)Low

If the yellowing started within the first 7–10 days and shows up in streaks or along roll edges, you can stop guessing: it is a watering problem in roughly 9 out of 10 cases. The rest of this guide walks each cause with the numbers that fix it.

Cause #1: underwatering (the cause 9 times out of 10)

A fresh sod roll is a severed plant with 1 inch of root zone and no way to drink from deeper soil. For the first two weeks it needs the top 1–1.5 inches of soil kept continuously damp — not flooded, just never dried out. The single biggest killer of new sod is watering it like an established lawn (twice a week, deep) instead of watering it like a transplant (daily, shallow, constant).

The math is bigger than most people expect. New sod needs about 1 to 1.5 inches of water per day in mild weather, and up to 2 inches per day in heat above 85°F. One inch of water over 1,000 square feet equals 623 gallons. A typical suburban front lawn of 2,500 sq ft therefore needs roughly 1,550–2,300 gallons a day in the first two weeks — far more than a casual sprinkler run delivers.

That is why coverage beats duration. Most pop-up sprinkler heads put down 0.4–0.6 inches per hour. At 0.5 in/hr, delivering 1.5 inches takes 3 hours of run time a day, and it has to actually reach the edges — the first place new sod dies is the outer 6 inches of each roll, where the exposed soil edge wicks moisture away. Run the classic tuna-can test: set empty cans around the lawn, run the sprinklers an hour, and measure. Anything under 0.5 inches needs another hour. Re-do the test at the edges, not the middle.

The schedule that works: twice daily for weeks 1–2 (morning and early afternoon), then once daily for weeks 3–4, then taper to 1 inch twice a week like an established lawn. Water before the heat of the day — dawn watering loses the least to evaporation — and never let the rolls dry to the point where they curl at the corners. Curled corners on day 2 are a flashing red light: water immediately, because a roll that dries through its thatch layer can die in a single hot afternoon.

Cause #2: overwatering (drowning is quieter, but just as deadly)

The opposite mistake yellows sod too, and it is sneaky because the instinct is "yellow means dry, add water." Sod sitting in soupy soil stops rooting for a different reason: roots need oxygen, and waterlogged soil has none. The signs that you have crossed from damp to drowned: squishy ground underfoot hours after watering, a sour smell when you lift a roll corner, and yellow that is uniform across the roll rather than starting at the edges.

The fix is simple and immediate: stop watering until the top inch of soil is only moist, not wet. Lift a roll corner — the soil underneath should crumble, not drip. Skip one watering cycle and the sod usually greens back up within a week. The deeper lesson: drainage matters more than duration. If your soil is heavy clay, keep the daily total at the lower end (about 1 inch) and split it into three shorter cycles instead of one long soak.

Cause #3: fertilizer burn (the well-meaning mistake)

Nobody's sod dies of hunger in its first week — but plenty of it gets scorched by fertilizer applied too much, too soon. Sod arrives from the farm already fed, and spreading granular fertilizer on fresh rolls in week one can burn the leaf tips yellow within days. The safe rate for new sod is about 1 pound of actual nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft, delivered as a starter fertilizer (high phosphorus, like 10-18-10) at laying time, into the soil underneath — not dumped on top of the rolls.

If you already over-fertilized and see yellowing with crispy tan tips, the rescue is heavy watering: a deep soak of 1–2 inches washes excess salts down past the root zone. Do it once, then resume the normal schedule. Rule of thumb after a burn: no more fertilizer for 4–6 weeks. Nitrogen on stressed sod is like feeding a marathon runner mid-race — the plant cannot use it, and the salt load makes the stress worse.

Cause #4: poor soil contact and bad soil underneath

Sod that is not touching the soil cannot root into it. If the install crew skipped rolling, or the ground has air pockets under the rolls, you get yellow patches that feel springy and lift like carpet. The fix: roll the sod with a lawn roller (a half-filled roller weighs about 100–150 lbs and presses the roots against the soil), and water deeply right after so the soil settles into the gaps.

The soil underneath matters just as much. Sod laid on rock-hard, unamended clay or on construction debris roots slowly and yellows while it starves. The prevention is done at prep time: loosen the top 4–6 inches and rake in compost. But if the sod is already down and yellowing from bad soil, do not rip it up — keep it consistently watered and it will usually push through in 3–4 weeks, slower but alive.

Cause #5: grubs, insects and fungal disease

Pests are the last thing to check, because they are the least common in the first month — but they are easy to rule out. Grubs: lift a yellow patch corner. If there are fat white C-shaped grubs in the soil and the patch has zero root anchoring, you have grub damage; treat with a grub control product and the surrounding healthy sod will fill in. A 2,500 sq ft lawn typically needs 3–5 bags of grub treatment at roughly $25–$35 a bag.

Fungus (brown patch, dollar spot): shows as circular yellow-to-tan patches that widen over days, worst in hot, humid weather with evening watering. The prevention is free: water early morning so leaves dry by nightfall. If fungus has already started, a fungicide runs $20–$40 for the same 2,500 sq ft lawn, but stopping evening watering often halts mild cases on its own.

Notice the pattern: five of the six rescues cost under $50, and the most common one (underwatering) is fixed by water you would have paid for anyway. That is why panic-replacing yellow sod is almost always the wrong move — rescue first, replace only what the pull test says is dead.

The pull test: dead or just dormant?

This is the test that ends the worry. Grab a handful of yellow blades and pull gently:

If the blades pull out easily and the crowns (the thick base of the blades) are brown and mushy, that patch is dead. If the blades resist pulling and the crowns are still firm and white or pale green, the grass is stressed but alive — it will come back green in 2–4 weeks with the fixes above. Yellow leaves on sod are like a sunburn: the leaf is damaged, but the plant is fine as long as the crown survives.

A simpler version: fold a roll corner back. If you see white root tips poking through the soil side, the sod is rooting and the yellow top will recover. No roots after 3 weeks, blades sliding off at a touch — that is dead, and it is a replacement job, not a watering job.

When replacement is the right call: the math

Dead patches smaller than a roll do not need a pallet. Sod is sold by the roll (about 10 sq ft each, ~$5–$8 per roll in 2026) or the pallet (400–500 sq ft, $175–$450 by grass type — see the pallet price breakdown). For five dead rolls, you are looking at $25–$40 plus a trip to the sod farm. Cut the dead sod out, loosen the soil underneath, and lay the new rolls on the same watering schedule as day one.

The one rule: do not buy replacement rolls more than a day before you lay them. Cut sod starts dying on the pallet — in heat, within hours. Pick it up the morning you need it, keep it in shade, and lay it before noon. Frequently asked questions

Why is my new sod turning yellow after one week?

In about 9 out of 10 cases, yellowing in the first week is underwatering. New sod has no roots reaching into your soil, so it needs 1–1.5 inches of water per day for the first two weeks — far more than most sprinkler setups deliver by default. Yellowing that starts at the roll edges or in streaks is the classic signature. Fix your coverage (the tuna-can test), water twice daily, and expect green-up in 7–14 days.

Can yellow sod turn green again?

Yes, if the crowns are alive. Do the pull test: grab a handful of yellow blades and pull gently. If they resist and the thick base of the blade is still firm and whitish-green, the grass is stressed but alive and will recover in 2–4 weeks with correct watering. If blades slide out easily with brown, mushy crowns, that patch is dead and needs replacing.

How much should I water new sod each day?

About 1–1.5 inches per day for weeks 1–2, 1 inch per day for weeks 3–4, then taper to normal lawn watering. One inch over 1,000 sq ft is 623 gallons, so a 2,500 sq ft lawn needs roughly 1,550–2,300 gallons a day at first. Most sprinklers deliver 0.4–0.6 inches per hour, so plan on 2–3 hours of run time daily, split morning and early afternoon.

Will fertilizer fix my yellow sod?

Almost never in the first month — and applying fertilizer to stressed new sod often makes it worse through salt burn. Yellow new sod is a water, contact, or pest problem, not a nutrient problem. Sod arrives pre-fed from the farm. Wait 4–6 weeks before any feeding, and never spread granular fertilizer on week-old rolls.

How do I know if my sod is dead or just dormant?

Use the pull test: dead grass slides out of the soil easily with brown, mushy crowns; stressed grass resists pulling and keeps firm, pale crowns. A second check is the root view: fold back a roll corner after 2–3 weeks — white root tips mean it is rooting and will recover. No roots at all by week 3 plus blades that detach at a touch means it is dead.

Should I replace yellow sod or wait?

Wait and rescue first, replace only what the pull test confirms is dead. Five of the six common causes recover in 1–4 weeks for under $50. If you do need replacement, individual rolls (10 sq ft, $5–$8 each in 2026) are cheaper than a full pallet — buy them the morning you lay them, since cut sod dies on the pallet within hours in heat.
