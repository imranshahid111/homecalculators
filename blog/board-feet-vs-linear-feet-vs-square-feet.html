Board Feet vs Linear Feet vs Square Feet: What's the Difference?

Board feet vs linear feet vs square feet is the most expensive confusion in lumber buying, because each unit answers a different question: board feet measure volume (how much wood), linear feet measure length (how far it reaches), and square feet measure area (how much surface it covers). Use the wrong one and you will mis-price a project by half — or double. This guide defines all three, works the same board through all three units with real numbers, gives you a conversion cheat sheet, and shows where each unit hides a pricing trap.

The 30-second answer

Linear foot (LF)Length onlyJust the length in feetTrim, moulding, decking, fence rails

A board foot is the only one of the three that accounts for thickness. That single difference is the entire story: 10 feet of 1×2 and 10 feet of 2×12 are both 10 linear feet, but one holds four times the lumber of the other.

What a board foot really measures

One board foot equals 144 cubic inches of wood — a block 1 foot long, 1 foot wide, 1 inch thick. The formula is:

Board Feet = Thickness (inches) x Width (inches) x Length (feet) / 12

Hardwood dealers price by the board foot because it scales fairly with how much wood you actually take home. A 2-inch-thick oak board holds twice the wood of a 1-inch board of the same width and length, so per-board-foot pricing charges roughly double for it. Length-only pricing could not do that: it would treat both boards as identical.

If you want the full formula walkthrough with the nominal-versus-actual trap explained, read our earlier guide on how to calculate board feet — it has the step-by-step math, a per-size reference table, and 2026 pricing.

What a linear foot measures (and why stores love it)

A linear foot is simply a foot of length. A 10-foot board is 10 linear feet whether it is a 1×2 batten or a 2×12 beam. The unit is honest about what it does — it tells you how far the piece reaches — and it is silent about everything else.

Stores use linear-foot pricing where every piece in the pile is already the same size, so thickness and width do not need pricing in: baseboards and crown moulding, picture-frame stock, decking boards sold in a uniform width, fence rails, and chain-link or wire fencing. The big-box store prices its 2×4×8 studs per piece for the same reason — the math is fixed, so there is nothing left for a volume unit to do.

The linear foot becomes dangerous the moment you compare prices across different sizes. "Both yards sell boards at $1.50 a linear foot" sounds like a tie, but it tells you nothing until you check the cross-section. A 1×6 at $1.50/LF costs $30 per board foot of wood; a 2×12 at the same $1.50/LF costs about $10.67 per board foot. Same length price, three times the price per unit of actual lumber. Never compare linear-foot prices without knowing the thickness and width behind them.

What a square foot measures

A square foot measures area: width in feet times length in feet. Flooring, decking coverage, wall sheathing, and siding are sold by the square foot because the job is about covering a surface — how thick the material is does not change how much surface one board covers.

Thickness re-enters the picture through the price, not the measurement. Five-quarter (1-inch actual) decking costs more per square foot than standard decking, because the thicker board contains more wood per square foot of coverage. So square-foot pricing is really a two-variable system: area determines quantity, thickness determines the price tier. Whenever you compare square-foot prices on decking or flooring, check the thickness column — a cheap quote on thinner stock is not a deal.

Same board, three numbers: worked examples

Let us run one real board through all three units. Take a 2×6, 8 feet long. Its actual dressed dimensions are 1.5 inches thick and 5.5 inches wide.

  • Linear feet: 8 LF. Just the length. Nothing else.
  • Board feet: 1.5 x 5.5 x 8 / 12 = 5.5 BF.
  • Square feet (face coverage): width in feet (5.5 / 12 = 0.458 ft) x 8 ft = 3.67 sq ft. One face of the board covers about 3.67 square feet.

Same board: 8 linear feet, 5.5 board feet, 3.67 square feet. Now a chunkier one — a 2×10, 12 feet long (actual 1.5 x 9.25 in):

  • Linear feet: 12 LF.
  • Board feet: 1.5 x 9.25 x 12 / 12 = 13.88 BF.
  • Square feet: (9.25 / 12) x 12 = 9.25 sq ft.

Notice the pattern in the conversion math. Board feet to linear feet is easy: Linear Feet = BF x 12 / (Thickness x Width) — for the 2×6, 5.5 x 12 / (1.5 x 5.5) = 8 LF, exactly the board's length. Square feet from linear feet is Square Feet = LF x Width (in) / 12. Keep those two formulas on hand and you can translate any lumber quote into the unit you actually need.

Where each unit is the right tool — and where it hides a trap

Hardwood for furniture: board feet. A walnut board at $10/BF is priced on its real wood content. A 1×8×8 (3.63 BF) costs about $36 — which is $4.50 per linear foot for this board, but a different width would produce a different per-linear-foot number at the same $10/BF. Dealers who quote you a "per linear foot" price on random-width hardwood are forcing a unit onto stock it does not fit; convert to board feet to compare dealers honestly.

Trim and moulding: linear feet. Every stick of a given moulding profile has the same cross-section, so length is the only variable. The trap: profiles with different widths and thicknesses at the same per-LF price are not equal value — the beefier profile simply contains more material. That is fine for trim (you buy the look), but do not carry that habit into structural lumber.

Decking and flooring: square feet. Your deck is 240 square feet; at 6.17 sq ft of coverage per 1×10×8 board (from the table above) you need about 39 boards plus waste — the area math does the quantity work. The trap: comparing a 5/4×6 composite decking quote at $8/sq ft against pressure-treated 2×6 at $5/sq ft without registering the thickness and lifespan difference. Use the free Fence Material Calculator and board-foot math together when a fence project mixes rails (linear), pickets (area coverage) and posts (volume-grade stock).

Cost-showdown example. Two yards quote you ten 10-foot boards each. Yard A sells 1×6 at $1.20/LF: you pay $120 for 50 BF of wood — $24 per board foot. Yard B sells 2×12 at $2.40/LF: you pay $240 for 140.6 BF — $17 per board foot. The linear-foot quote made Yard A look cheaper ($1.20 vs $2.40 per foot); the board-foot math shows Yard B's wood is nearly 30% cheaper per unit of actual lumber. Convert every quote to the unit that matches the material, not the unit the seller chose.

Mistakes people make with lumber units

Comparing linear-foot prices across different sizes. Covered above — the single most expensive habit in lumber buying. Convert to price per board foot before comparing.

Ordering coverage by the wrong unit. If your project covers a surface, measure in square feet. People who order decking in "board feet" routinely over- or under-order because the deck does not care how thick the boards are — the area does.

Using nominal sizes in every formula. The conversion math uses actual dressed dimensions, exactly like the board-foot formula. A nominal 2×6 is really 1.5 × 5.5 inches, and using 2 × 6 inflates every conversion by roughly 50%. Our board-foot guide has the full nominal-vs-actual table.

Forgetting waste differs by unit. Ten percent extra length (linear) is not the same purchase as ten percent extra volume (board feet). Add your waste allowance after converting to the unit you will actually order in.

Pricing plywood in board feet. Sheet goods are sold by the sheet and priced by the square foot or per sheet — board feet is a dimension-lumber unit. Bring the wrong unit to sheet goods and the yard will politely hand you the wrong quote.

Frequently asked questions

Is a board foot the same as a linear foot?

No. A linear foot measures length only — a 10-foot board is 10 linear feet regardless of its width or thickness. A board foot measures volume (thickness x width x length / 12), so a 2x12x10 contains 20 board feet while a 1x6x10 of the same length contains only 5 board feet.

How do I convert linear feet to board feet?

Use: Board Feet = Linear Feet x Thickness (in) x Width (in) / 12. For example, 10 linear feet of 2x6 lumber (actual 1.5 x 5.5 in): 10 x 1.5 x 5.5 / 12 = 6.88 board feet. Always use the actual dressed dimensions, not the nominal size.

How many square feet does a board cover?

Square feet of face coverage = Linear Feet x Width (in) / 12. An 8-foot 1x8 (actual 7.25 in wide) covers 8 x 7.25 / 12 = 4.83 square feet of face area.

Why is lumber sometimes priced by the linear foot?

Because when every piece in a stack is the same size — trim, moulding, uniform decking boards — length is the only thing that varies, so linear-foot pricing is simple and fair. It becomes unfair when used to compare pieces of different thickness or width.

Which unit should I use for a deck project?

Measure the deck surface in square feet to get board quantities for the decking boards, then convert the framing lumber (joists, beams, posts) to board feet or count pieces. Use the board-foot formula whenever you are comparing lumber prices across different sizes.

Can I convert square feet directly to board feet?

Yes, if you know the thickness: Board Feet = Square Feet x Thickness (in) / 12. For example, 100 sq ft of 1.5-inch-thick lumber = 100 x 1.5 / 12 = 12.5 board feet. The conversion only works when thickness is uniform.
