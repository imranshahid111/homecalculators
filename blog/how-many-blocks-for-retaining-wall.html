How Many Blocks for a Retaining Wall? Row-by-Row Examples

Most people order retaining wall blocks by guessing — a pallet here, a truckload there — and end up either paying restocking fees or making a second trip with a trailer of mismatched blocks. There is a clean, exact way to size the order: count rows, count blocks per row, add the buried row nobody remembers, and tack on waste. With the industry-standard 8-inch-tall by 16-inch-long segmental block, a wall needs 0.75 blocks per linear foot per course. So a 40-foot wall, 3 feet tall, needs 6 courses × 40 feet × 0.75 = 180 blocks — plus waste, caps and the gravel underneath. This guide gives you the row-by-row formula, three fully worked walls (25×2, 40×3, and 60×4 feet), cap-stone and gravel math, and 2026 US costs — everything between you and a one-trip material order.

The formula: rows × blocks per row, then the forgotten row

A retaining wall is a stack of identical courses (rows). That makes the math mechanical once you know three things about your block: its length, its height, and whether the first course goes underground (it does — see below).

Blocks per course = (wall length in feet × 12) ÷ block length in inches. For the standard 16-inch-long block, that simplifies to length × 0.75. A 40-foot wall needs 40 × 0.75 = 30 blocks per course.

Courses = (exposed height in inches ÷ block height) rounded up + 1 buried course. For 8-inch-tall blocks: a 3-foot wall exposes 36 inches, so 36 ÷ 8 = 4.5 → 5 exposed courses, plus 1 buried = 6 courses total. You always round exposed courses up, because half a course of block height is finished with a cap course at a slightly higher elevation — you cannot build half a block.

Always round up at every step. A rounded-up fraction becomes the waste allowance instead of a shortage. Then add an explicit 5–10% waste on top for cuts at ends and corners, broken blocks, and the occasional color match that forces you to toss a unit. Walls with corners, steps or curves go at the top of that range; a straight single-length wall gets away with 5%.

The buried course: the row everyone forgets

Every retaining wall hides one full course below grade. That buried course — set into a compacted gravel trench — is what anchors the wall, keeps the bottom from kicking out, and keeps the first visible course dead level. The classic mistake is to measure the wall at grade and count only visible courses; the order then comes up one course short, which is 12–20% of the whole wall depending on height.

The trench is also where the batter (the slight backward lean) begins. Segmental blocks step back roughly 3/4 to 1 inch per course through their pin or lip system, so a 6-course wall leans back about 5–6 inches from bottom to top. That setback is structural, not cosmetic: it lets the wall lean into the hill it holds back. Because each course steps back, the top of the wall is shorter than its base footprint — a subtlety that matters for cap measurement (measure caps at the top length) but does not change the block count.

Worked example 1: 25 ft long × 2 ft tall (garden bed wall)

The classic small project: a 25-foot flower-bed wall, 2 feet of exposed height, using 8×16 blocks.

Courses: 24 inches ÷ 8 = 3 exposed courses, + 1 buried = 4 courses.
Blocks per course: 25 × 0.75 = 18.75 → round up to 19.
Total: 4 × 19 = 76 blocks. Add 5% waste (straight wall, no corners): 76 × 1.05 = 79.8 → order 80 blocks.

Caps: cap stones are usually about 12 inches long, so roughly 1 per foot → 25 feet = 25 caps, plus 2–3 extras for breakage. Gravel base: a 6-inch-deep trench, about 24 inches wide (roughly twice the block depth) → 25 × 2 × 0.5 = 25 cubic feet = 0.93 cubic yards compacted; order ~1.2 cubic yards loose. Backfill gravel (12 inches behind the wall, full height): 25 × 1 × 3 = 75 cubic feet ≈ 2.8 compacted yards, ≈ 3.6 loose.

This is a one-pallet job: standard pallets hold 60–70 large blocks or 120+ small garden-wall units, so confirm your block's pallet quantity with the supplier — the count above is what drives the order, not the pallet.

Worked example 2: 40 ft long × 3 ft tall (the yard-terrace wall)

The most common homeowner wall: 40 feet of wall stepping a yard down 3 feet.

Courses: 36 inches ÷ 8 = 4.5 → round up to 5 exposed, + 1 buried = 6 courses.
Blocks per course: 40 × 0.75 = 30.
Total: 6 × 30 = 180 blocks. Add 10% waste (ends plus a slight step): 180 × 1.10 = 198 → order 200 blocks.

Caps: 40 feet → 40–42 caps. Gravel base: 40 × 2 × 0.5 = 40 cubic feet ≈ 1.5 compacted yards (≈ 2 loose yards). Backfill: 40 × 1 × 4 = 160 cubic feet ≈ 5.9 compacted yards (≈ 7.7 loose yards, roughly 10–11 tons of gravel — this is a tandem-load, not a pickup load, so schedule delivery). Drainage: one run of 4-inch perforated pipe along the heel, daylighted at the low end or tied to a drain — about 40–45 feet of pipe.

Worked example 3: 60 ft long × 4 ft tall (the property-line wall)

At 4 feet of exposed height you are near the top of the DIY range — many jurisdictions require an engineered design or a permit above 4 feet total height, so confirm before ordering (our permit-height guide covers the rules). Assuming a straight 60-footer:

Courses: 48 inches ÷ 8 = 6 exposed, + 1 buried = 7 courses.
Blocks per course: 60 × 0.75 = 45.
Total: 7 × 45 = 315 blocks. Add 10% waste: 315 × 1.10 = 346.5 → order 350 blocks.

Caps: 60–62 caps. Gravel base: 60 × 2 × 0.5 = 60 cubic feet ≈ 2.2 compacted yards (≈ 3 loose). Backfill: 60 × 1 × 5 = 300 cubic feet ≈ 11.1 compacted yards (≈ 14.4 loose, ~20 tons — plan for staged deliveries). Drain pipe: 60–65 feet.

At this size, check two things that don't matter on small walls: the batter (7 courses × ~3/4 inch = about 5 inches of setback from base to top — keep the top of the wall inside your property line) and block delivery (350 blocks weigh roughly 10–14 tons; standard delivery is a flatbed with a forklift, not a pickup truck).

The three side orders: caps, gravel and the drain pipe

Blocks are only one of four line items, and the other three are where orders go wrong most often.

Caps. One cap per linear foot is the rule with 12-inch caps; the actual formula is the same as blocks (length × 12 ÷ cap length). Caps glue down with concrete adhesive and finish the wall — they also take the batter into account because you measure them at the top course length. Order 2–3 extra: caps crack in transit more than any other unit.

Gravel. Two jobs, two quantities: the base (6 inches deep, about twice the block depth wide, compacted in lifts) and the backfill (12 inches of clean crushed stone directly behind the wall, full height — this is the drainage column that feeds the perforated pipe). Convert compacted yards to loose yards with a 1.3 multiplier, and price in tons when the yard sells by weight: crushed stone weighs roughly 1.4 tons per loose yard.

Drain pipe. One 4-inch perforated pipe laid at the base of the backfill, on the uphill side, sloping gently to daylight or a storm tie-in. The quantity is simply wall length plus 10%. A wall without drainage is a dam, and dams fail — this is the $50 item that protects the $2,000 wall.

The mistakes that ruin the count

Forgetting the buried course. Worth restating: it is 12–20% of the block order. Every short-order story starts here.

Measuring height from the wrong side. Exposed height is measured on the low (downhill) side. On a slope, a wall can be 2 feet tall at one end and 4 feet at the other — count courses for the tall end and step the courses down as the grade rises.

Ignoring the step-down. Where the ground rises, you don't bury extra courses; you step the wall down one course at a time. Each step needs the course to start with a full unit at the low end, so add a few blocks per step — 3–5 per step is a safe planning number.

Corners cost blocks. An outside corner needs cut blocks on alternating courses (you can't wrap a 16-inch unit around a corner). Budget 5–10 extra blocks per corner. Inside corners are cheaper — they usually need just one cut per course.

Mixing pallets. Concrete block color varies slightly between production runs. If you do need a second trip, bring one of the original blocks to match the run — or better, order from the formula and make one trip.

Frequently asked questions

How many retaining wall blocks do I need per foot?

It depends on block length: 1.0 blocks per linear foot per course for 12-inch blocks, 0.75 per foot for 16-inch blocks, and 0.67 per foot for 18-inch blocks. Multiply by your course count (including the buried course) and add 5-10% waste. So a 40-foot wall of 16-inch blocks needs 30 blocks per course; at 6 courses that's 180 blocks plus waste.

Do I really need to bury the first course of blocks?

Yes — one full course below grade is the standard for segmental retaining walls. The buried course sits in a compacted gravel trench and anchors the wall against sliding and overturning. Skipping it is the most common reason walls lean or push out. It also means your block order is one full course more than the visible height — 12 to 20% more blocks depending on wall height.

How deep should the trench be for a retaining wall?

Dig the trench so the first (buried) course sits on 6 inches of compacted gravel base, with the block fully below grade — typically 7 to 8 inches deep overall for 8-inch blocks. Make the trench about twice the block depth wide (around 24 inches for 10-12 inch deep blocks) so you have working room to set and level the base course.

Do I need drainage behind a retaining wall?

Yes, on any wall holding back soil. The standard is 12 inches of clean crushed gravel directly behind the wall from base to near the top, with a 4-inch perforated drain pipe at the bottom of that gravel column, daylighted or tied into a drain. Water trapped behind a wall is the number one cause of wall failure — a $50 pipe protects a $2,000 wall.

How tall can a retaining wall be without a permit?

In many US jurisdictions, walls up to 4 feet of total height (measured from the bottom of the base to the top, on the low side) can be built without a permit or engineered design — but rules vary by city and county, and some require permits lower, especially near property lines or slopes. Always confirm with your local building department; taller walls need engineered plans.

What kind of blocks are best for a retaining wall?

Segmental concrete retaining-wall blocks (SRWs) with a pin or lip system are the standard for DIY walls — they stack dry (no mortar), step back automatically for batter, and are made for this job. Avoid plain concrete pavers or landscape edging blocks for anything structural: they lack the interlock and mass. For the standard 8x16 block, expect roughly $3 to $4.50 per block in 2026.
