How Much Does a Board Foot of Oak, Pine and Walnut Cost in 2026?

The board foot price you pay depends almost entirely on the species you pick: as of 2026, a board foot of pine costs roughly $2 to $4, oak runs about $4 to $8, and walnut typically lands between $8 and $15 — a 7x spread between the cheapest and most expensive boards in this guide. This article breaks down current per-board-foot prices for oak, pine, walnut, cherry and maple, explains the five factors that move the number up or down, and walks through real project budgets — from a $90 pine bookshelf to a $700 walnut dining table — with every line of the math shown.

What is a board foot, and why does lumber price by it?

One board foot (BF) equals 144 cubic inches of lumber — the volume of a board 12 inches long, 12 inches wide and 1 inch thick. Pricing by the board foot scales with how much wood you actually get: a 2-inch-thick plank holds twice the material of a 1-inch plank of the same width and length, so per-BF pricing charges it fairly at roughly double. Linear-foot pricing would charge the same for both, which is why hardwood dealers never use it.

If you have not run the math before, read our companion guide on how to calculate board feet — it walks through the T x W x L / 12 formula with worked examples on real board sizes. And because dealers and DIYers often talk past each other on units, board feet vs linear feet vs square feet clears up which unit applies where.

The 5 factors that move the board foot price

1. Species. The biggest driver by far. Fast-growing, widely planted species (pine, poplar) are cheap because supply is enormous. Slow-growing or less common species (walnut, cherry) cost more because fewer logs reach the market. Within one genus, white oak runs $1-2/BF above red oak mostly because of demand from white-oak flooring and furniture trends.

2. Grade. Hardwood is graded FAS, #1 Common, #2 Common and so on, based on how many clear (defect-free) cuttings a board yields. FAS (Firsts and Seconds) walnut might quote at $14/BF while #1 Common of the same species is $8-9/BF. For small projects where you can cut around knots, buying a lower grade saves 30-40% — you just need to buy more board feet to get the same clear yield.

3. Thickness. Standard 4/4 stock (roughly 1 inch) is the baseline. Thicker boards (8/4, two inches) are usually priced 10-25% higher per board foot because large, thick logs are rarer and take longer to dry. Thin stock (5/4, about 1-1/4 inches) often prices similarly to 4/4.

4. Region and supply chain. Oak and walnut grow natively in the US Midwest and Appalachia, so prices near those forests are typically at the low end of the ranges above. On the coasts, the same boards can run 20-40% higher after freight. Locally grown but imported species (like most exotics) carry the heaviest freight premium of all.

5. Season and market conditions. Lumber is a commodity. Mill shutdowns, storms that damage timber, and surges in housing demand all ripple into retail board-foot prices within a few months. Spring — when building season starts — is usually the priciest time to buy. Prices quoted here reflect 2026 conditions; if you are planning a big project, re-check your dealer's current list rather than budgeting from a stale figure.

Worked budget 1: a 6-ft pine bookshelf

Say you are building a simple freestanding bookshelf, 6 ft tall and 3 ft wide, from 1x10 pine. First, the board feet. One linear foot of a 1x10 (actual 0.75 x 9.25 in) is 0.75 x 9.25 / 12 = 0.58 BF per foot.

  • Sides: 2 pieces x 6 ft = 12 ft x 0.58 = 6.96 BF
  • Shelves: 5 pieces x 3 ft = 15 ft x 0.58 = 8.70 BF
  • Top: 1 piece x 3.5 ft = 3.5 ft x 0.58 = 2.03 BF

Subtotal: 6.96 + 8.70 + 2.03 = 17.69 BF. Add 15% waste for knots and trim: 17.69 x 1.15 = 20.34 BF, round up to 21 BF. At $3/BF for pine, the lumber budget is 21 x $3 = $63. Add screws and finish (~$25) and the whole project lands around $88 — which is why pine owns the beginner-furniture market.

Worked budget 2: the same bookshelf in red oak vs walnut

The board feet do not change — 21 BF is 21 BF regardless of species. Only the price per board foot moves:

  • Red oak at $5.50/BF: 21 x $5.50 = $115.50 in lumber — only about $52 more than pine for a dramatically nicer-looking, harder bookshelf.
  • Walnut at $10/BF: 21 x $10 = $210 in lumber — a real jump, but still less than what most ready-made walnut bookcases cost.

This is the practical way to read the price table: estimate your total board feet once, then multiply by the price of each species you are considering. The species premium for a mid-size project is often smaller than the fear of it.

Worked budget 3: a walnut dining table top

Now a bigger, thicker build — a 3 x 6 ft table top from 8/4 (2-inch) walnut, glued up from random-width boards, with legs from 8/4 stock too. First compute the top's board feet from volume: 36 in x 72 in x 2 in = 5,184 cubic inches, divided by 144 = 36 BF of net top. Legs and apron add roughly 10 BF net. Net total: 46 BF.

Furniture this visible gets a 20% waste allowance (grain matching, avoiding sapwood): 46 x 1.20 = 55.2 BF, round to 56. Walnut in 8/4 carries a thickness premium — call it $12/BF at retail. Lumber cost: 56 x $12 = $672. Add finish and hardware (~$60), and the table's materials land near $730 — versus several thousand dollars for a comparable store-bought solid-walnut table. That spread is exactly why people mill their own furniture.

Board foot price vs per-piece price: which is cheaper?

Big-box stores sell dimensional lumber per piece — a 2x4x8 has a fixed price tag. Compare apples to apples: a big-box 1x8x8 (3.63 BF at actual 0.75 x 7.25 in) priced at $14 is effectively $14 / 3.63 = $3.86/BF. If the local lumber yard sells the same species at $3.20/BF, the yard is cheaper for any project — but you have to buy the full boards and do the math. Per-piece pricing only wins when the big-box stock is on sale or when you need exactly one small board and the yard has a minimum purchase.

For hardwood, there is no contest: hardwood dealers quote per board foot almost universally, and comparison shopping is simply a matter of comparing the per-BF numbers on the same grade and thickness. Get quotes from at least two dealers — a $1.50/BF spread on walnut is completely normal, and on a 50-BF project that is $75 left on the table.

How to keep your board foot bill down

Buy a lower grade and cut around defects. For small-to-medium projects, #1 Common walnut at $8/BF with 25% extra footage can beat FAS at $14/BF — $10 of effective material cost per BF of finished part versus $14. The catch: you need to be comfortable with the extra milling and the extra waste you have already accounted for.

Design to standard widths. Boards are sold in 4-12 inch random widths. Designing shelves at 9 inches wide instead of 11 means you can buy narrower stock and waste less width — narrow boards are cheaper per board foot, too.

Buy it all at once. Grain and color shift between kiln batches. One trip using your total board-foot estimate keeps the lumber consistent and saves the gas money and time of repeat trips.

Check the offcut rack. Many hardwood dealers sell short offcuts at a discount per board foot. A cutting board, a small box, or a picture frame can come almost entirely from the $3/BF offcut bin while the main rack quotes $10.

Buy in spring's off-season. Lumber demand peaks with building season. Buying your hardwood in late fall or winter, when dealer inventory turns slowly, often earns a small discount or at least friendlier pricing — it never hurts to ask.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a board foot of oak cost in 2026?

Red oak runs about $4 to $7 per board foot at retail in 2026, with most dealers quoting near $5-6/BF for 4/4 select stock. White oak is pricier, roughly $5 to $8/BF, driven by strong demand for oak flooring and furniture.

How much does a board foot of pine cost in 2026?

Construction-grade pine is about $2 to $4 per board foot, often sold per piece at big-box stores. Clearer ponderosa or sugar pine for trim and carving runs $3 to $5 per board foot.

How much does a board foot of walnut cost in 2026?

Black walnut runs about $8 to $15 per board foot at retail in 2026, with FAS select grade near the top of that range and #1 Common near the bottom. Thick 8/4 walnut usually carries an extra 10-25% premium.

Why is walnut so much more expensive than oak?

Walnut trees grow more slowly and produce fewer harvestable logs than oak, so supply is much smaller while demand for its dark, rich look stays strong. Freight adds more on top, since walnut is mostly harvested in the US interior.

Is it cheaper to buy lumber per board foot or per piece?

For construction lumber, convert the per-piece price to a per-board-foot number (price / board feet of that board) and compare it to the yard's per-BF price — the yard is usually cheaper. For hardwood, dealers quote per board foot universally, so just compare the per-BF figures across dealers on the same grade.

How many board feet do I need for a project?

Add up the board feet of every piece using (thickness in inches x width in inches x length in feet) / 12, then add 10-20% waste depending on the project. Our step-by-step guide to calculating board feet walks through this with real board sizes.
