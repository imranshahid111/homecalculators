How High to Hang Curtains (Decorator Rules)

How high to hang curtains is the question that decides whether a room looks tall and finished or squat and accidental — and the answer is not "just above the window." The decorator rule is to mount the rod 4 to 6 inches above the window frame, and to extend it 8 to 12 inches past the frame on each side, so the curtain covers the wall around the window rather than the window itself. A rod hung too low chops the wall in half and makes the ceiling feel lower; a rod that is too narrow leaves the curtain bunched over the glass, blocking light and shrinking the window. This guide works through both rules with the exact measurements for 8, 9 and 10 foot ceilings, three worked examples with real windows, a placement table for every common window size, and the cost of the rod, brackets and hardware that hold it all up.

The short answer: 4 to 6 inches up, 8 to 12 inches out

Every curtain placement starts from two numbers. The height number is the gap between the top of the window frame and the rod: 4 to 6 inches on a standard 8-foot ceiling, measured from the top of the trim, not the top of the glass. The width number is the gap between the side of the frame and the end of the rod: 8 to 12 inches past the trim on each side, so the rod is 16 to 24 inches wider than the window itself. Those two numbers do three jobs at once. They make the window look bigger than it is, because the curtain covers wall instead of glass. They let the curtain stack back clear of the glass when it is open, so the whole window is visible and the light comes in. And they make the ceiling look higher, because the eye follows the curtain from the rod to the floor and reads that height as the height of the room.

The two numbers are not guesses. The 4 to 6 inch height rule comes from the standard panel lengths: on an 8-foot ceiling, a rod 4 to 6 inches above the frame sits about 84 inches from the floor, which is exactly the 84-inch ready-made panel length, and that is not a coincidence — the ready-made ladder was built around this rule. The 8 to 12 inch width rule comes from the stack-back width of a gathered curtain: a 54-inch panel gathered at 2 times fullness takes up about 10 to 12 inches of rod when it is open, so the rod needs roughly that much wall space on each side for the curtain to clear the glass. A rod that extends only 4 inches past the frame cannot hold a gathered panel without it sitting on the glass, and a rod that extends 18 inches past the frame leaves a bare stretch of rod showing on each side. Both rules are ranges because rooms are different, but the middle of each range — 5 inches up, 10 inches out — is the right default for almost any window.

The height rule: how high above the frame, by ceiling

Rod height is measured from the floor, and the gap above the frame is what sets it. On an 8-foot ceiling (96 inches), the standard window frame top sits at about 78 to 80 inches from the floor, so a rod 4 to 6 inches above the frame sits at 84 inches — the 84-inch panel length. On a 9-foot ceiling (108 inches), the frame top is usually at 88 to 90 inches, so a rod 4 to 6 inches above it sits at 95 inches — the 95-inch panel length. On a 10-foot ceiling (120 inches), the frame top is at about 102 to 104 inches, so a rod 4 to 6 inches above it sits at 108 inches — the 108-inch panel length. The pattern is the point: the ready-made panel lengths are the rod heights for the standard ceilings, and the 4 to 6 inch rule is what puts the rod at those heights.

There are three exceptions, and each one changes the height. The first is a ceiling-mounted rod or track: when the ceiling is very high, or the window is floor-to-ceiling, the rod goes on the ceiling itself, and the panel length is the ceiling height minus nothing — a 108-inch ceiling takes a 108-inch panel, and a 120-inch ceiling takes a custom length. The second is a window with a transom or a tall header: if the frame top is already close to the ceiling, there is no room for a 4 to 6 inch gap, and the rod goes as high as it can — 1 to 2 inches below the ceiling — which is the correct answer, not a compromise, because the rule is "as high as possible," and the ceiling is the limit. The third is a short window in a room with a low ceiling, such as a basement: if the frame top is at 70 inches and the ceiling is at 84 inches, a rod 4 to 6 inches above the frame sits at 76 inches, and the buy is a 63-inch panel hemmed up — no, hemmed to 76 inches is impossible from a 63-inch panel, so the buy is an 84-inch panel hemmed down to 76 inches, which wastes 8 inches and costs $15 to $25 per panel in hemming, or the rod goes 2 inches below the ceiling at 82 inches and the buy is the 84-inch panel with a 2-inch hem. In a basement, the ceiling is the constraint and the panel length follows it.

Two height traps catch people. The first is measuring the gap from the glass instead of the trim: the trim is usually 2 to 3 inches wide, so a rod 4 inches above the glass is only 1 to 2 inches above the trim, and the curtain will look cramped against the frame. Measure from the top of the trim — the outermost edge of the moulding — and the gap will be right. The second is hanging the rod at the same height as the top of the window frame, flush with it: a rod flush with the frame leaves no wall showing between the frame and the rod, and the curtain looks like it is glued to the window. The 4 to 6 inch gap is not decoration, it is the space that makes the curtain look like it belongs to the wall, and without it the whole window looks smaller.

The width rule: how far past the frame, and why it matters

Rod width is the window width plus the extension on each side, and the extension is 8 to 12 inches. A 36-inch window takes a rod 52 to 60 inches wide (36 plus 16 to 24 inches of extension). A 60-inch window takes a rod 76 to 84 inches wide. A 72-inch window takes a rod 88 to 96 inches wide. The extension does two things: it gives the gathered curtain somewhere to sit when it is open, and it makes the window look wider than it is, because the curtain covers wall on both sides and the eye reads the whole span — curtain to curtain — as the window.

The extension is set by the stack-back width, which is the width a gathered panel takes up on the rod when it is open. A 54-inch panel at 2 times fullness gathers to about 10 to 12 inches of rod, so one panel needs about 10 to 12 inches of wall on one side, and two panels need about 10 to 12 inches on each side. That is why the rule is 8 to 12 inches and not 4 inches: a 4-inch extension cannot hold a gathered 54-inch panel, and the panel will sit on the glass, blocking a quarter of the window and most of the light. A 12-inch extension holds the panel with room to spare, and the window is fully clear. The extension also sets the panel count: a wider rod needs more fabric to cover it, and the fullness math is covered in What Size Curtains Do I Need? — rod width times fullness, divided by panel width, rounded up to whole panels.

The width rule has one hard limit: the wall. If the window is close to a corner, the extension on the corner side is limited by the wall that is there — a window 6 inches from a corner can only extend 6 inches on that side, and the rod goes 6 inches past the frame on the corner side and 10 inches on the other, which is asymmetric but correct, because the alternative is a rod that runs into the corner and a bracket with nothing to screw into. If the window is between two walls with less than 8 inches on either side, the rod goes wall to wall, and the curtain covers the whole wall — which is a legitimate look, and often the best one for a small room, because a wall-to-wall curtain makes the wall read as one wide window. And if the window is a sliding door, the extension is the same 8 to 12 inches, but the rod must clear the door handle: the bracket projection — the distance the rod sits from the wall — needs to be at least 3 to 4 inches, so the curtain clears the handle when it slides past.

Worked example 1: a 36-inch bedroom window, 8-foot ceiling

Take the standard bedroom window: 36 inches wide, 48 inches tall, 8-foot ceiling, hardwood floor. The frame top is at about 79 inches from the floor, and the trim is 2½ inches wide on each side, so the frame plus trim is 41 inches wide. The rod goes 5 inches above the trim, which puts it at 84 inches from the floor — the 84-inch panel length, exactly as the rule predicts. The rod extends 10 inches past the trim on each side, so the rod is 41 plus 20, which is 61 inches wide, and the buy is a 48 to 72-inch adjustable rod set to 61 inches, which costs $20 to $35 in a basic finish.

The panel math: 61-inch rod times 2 times fullness is 122 inches of fabric, and 3 standard 54-inch panels give 162 inches, which is 2.66 times — fuller than the minimum, and right for a bedroom, where the curtain is closed at night and the folds matter. The buy is 3 panels, 54 by 84 inches, in a mid-range cotton at $40 each, which is $120, plus the rod at $30, rings at $12 and brackets at $10 — about $172 all in, with no hemming, because the standard length matches the rod height exactly. When the curtains are open, each panel gathers to about 11 inches of rod, and the 10-inch extension on each side holds it with a half inch to spare — the window is fully clear, and the glass is unobstructed.

The alternative, and the mistake, is a rod hung 2 inches above the frame and 4 inches past it on each side: a 49-inch rod at 81 inches from the floor, with 2 panels. That rod saves about $10 and one panel, but the curtain covers a quarter of the glass when it is open, the window reads as 36 inches wide instead of 61, and the ceiling reads as 81 inches instead of 84 — the room looks smaller in every direction, and the saving is $10. The 5-inch gap and the 10-inch extension cost almost nothing and change the whole room.

Worked example 2: a 60-inch living room window, 8-foot ceiling

Now the living room picture window: 60 inches wide, 60 inches tall, 8-foot ceiling, and this is the room where the curtains are part of the furniture. The frame top is at about 80 inches, and the rod goes 5 inches above the trim to 85 inches — one inch above the 84-inch standard, so the buy is the 84-inch panel, which hangs with its hem one inch off the floor instead of half an inch, which is invisible from the sofa. Or the rod goes 4 inches above the trim to 84 inches exactly, and the panel hangs perfectly. Either is right; the 4-inch gap is the better choice here, because it puts the rod at the standard height and the panel at the standard length.

The rod extends 10 inches past the trim on each side, so the rod is 60 plus 5 inches of trim (2½ inches on each side) plus 20 inches of extension, which is 85 inches wide, and the buy is a 72 to 120-inch adjustable rod at about $35, or a fixed 86-inch rod at $40 to $60. The panel math: 85-inch rod times 2 times fullness is 170 inches of fabric, and 4 standard 54-inch panels give 216 inches, which is 2.54 times — the right look for a living room. The shopping list is 4 panels, 54 by 84 inches, in a mid-range linen at $45 each, which is $180, plus the rod at $35, rings and finials at $25, and a centre bracket at $8 — about $248 all in, with no hemming.

The centre bracket is the detail that catches people on wide rods: any rod over 72 inches needs a centre support, or it will sag in the middle under the weight of the panels, and a sagging rod makes the curtain hang unevenly and the rings stick. The bracket goes at the centre of the rod, and the curtain panels split around it — two panels on each side — so the bracket is hidden behind the gathered fabric when the curtains are open and behind the closed curtain when they are shut. A rod over 96 inches needs two centre brackets, at the one-third and two-thirds points, and the panels split into three groups. The bracket costs $8 to $15 and takes 10 minutes to install, and it is the difference between a rod that stays level for a decade and one that sags in a year.

Worked example 3: a 96-inch sliding door, 9-foot ceiling

Finally, the wide case: a 96-inch sliding glass door, 80 inches tall, 9-foot ceiling, carpeted floor, and the door is used every day, so the curtain has to clear the handle and the track. The frame top is at about 88 inches, and the rod goes 6 inches above the trim to 94 inches — one inch below the 95-inch standard, so the buy is the 95-inch panel, which hangs with its hem one inch off the carpet, which is right for carpet, because a thick-pile carpet compresses under the hem and a panel that measures exactly to the top of the carpet will drag once it is walked on.

The rod extends 12 inches past the trim on each side — the top of the range, because a sliding door is wide and the stack-back is large — so the rod is 96 plus 5 inches of trim plus 24 inches of extension, which is 125 inches wide, and the buy is a 120 to 144-inch rod at $50 to $80, or a ceiling-mounted track at $60 to $120. The panel math: 125-inch rod times 2 times fullness is 250 inches of fabric, and 5 standard 54-inch panels give 270 inches, which is 2.16 times — the right fullness for a door, where the curtain is mostly open and the folds matter less. The shopping list is 5 panels, 54 by 95 inches, in a mid-range polyester at $35 each, which is $175, plus the rod at $65, rings at $20, two centre brackets at $16, and a 4-inch projection bracket set at $15 — about $291 all in.

The projection is the detail that matters on a sliding door: the bracket must hold the rod 3 to 4 inches from the wall, so the curtain clears the door handle when it slides past. A standard 2-inch projection bracket will catch the handle, and the curtain will snag every time it is opened. The 4-inch projection costs $5 to $10 more than the standard bracket and it is the only bracket that works on a door with a protruding handle. If the door handle sticks out more than 4 inches, the answer is a ceiling-mounted track, which holds the curtain 6 inches from the wall and clears any handle — and a track is also the better choice if the door is used daily, because the panels glide instead of dragging on a rod.

The first two mistakes are the ones that show up in almost every "why do my curtains look wrong" photo: the rod was hung at the frame instead of above it, and it was cut to the window instead of wider than it. The next two are hardware mistakes, and they are the ones that show up a year later — the sagging rod and the snagging handle — because they are invisible on installation day. The last two are measurement mistakes, and they are both avoidable with the same discipline: measure from the trim, not the glass, and measure the extension from the trim, not the window. The rod is the cheapest part of the job and the hardest to fix after the fact, so measure it twice.

How to measure and hang the rod, step by step

Do the measuring in this order, with a steel tape, a pencil, a level and a notepad — not a cloth tape, which stretches, and not from memory. First, measure the window width from the outermost edge of the trim on one side to the outermost edge on the other, and write it down. Second, add 16 to 24 inches for the extension — 8 to 12 inches on each side — and write the rod length down; this is the number to shop with, not the window width. Third, measure the frame top from the floor, add 4 to 6 inches, and write the rod height down; this is the number the panel length follows. Fourth, hold the rod at that height with the level and mark the bracket positions — two brackets for a rod under 72 inches, three for a rod up to 96 inches, four for a rod over 96 inches, with the centre bracket at the middle of the rod. Fifth, check the wall behind each bracket mark: if it is drywall with no stud, use a toggle bolt or a wall anchor rated for at least 20 pounds per bracket, because a rod with four panels weighs 8 to 12 pounds and a child pulling on a curtain can double that in an instant.

Two final traps deserve a note. The first is the stud trap: brackets screwed into drywall with no anchor will pull out within months, and the rod will come down with the curtains — always use an anchor or a stud, and always test the bracket by pulling down on it with your full hand weight before you hang the rod. The second is the level trap: a rod that is level on the wall but not level with the ceiling will look crooked, because the eye compares the rod to the ceiling, not to the floor — if the ceiling slopes, which most do by a quarter inch over 8 feet, level the rod to the ceiling line, not to the bubble, or the gap above the frame will be uneven and the curtain will look tilted. Hang the rod, hang the panels, and stand back in the doorway: if the rod looks high, the window looks wide, and the light comes in when the curtains are open, the placement is right.

Frequently asked questions

How high should I hang curtains above the window?

4 to 6 inches above the top of the window trim on a standard 8-foot ceiling, which puts the rod about 84 inches from the floor — the 84-inch standard panel length. On a 9-foot ceiling the rod sits at about 95 inches, and on a 10-foot ceiling at about 108 inches. If the window is close to the ceiling, hang the rod as high as it will go, 1 to 2 inches below the ceiling — the rule is "as high as possible," and the ceiling is the limit.

How far past the window should a curtain rod extend?

8 to 12 inches past the trim on each side, so the rod is 16 to 24 inches wider than the window. The extension gives the gathered curtain somewhere to sit when it is open — a 54-inch panel at 2 times fullness takes up about 10 to 12 inches of rod — and it makes the window look wider than it is, because the curtain covers wall instead of glass.

Should curtains hang from the ceiling?

Only when the window is floor-to-ceiling, or the ceiling is very high, or the window is a sliding door with a handle that sticks out more than 4 inches. A ceiling-mounted rod or track holds the curtain 6 inches from the wall and clears any handle, and the panel length is the ceiling height. On a standard window, a wall-mounted rod 4 to 6 inches above the frame is the better choice — it is cheaper, easier to install, and the panel length matches the ready-made ladder.

How wide should curtains be for a 60-inch window?

The rod should be 76 to 84 inches wide — the 60-inch window plus trim plus 8 to 12 inches of extension on each side — and the panels should total 152 to 168 inches of fabric at 2 times fullness, which is 4 standard 54-inch panels. The rod needs a centre bracket, because any rod over 72 inches will sag in the middle without one.

What happens if the curtain rod is too low?

The ceiling looks lower, the wall is chopped in half, and the window looks smaller than it is. A rod hung flush with the frame leaves no wall showing between the frame and the rod, and the curtain looks glued to the window. The fix is to move the rod 4 to 6 inches above the trim — the old holes patch with spackle in 10 minutes — and buy panels in the next standard length up if the rod height changes.

How do I hang curtains on a window close to a corner?

Extend the rod as far as the wall allows on the corner side — even 6 inches is enough — and 8 to 12 inches on the other side. The asymmetry is correct; the alternative is a rod that runs into the corner and a bracket with nothing to screw into. If the window has less than 8 inches of wall on both sides, run the rod wall to wall and let the curtain cover the whole wall, which makes the wall read as one wide window.
