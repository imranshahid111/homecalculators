Paver Patio Cost in 2026: DIY vs Contractor, Per Square Foot

What does a paver patio cost in 2026? Build it yourself and the materials run $6–$12 per square foot; hire a contractor and the installed price runs $15–$30 per square foot. On a standard 12×12 patio (144 sq ft), that is roughly $900–$1,700 DIY versus $2,200–$4,300 installed. The 2.5× gap is real, but it is not the whole story — DIY has hidden costs (tool rentals, deliveries, your weekends, and the base work nobody budgets) and contractor quotes vary by $5,000 on the same patio depending on what is actually included. This guide gives you the real per-square-foot numbers, works a full budget for three common patio sizes both ways, and shows you exactly what moves a quote so you can compare bids like-for-like instead of picking the lowest number and hoping.

The DIY costs people forget

The DIY column in the table above includes the three costs that most first-time budgets miss. If your plan shows only pavers and gravel, add these:

1. Tool rentals — $130–$300. A plate compactor runs $70–$100 per day ($110–$140 for a weekend), and a wet saw with a diamond blade runs $60–$90 per day. You need the compactor twice — once for the gravel base, once for the finished pavers — and if you cut more than a few pavers, the saw is non-negotiable. A 4-inch angle grinder with a masonry blade can cut edges in a pinch, but it is slower and dustier for more than a dozen cuts.

2. Excavation disposal — $50–$200. A 12×12 patio at 7 inches of dig (4-inch base + 1-inch bedding + paver thickness) produces about 3 cubic yards of dirt. It does not vanish — it goes to a dump ($30–$60 per yard in tipping fees plus a truck), or it becomes a berm in the corner of the yard, which costs nothing but the wheelbarrow time. Most budgets show zero here because the hole is the part nobody pictures.

3. Your time — 3–5 full days. A 12×12 patio is roughly 25–35 labor-hours for one fit person with no help: a day of digging, a day of base work, a day of laying, half a day of cutting and edging, half a day of cleanup. Value that honestly against your weekend. Hiring a day laborer for the digging day alone ($150–$250) is the highest-value money a DIY patio can spend.

What moves a contractor quote up or down

Two contractors can quote the same 12×12 patio $2,600 and $4,300, and both can be legitimate — because quotes are bundles of choices. Compare bids on these five line items, not on the bottom number:

Base depth and type. A 4-inch crushed-stone base over landscape fabric is the residential standard; a quote that quietly assumes 2 inches will be cheaper today and sink tomorrow. Ask for the compacted depth in inches, in writing.

Tear-out and disposal. Removing an old concrete slab or wooden deck costs $2–$5 per square foot extra ($300–$700 on a 12×12) and is where the cheapest-looking quote usually hides its gap. If you already have a demoed, level site, say so — you should not pay for tear-out that does not exist.

Pattern complexity. Running bond parallel to the house is the fastest lay; herringbone and 45-degree diagonals add 20–40% to labor time (and 5% more material waste). A contractor charging by the hour should show you the difference; a flat-rate quote in a fancy pattern should explain why it matches the simple-pattern price.

Site access and grading. If the backyard is only reachable by wheelbarrow through a 36-inch gate, excavation doubles. If the yard needs regrading for drainage away from the house, that is a separate line ($500–$1,500) — worth every cent, since a patio that drains toward the foundation is a future repair.

Edge restraint and sand type. Polymeric sand costs about $20–$30 per bag versus $5 for plain masonry sand, and a 12×12 needs 2–3 bags — the upcharge is real but small. Cheap quotes use plain sand; good quotes name the polymeric brand. Ask which.

Red flags in a quote

Three signals that a low quote is not a good deal: no written base specification (inches of compacted stone, fabric yes/no), no payment schedule tied to milestones (a 50% upfront demand on a 3-day job is a walk-away), and no mention of compaction equipment or edge restraint. A paver patio's lifespan is decided by its base — a sunk patio costs more to lift and relay than the gravel the cheap quote skipped.

Frequently asked questions

What is the average paver patio cost per square foot in 2026?

DIY runs $6–$12 per square foot for standard concrete pavers (materials, tool rentals and deliveries included). Contractor-installed patios run $15–$30 per square foot, depending on region, paver style, base depth and site access. Most homeowners report installed prices landing in the $18–$25 range for a standard concrete paver patio.

How much does a 12x12 paver patio cost?

About $970–$1,325 DIY (materials, tool rentals and one delivery) or $2,600–$3,600 installed by a contractor, using standard gray 4×8 concrete pavers with a 4-inch compacted gravel base. Tumbled or premium pavers add $200–$350 to the DIY number; natural stone roughly doubles it.

Is it cheaper to DIY a paver patio or hire a contractor?

DIY is cheaper in dollars by roughly 40–60% — you save $1,300–$2,600 on a 12×12. But budget the real DIY cost: tool rentals ($130–$300), excavation disposal ($50–$200) and 3–5 full days of labor. If your weekends are worth more than the savings, or the site needs serious grading, the contractor premium buys skill and speed.

What does a 20x20 paver patio cost?

About $2,420–$2,950 DIY or $7,200–$10,000 installed. The per-square-foot DIY cost actually drops at this size because tool rentals and delivery fees spread over more area — which is why building the full patio once beats building small and expanding later.

How much does labor cost for a paver patio?

Labor is the gap between the DIY and installed columns: roughly $8–$18 per square foot, which is 50–60% of a contractor quote. It covers excavation, base compaction, laying, cutting, edging and cleanup. Complex patterns (herringbone, diagonal) and poor site access push it toward the top of the range.

Does a paver patio increase home value?

A well-built paver patio typically returns a meaningful share of its cost at resale — most real-estate estimates put it at 50–80% of installed cost, more in markets where outdoor living space is at a premium. The return depends on build quality: a level, well-drained patio adds value; a sinking, uneven one subtracts it.
