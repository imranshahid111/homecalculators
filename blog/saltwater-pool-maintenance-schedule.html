Saltwater Pool Maintenance Schedule: Weekly to Yearly

A saltwater pool is sold as the low-maintenance pool, and it is — compared with a chlorine pool, it asks for roughly 20 to 30 minutes a week of hands-on work and about $150 to $300 a year in chemicals and consumables for a typical 20,000 gallon pool. But "low maintenance" is not "no maintenance", and the pools that turn green, eat their salt cells in two years, or cost a fortune in repairs are almost always the ones whose owners believed the sales pitch and skipped the routine. This saltwater pool maintenance schedule breaks the work into what you do every week, every month, every season and every year — with the time and cost of each job, the exact water-balance numbers to hold, and the two jobs (salt-cell cleaning and salt testing) that most owners get wrong.

The numbers to hold all year

Every task on this schedule exists to keep five numbers inside their ranges. If you remember nothing else, remember this table — it is the whole job, and everything below is just how you keep these numbers there.

ReadingTarget rangeTest how oftenWhat moves it
Free chlorine1–3 ppmWeeklyCell output %, pump run time, sun, bather load
pH7.4–7.6WeeklySalt cells push pH up; muriatic acid brings it down
Salt2,700–3,400 ppm (check your manual)MonthlySplash-out, backwash, rain, leaks — never evaporation
Total alkalinity80–120 ppmMonthlyAcid lowers it; baking soda raises it
Calcium hardness200–400 ppmMonthlyFill water, calcium increaser, dilution
Cyanuric acid (stabilizer)30–50 ppmEvery 2–3 monthsStabilizer additions; dilution lowers it

Two of these deserve a note before the schedule starts. First, pH is the one that drifts fastest in a saltwater pool: the electrolysis inside the cell produces chlorine and, as a side effect, pushes pH upward, so most saltwater owners add a small dose of muriatic acid every week or two — a gallon a month is a normal rate for a 20,000 gallon pool. Second, salt is the one people test least and get wrong most: the cell's own display can read hundreds of ppm off as it ages, so the monthly salt check belongs to a liquid test kit or fresh strips, never to the display alone. If your dose depends on a number, get that number right first — the free Pool Salt Calculator turns your gallons and your two ppm readings into pounds and bags, but it can only be as accurate as the reading you feed it.

Weekly tasks: about 20–30 minutes

This is the core of the schedule. Do it the same day every week and the pool mostly takes care of itself; skip it for a month and you will spend a weekend and a few hundred dollars getting the water back.

1. Test free chlorine and pH (5 minutes). Take the sample from elbow depth, away from the return jets, and test with a liquid kit or fresh strips. Free chlorine should sit between 1 and 3 ppm. If it is under 1, raise the cell output percentage by 10 points or add an extra hour of pump run time, then retest in 24 hours. If it is over 3, drop the output by 10 points. pH should sit at 7.4–7.6. If it has crept to 7.8 or above — the usual saltwater direction — add muriatic acid in small doses: roughly 8–12 fluid ounces per 10,000 gallons drops pH by about 0.2, but dose half, wait 4 hours with the pump running, and retest rather than pouring the whole calculated amount at once.

2. Skim, brush and empty the baskets (10 minutes). Skim leaves and debris off the surface, brush the walls and steps — especially the waterline, where a saltwater pool builds a crusty tide mark faster than a chlorine pool — and empty the skimmer basket and the pump basket. A full basket cuts circulation, and poor circulation is what turns a small chemistry problem into a green pool. While you are there, glance at the pump pressure gauge: a reading 8–10 psi above the clean-filter baseline means the filter needs attention (see the monthly section).

3. Check the salt cell display (2 minutes). Look at what the cell reports: salt reading, output percentage, and any warning lights. Do not act on the salt number alone — it is a rough guide, not a test — but a sudden change is a signal. A cell that reports salt 500 ppm lower than last week, or that flashes a "check salt" or "low salt" light while the water tests fine, is usually telling you the cell is scaling up or its sensor is drifting. Note the reading in a log (a phone note is enough); the trend over a season is far more useful than any single reading.

4. Top up the water level (5 minutes, as needed). Keep the water at mid-skimmer — about halfway up the skimmer opening. Low water starves the pump and can run it dry; high water after rain floods the skimmer and slows surface cleaning. In hot weather a 20,000 gallon pool can lose a quarter inch a day to evaporation, which is roughly 125 gallons a day, so a garden hose top-up every week or two is normal. Remember that topping up dilutes salt slightly and rain dilutes it more — both are reasons the monthly salt test matters.

5. Adjust the cell output for the weather. Chlorine demand is not constant: a hot sunny week with heavy swimming can double it, while a cool cloudy week halves it. As a rule of thumb, start the season at 50% output with the pump running 8 hours a day, and move the output in 10-point steps rather than the pump hours. In peak summer many 20,000 gallon pools settle around 60–70% output and 8–10 pump hours; in spring and fall 40–50% and 6–8 hours is often enough. The weekly chlorine test tells you whether the setting is right — chase the test result, not the calendar.

Monthly tasks: about 45–60 minutes

Once a month, go one level deeper than the weekly routine. These are the jobs that catch a problem while it is still cheap.

Test the full balance: salt, alkalinity, calcium, stabilizer. Run the full panel — salt with the kit, total alkalinity (80–120 ppm), calcium hardness (200–400 ppm) and, every second or third month, cyanuric acid (30–50 ppm). Fix problems in this order: alkalinity first, then pH, then the rest, because alkalinity buffers pH and a pool with alkalinity at 50 ppm will swing pH wildly no matter how much acid you add. To raise alkalinity by 10 ppm in a 20,000 gallon pool takes about 1.5 pounds of baking soda; to lower it, acid in stages with aeration. Calcium below 200 ppm is aggressive water that etches plaster and corrodes heaters — raise it with calcium chloride, about 1.25 pounds per 10 ppm per 10,000 gallons. Calcium above 400 ppm plus high pH is what scales the cell, so if both are high, bring pH down first and keep it there.

Inspect the salt cell for scale. Switch the pump off, close the valves, and look inside the cell (most units have a clear housing or a removable cell). A light white film is normal; thick, crusty white buildup between the plates is scale, and scale is the single biggest killer of salt cells — it insulates the plates, forces the unit to work harder, and shortens its life from 5–7 years to 2–3. If you see heavy scale, clean the cell (below). If the plates look pitted, brown or eroded, the cell is near the end of its life regardless of age.

Clean the salt cell when it needs it — not on a calendar. Clean only when you see scale or the unit reports low salt while the water tests fine; cleaning a clean cell with acid wears the plates for nothing. The method: mix one part muriatic acid to four parts water in a plastic bucket (always acid into water, never water into acid), stand the cell upright in the solution so the plates are covered, and soak for 5–15 minutes until the fizzing stops. Rinse thoroughly, reinstall, and restart. Never use a metal brush or a screwdriver on the plates, and never soak longer than needed — a cell cleaned four times a year with acid lasts noticeably less long than one cleaned twice. With balanced water (pH held at 7.4–7.6, calcium under 400 ppm), most cells need cleaning only two or three times a season.

Backwash or clean the filter. For a sand filter, backwash when the pressure gauge reads 8–10 psi above the clean baseline, then rinse for 30 seconds. For a cartridge filter, pull the cartridge, hose it down pleat by pleat, and soak it in filter cleaner once or twice a season. For a DE filter, backwash and recharge with fresh DE powder. A dirty filter is the quiet cause of half of all "my chlorine won't hold" complaints — the cell can be making chlorine perfectly well, but if the water is not circulating through it properly, the pool does not see it.

Check the equipment pad. Walk the pad and look for the cheap problems: drips at unions and pump seals, a salt crust on a fitting (a leak evaporating and leaving its salt behind), a pump basket lid o-ring that is flattened or cracked, and the cell's cable and flow switch for wear. A drip that loses a gallon an hour is 24 gallons a day — over a month that is 720 gallons, and at 3,200 ppm that is nearly 20 pounds of salt carried out with the water. Fix the drip before you dose the salt, or you are pouring salt into a leak.

Seasonal tasks: opening, peak summer, and closing

Spring opening. Before the first swim, run the full test panel and fix the balance in order — alkalinity, pH, calcium, then salt. Salt is usually low after winter: rain, snowmelt and a winter's worth of top-ups dilute it, so expect a top-up of two to four 40-pound bags on a 20,000 gallon pool. Dose in two stages (two-thirds, circulate 24 hours, retest, then the rest) and keep the cell off while the salt dissolves. Inspect the cell for winter scale, clean it if needed, check the pump and filter o-rings, and confirm the flow switch moves freely. If the pool was closed with a cover, brush and vacuum the settled debris before the first test, or the debris will eat chlorine for a week.

Peak summer. This is when the schedule works hardest: test chlorine twice a week if the pool is used daily, keep the pump running 8–10 hours, and expect to add acid weekly as the cell pushes pH up. Watch the water level daily in a heatwave — a quarter inch a day of evaporation is normal — and top up before the skimmer sucks air. After a pool party or a storm, shock the pool with liquid chlorine even though the cell is running: a heavy bather load or a load of organic debris can overwhelm the cell's output for a day, and a single shock dose (about a gallon of liquid chlorine per 10,000 gallons) resets the water faster than waiting for the cell to catch up.

Fall and winter closing (cold climates). Where winters freeze, closing is a real job: balance the water, clean the cell and remove it (or drain it in place per the manual), drain the pump, filter and heater, blow out the lines, and add winterizing chemicals. Salt itself does not need removing — it stays in the water all winter and is still there in spring, minus what rain and snowmelt diluted. In warm climates where the pool runs all year, winter is the easy season: cut the pump to 4–6 hours, drop the cell output to 30–40%, and test every two weeks instead of weekly, but keep the monthly salt and balance checks — cold water holds chlorine better, but the pH drift from the cell does not stop.

Yearly tasks: the once-a-year jobs

Once a year, usually at opening, do the jobs that only make sense annually. Replace the pump basket and skimmer basket if they are cracked — a cracked basket lets debris into the impeller, and an impeller job costs far more than a $15 basket. Replace the pump lid o-ring and any valve o-rings that have gone flat; a $5 o-ring is the cheapest leak prevention there is. Check the cell's age against its rated life: most cells are rated for about 10,000 hours of operation, which at 8 hours a day for a 6-month season is roughly 3–5 years of real use, though well-balanced water stretches that toward 5–7 years. Budget $500–$900 for a replacement cell and do not be surprised when the time comes — a cell is a consumable, not a lifetime part. Have a professional inspect the heater, the automation panel and the bonding once a year if the system is more than five years old, and keep a written log of salt readings, cell cleanings and part replacements: when something fails, the log is what turns a guess into a diagnosis.

What the schedule costs in time and money

The numbers below are for a 20,000 gallon in-ground pool with a standard salt system, a 6-month swim season, and the owner doing the work. A pool service doing the same routine typically charges $80–$150 a month in the USA, which is $480–$900 a season — the DIY schedule below is the comparison.

Two things stand out. First, the time is small — under half an hour a week in season — which is why a saltwater pool earns its reputation. Second, the cell dominates the money: the $100–$180 a year amortized cell cost is a third to a half of the whole budget, which is why the habits that extend cell life (holding pH at 7.4–7.6, keeping calcium under 400 ppm, cleaning only when scale is visible) are worth more than any chemical saving. Compare with a traditional chlorine pool of the same size, which typically runs $300–$600 a season in chlorine, shock and stabilizer alone, and the salt system is cheaper to run even after the cell is counted — the savings just arrive slowly, over years, not in the first month.

If a problem survives two rounds of the fix in the table, test the water at a pool store — their photometer reads more precisely than a home kit, and a second opinion on salt and stabilizer is worth more than a third guess. And if you are dosing salt on the strength of a cell display that disagrees with your kit by more than 200 ppm, trust the kit: a wrong salt number is how pools end up hundreds of ppm over target, and the only cure for too much salt is draining water out. Frequently asked questions

How much time does a saltwater pool take each week?

About 20 to 30 minutes in swim season: 5 minutes testing chlorine and pH, 10 minutes skimming, brushing and emptying baskets, a 2-minute check of the salt cell display, and topping up the water as needed. Monthly jobs add about an hour a month.

How often should I test the salt level in my pool?

Test salt monthly with a liquid test kit or fresh strips, and always test before adding salt. The cell's own display can read hundreds of ppm off as it ages, so never dose on the display alone.

How often should I clean my salt cell?

Only when you see heavy white scale between the plates or the unit reports low salt while the water tests fine — typically 2 to 3 times a season with balanced water. Cleaning a clean cell with acid wears the plates for nothing and shortens its life.

Why does my saltwater pool pH keep rising?

The electrolysis inside the salt cell pushes pH upward as a side effect, so a weekly muriatic acid dose is normal — about a gallon a month for a 20,000 gallon pool. Hold total alkalinity at 80-120 ppm first, because low alkalinity lets pH swing wildly.

How much does it cost to maintain a saltwater pool per year?

About $250 to $475 a year for a 20,000 gallon pool doing the work yourself, including chemicals, salt top-ups, test kit refills and the salt cell amortized over its 5-year life. A pool service doing the same routine typically charges $80-150 a month.

Do I need to shock a saltwater pool?

Yes, occasionally. The cell handles normal daily demand, but after a pool party, a storm or an algae bloom the organic load can overwhelm it. One shock dose of liquid chlorine (about a gallon per 10,000 gallons) resets the water faster than waiting for the cell to catch up.
