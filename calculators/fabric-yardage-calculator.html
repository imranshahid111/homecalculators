Fabric Yardage Calculator

How many yards of fabric does your project need? No more guessing at the cutting counter.

Calculate

Widest measurement of one piece — e.g. cushion top, curtain panel, quilt.
For patterned fabric, cuts are rounded up to full repeats so motifs align.

A worked example

Example: Two cushion covers 40 in wide × 60 in of cut length, from standard 44-inch quilting cotton: each piece fits in one width, total length 120 in = 3.33 yards — buy 3.75 yards with a 10% allowance for shrinkage and cutting errors.

The formula

Yards = (Cut Length × Pieces × Widths Needed) ÷ 36. Widths Needed = Project Width ÷ Fabric Width, rounded up. If the fabric has a pattern repeat, each cut length is first rounded up to a whole number of repeats so the pattern lines up across pieces.

How to get an accurate result

Fabric is sold by the yard (36 inches of length) cut from a bolt of fixed width — and the width is what trips people up. The same project can need 2 yards from a 60-inch bolt or 4 yards from a 36-inch bolt, because a wide piece that does not fit across the fabric must be sewn from multiple widths, each consuming full cut length. This calculator works in exactly that order: fit the width first, then multiply the length.

Standard widths to know: quilting cottons are 44–45 inches, home-decor fabrics 54 inches, many apparel fabrics 58–60 inches, and craft felt and some cottons just 36 inches. The usable width is slightly less than the bolt width — the selvage edges (about 1/2 inch each side) are usually trimmed or excluded from the visible piece, so if your project width is within an inch of the fabric width, treat it as not fitting and plan two widths.

Pattern repeats and fabric direction add quiet yardage. A large floral or stripe must align across panels and pieces, so each cut is rounded up to complete repeats — enter the repeat (printed on the bolt end or product page) and the calculator adjusts. Directional prints and nap fabrics like velvet must all be cut facing the same way, which forbids flipping pieces to save space; the 10% buying allowance covers normal cases, but for bold patterns on big projects, 15–20% is safer.

Pre-wash shrinkage is real for natural fibers: cotton can shrink 3–5%, linen more. The allowance in the result covers one wash for most cottons. As for price, USA fabric stores price quilting cotton around $8–15 per yard, decorator fabric $15–40, and apparel fabrics anywhere in between — which is why a half-yard of over-buying is a pleasant spare for test seams and future repairs, while a half-yard short on a discontinued print is a project-ender.

Step by step

  1. Measure one piece: its widest width and its full cut length (incl. hems/seams).
  2. Count identical pieces.
  3. Choose the fabric width of the bolt you will actually buy.
  4. Add the pattern repeat if the fabric is patterned (bolt label shows it).
  5. Buy the rounded-up yardage shown — with shrinkage allowance included.

Frequently asked questions

How much fabric do I need for curtains?

For two panels covering a 72 in rod at 2× fullness with a 54 in decorator fabric, each panel is one width; yardage is driven by length: an 84 in drop + hems ≈ 96 in per panel, so about 5.5 yards total. Use the curtain calculator first for panel counts.

How many yards is a queen quilt?

A queen quilt top (about 90 × 108 in) from 44 in fabric typically needs about 8–9 yards for the top if pieced from one fabric, plus about 8 yards of backing (or 3 yards of 108 in wide backing fabric).

What does fabric width 44/45 mean?

The fabric is 44–45 inches wide on the bolt. Yardage is the length cut from that bolt — a "yard" is always 36 inches of length, whatever the width.

Should I buy extra fabric?

Yes — 10% for plain fabrics and 15–20% for patterns that must match, nap fabrics, or first-time projects. Dye lots differ; you usually cannot buy a perfect match later.
